                                                                               1. Introduction
                                              Incompressible orientable surfaces occupy a central position in 3-manifold the-
                                           ory. It is therefore very natural to ask whether a given orientable surface properly
                                           embedded in a 3-manifold is incompressible. The following result provides an algo-
                                           rithm to determine this.
                                           Theorem 1.1. Let T be a triangulation of a compact orientable irreducible 3-
                                           manifold M , possibly with boundary. Let S be a compact orientable normal surface
                                           properly embedded in M . Then there is an algorithm to determine whether S is
                                           incompressible.
                                                                                         1
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                     2


   This theorem is not new, but the algorithm is. The first algorithmic solution
to the problem in Theorem 1.1 was given by Haken [3]. His procedure was to cut
the manifold M along S and then triangulate the new 3-manifold M \\S. Then he
searched for possible normal compression discs for the two copies of S in M \\S.
In order to make this a finite procedure, he showed that it was possible to restrict
to ‘fundamental’ normal discs. A more recent algorithm, due to the author [16],
is more complicated, and uses sutured manifold hierarchies. Both these algorithms
are useful and have interesting theoretical consequences. But they both have the
downside that they require a search through a possibly very extensive search space,
for example, the set of all fundamental normal surfaces. The algorithm presented
here has the significant advantage that it does not require examination of a large
search space. An interesting application of Theorem 1.1 is the following.
Corollary 1.2. There is an algorithm to determine whether a given knot in the
3-sphere is trivial.
   This follows from Theorem 1.1 because the exterior of a knot in the 3-sphere has
incompressible boundary if and only if the knot is non-trivial. Again, this result is
not new. There are now many known algorithms to detect the unknot, the first of
which was due to Haken [3]. Some require the computation of knot invariants, such
as Khovanov homology [10] or Heegaard Floer homology [19, 20]. Others require a
possibly extensive search: a search for a spanning disc [5, 16], a homomorphism from
the fundamental group of the knot complement to a finite group with non-abelian
image [11], sequences of Reidemeister moves [15] or sequences of grid diagrams [2].
As a result, none of these algorithms are likely to run in polynomial time. Indeed,
in the case of Khovanov homology, there is an exponential lower bound on its worst-
case running time [7], subject to some standard conjectures in complexity theory.
The algorithm that we give seems amenable to possible speed-up, which we will
explore in future work. Furthermore, in a companion paper [17], we will show how
this algorithm can be extended to determine whether a given link in the 3-sphere
is hyperbolic.
   The algorithm generalises to 3-manifolds with a boundary pattern. Recall that
a boundary pattern for a compact orientable 3-manifold M is a collection P of
disjoint simple closed curves and trivalent graphs embedded in ∂M . A boundary
pattern P is essential if, for every disc D properly embedded in M that intersects
P transversely in at most three points away from the vertices of P , there is a
disc D′ in ∂M that intersects P in the empty set, a single arc, or a graph with a
single 3-valent vertex in the interior of D′ and three 1-valent vertices on ∂D′ . (See
Figure 1.) If there is a disc D properly embedded in M intersecting P transversely
at most three times away from the vertices, but there is no corresponding disc D′ ,
then D is termed a violating disc. In the final case, when D′ intersects P in a graph
with a single 3-valent vertex in the interior of D′ and three 1-valent vertices on ∂D′ ,
this graph is known as a tripod. Boundary patterns will be the focus of this paper.
At this stage, we observe that when P is the empty pattern, then P is essential if
and only if ∂M is incompressible.
Theorem 1.3. Let (M, P ) be a compact orientable irreducible 3-manifold with
boundary pattern. Let T be a triangulation of M in which P is simplicial. Then
there is an algorithm to determine whether P is essential. Moreover, if P is not
essential, the algorithm provides a violating disc.

   When P is essential and M irreducible, the algorithm outputs an essential hi-
erarchy for (M, P ). Such hierarchies, which are defined in Section 2, will play a
central role in this paper. They are a finite sequence of decompositions along prop-
erly embedded orientable surfaces where the final manifold is a collection of 3-balls
with an essential boundary pattern.
   In the later parts of the paper, we will establish that the algorithms given above
can be used to show that the decision problems are in NP. Moreover, we will drop
the hypothesis that the 3-manifold is irreducible. Specifically, we will consider the
following problems.
   The decision problem Incompressible boundary takes, as its input a trian-
gulation of a compact orientable 3-manifold M and it asks whether ∂M is incom-
pressible.
Theorem 1.4. The decision problem Incompressible boundary is in NP.
   This theorem was proved by the author in [16], using sutured manifold hierar-
chies, based on argument of Agol. The problem was also shown to be in co-NP by
Ivanov [6].
   This has applications for the famous problem Knottedness. This takes, as its
input, either a diagram of a knot K or a triangulation of its exterior, and it asks
whether K is a non-trivial knot.
Corollary 1.5. The decision problem Knottedness is in NP.
   This was one of the main theorems in [16]. Previously, Kuperberg [11] showed
that Knottedness is in NP, conditionally on the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis.
   The decision problem Essential boundary pattern takes, as its input, a
triangulation of compact orientable 3-manifold M with boundary pattern P that
is simplicial, and it asks whether P is essential. The following is a new result.
Theorem 1.6. The decision problem Essential boundary pattern is in NP
and co-NP.
    When P is inessential, the certificate is a violating disc in normal form. When P
is essential and M is irreducible, the certificate is an essential hierarchy for (M, P ).
The fact that essential hierarchies can be encoded efficiently and can be certified
as essential will be extremely useful in future work [17].

    Much of the theory of hierarchies as explained in Section 2 is due to Johannson
[8]. The presentation here follows that of a graduate course [12] given by the
author in 1999. One of the goals of that course was to provide a new proof of
the loop theorem using hierarchies. The fact that the theory also has algorithmic
applications was observed in a conversation with Ian Agol in 2013. The author
developed and expanded the theory over the intervening years, resulting in this
paper and its sequel [17].
    The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 2, we recall some basic
material on hierarchies and boundary patterns. In Section 3, we explore essential
hierarchies and prove one direction of Theorem 2.5, which asserts that when a
3-manifold admits an essential hierarchy, then it is irreducible and has essential
boundary pattern. In Section 4, we introduce normal surfaces, in the context of
handle structures with a boundary pattern. In Section 5, we examine how handle
structures behave when decomposed along a normal surface. The goal here is to
establish an upper bound on the number of decompositions in a hierarchy. This
gives the other direction of Theorem 2.5, which gives a sufficient condition for a
3-manifold with boundary pattern to admits an essential hierarchy. In Section 6, we
present the algorithm required by Theorem 1.3, and in Section 7, an implementation
of the algorithm in a specific example is given. The algorithm to determine whether
a surface is incompressible, as required by Theorem 1.1, is given in Section 8.
A crude analysis of the number of steps in the algorithm is given in Section 9.
The crucial quantity that controls an upper bound on the running time is the
number of decompositions in the hierarchy. Therefore, in Section 10, we give a
more complicated algorithm for constructing a hierarchy, that has the advantage
that the number of decompositions is explicitly controlled. In Section 11, we show
that the handle structures created in the hierarchy have uniform type, which means
that each handle meets the pattern and its neighbouring handles in one of finitely
many types, independent of the manifold. This is important for the later parts of
the paper, in Sections 12, 13 and 14, where we show that the decision problem in
Theorem 1.6, deciding whether a pattern is essential, is in NP and co-NP.

                   2. Hierarchies and boundary patterns
  We will use the following terminology, which has become standard.
Definition 2.1. Let M be a manifold and let S be either a codimension one
submanifold properly embedded in M or a compact codimension zero submanifold.
Then the manifold M \\S obtained by cutting M along S is defined as follows. Pick
a Riemannian metric on M . Then M − S inherits a Riemannian metric and M \\S
is the defined to be metric completion of M − S. We also permit S not to be a
subset of M as long as S and M are both subsets of some larger space and S ∩ M
satisfies the above conditions, in which case M \\S is defined to be M \\(S ∩ M ).
When M is compact and S is a codimension one submanifold, then the inclusion
M − S → M induces a quotient map M \\S → M .
   Our algorithm makes use of hierarchies. Here, one starts with a compact ori-
entable 3-manifold, and then one cuts along a properly embedded orientable surface,
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                  5


forming a new 3-manifold. In this manifold, one finds another properly embedded
orientable surface, and one cuts along this. The process continues until the final
manifold is a collection of 3-balls. The manifolds in the hierarchy will inherit a
boundary pattern, as follows.
Definition 2.2. Let (M, P ) be a compact orientable 3-manifold with boundary
pattern. Then a compact orientable surface S properly embedded in M is transverse
to P if it is disjoint from the vertices and intersects the edges transversely. We
then say that S is a decomposing surface. The manifold M \\S inherits a boundary
pattern P ′ , where P ′ is the union of P ∩ (M \\S) and the copies of ∂S in ∂(M \\S).
We write
                                          S
                                 (M, P ) −→ (M \\S, P ′ )
for this decomposition. We also denote (M \\S, P ′ ) by (M, P )\\S. (See Figure 2.)

Definition 2.3. Let (M, P ) be a compact orientable 3-manifold with boundary
pattern. Then a partial hierarchy for (M, P ) is a sequence of decompositions
                                   1S           S2      S ℓ
             (M, P ) = (M1 , P1 ) −→ (M2 , P2 ) −→ . . . −→ (Mℓ+1 , Pℓ+1 ).
It is a hierarchy if Mℓ+1 is a disjoint union of 3-balls.
   This is a modification of the standard terminology, since we do not require the
surfaces to be incompressible (although they will end up being so in the cases of
interest to us).
   Recall, from Section 1, the definition of an essential boundary pattern. At first
sight, this is a strange definition, because it is not at all clear why one should be
interested only in simple closed curves that intersect the pattern in at most three
points. We will give more motivation for it in the results that follow.
Definition 2.4. An essential hierarchy for a compact orientable 3-manifold M
with boundary pattern P is a sequence of decompositions
                                   1S           S2      S ℓ
             (M, P ) = (M1 , P1 ) −→ (M2 , P2 ) −→ . . . −→ (Mℓ+1 , Pℓ+1 ).
where (Mℓ+1 , Pℓ+1 ) is a collection of balls with essential boundary pattern.
   Again, it is not immediately clear why one should only be concerned with whether
the final pattern is essential. But this is explained by the following result.
Theorem 2.5 (Essential hierarchy iff essential pattern and irreducible). Let M be
a compact orientable 3-manifold with a boundary pattern P . Then (M, P ) admits
an essential hierarchy if and only if the following all hold:
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                       6


    (1) either M contains a properly embedded orientable incompressible surface
        with no 2-sphere components, or ∂M is non-empty;
    (2) P is essential;
    (3) M is irreducible.

   It is relatively straightforward to determine quickly whether a boundary pattern
for a 3-ball is essential. This can be rephrased in standard graph-theoretic terms
as follows. Recall that a graph is simple if it has no edge loops and does not have
multiple edges between pairs of vertices. A simple graph is n-connected for some
positive integer n if it has at least n + 1 vertices and there is no set of n − 1 vertices
whose removal disconnects the graph.

Proposition 2.6 (Essential pattern for a ball). Let M be a 3-ball, and let P be a
boundary pattern for M . Then P is essential if and only if P is empty, a simple
closed curve or a connected graph, and in the latter case, the planar graph dual to
P is either a simple 4-connected graph or the complete graph on 3 or 4 vertices.

Proof. We first show that the given conditions on P imply that P is essential. If
P is empty or a simple closed curve, then clearly P is essential. So suppose that
P is a connected graph and that the graph G dual to P is simple and 4-connected
or the complete graph on 4 vertices. Consider a simple closed curve C in ∂M that
intersects P transversely in at most 3 points. If C is disjoint from P , then it must
bound a disc in ∂M disjoint from P , since P is connected and ∂M is a sphere.
If C intersects P at one point, this specifies an edge loop in G, contrary to our
assumption that G is simple. If C intersects P at two points, then these two points
must lie on the same edge of P , as otherwise we obtain two edges between the same
pair of vertices of G. Hence, C bounds a disc in ∂M intersecting P in an arc. If
C intersects P at three points, then this specifies a closed walk of length 3 in G.
This is a cycle because otherwise G is not simple. Removing the vertices in this
cycle does not separate G, because G is 4-connected or the complete graph on 3 or
4 vertices. Hence, C must bound a disc intersecting no regions of ∂M − P other
than the three that C visits. This implies that P intersects this disc in a tripod.
Thus we have shown that P is essential.
   Conversely, suppose that P is essential. We may suppose that P is non-empty
and not a simple closed curve, as the implication is immediate in those cases. Then
P is clearly connected as otherwise there is a simple closed curve in ∂M − P that
does not bound a disc in ∂M disjoint from P . Let G be its dual planar graph.
An edge loop in G would give a simple closed curve intersecting the pattern once,
contradicting the assumption that P is essential. More than one edge between a
pair of vertices would give a simple closed curve in ∂M intersecting P in exactly
two points that lie in different edges of P . Again this contradicts the assumption
that P is essential. Consider a collection of at most three vertices in G. We will
show that these do not separate G. Each vertex corresponds to a region of ∂M − P ,
and for these vertices to disconnect G, the union of these regions must disconnect
∂M . If they do then there must be a simple closed curve lying in the union of these
regions that separates at least two other regions of ∂M − P . Since P is essential,
this curve bounds a disc in ∂M intersecting P in an arc or a tripod. This implies
that these three vertices did not in fact separate G. Note that removing these three
vertices of G may create the empty graph, but in this case, G is the complete graph
      INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                             7


on 3 vertices. Hence, we have shown that G is 4-connected or the complete graph
on 3 or 4 vertices.                                                           □
   Note that it can be determined in polynomial time (as a function of the number
of vertices and edges) whether a graph is simple and 4-connected or the complete
graph on 4 vertices.
   Theorem 2.5 is an extremely powerful tool for showing that a pattern is essential,
as the following example demonstrates.

be the empty pattern. A hierarchy for (M, P ) is given. The final manifold consists

                                3. Essential hierarchies
   Our aim is to prove Theorem 2.5. We first show that if (M, P ) admits an essential
hierarchy, then P is essential and M is irreducible. We will also establish that the
decomposing surfaces have the following important property.
Definition 3.1. Let M be a compact orientable 3-manifold and let P be a boundary
pattern. Let S be a compact surface properly embedded in M . Then a pattern-
compression disc for S is a disc D embedded in M such that
    (1) D ∩ S is an arc α in ∂D;
    (2) ∂D\\α = D ∩ ∂M intersects P at most once; and
    (3) α does not separate off a disc from S intersecting P at most once.
If no such pattern-compression disc exists, then S is pattern-incompressible. (See
Figure 4.) If D is a pattern-compression disc for S, then the surface obtained from
S by cutting along S ∩ D and then attaching two parallel copies of D is the surface
obtained by pattern-compressing along D.

  A key step in the proof of Theorem 2.5 is the following.
Lemma 3.2 (Essential pattern pulls back). Let
                                              S
                                             → (M ′ , P ′ )
                                     (M, P ) −
be a decomposition and suppose that P ′ is essential. Then P is essential and S is
incompressible and pattern-incompressible.
Proof. We first show that P is essential. Let D be a disc properly embedded in M
that is transverse to P and that intersects P at most three times. We wish to show
that D is not a violating disc.
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                     9


   Perform a small isotopy to S, leaving P invariant, so that afterwards S ∩ D is a
collection of simple closed curves and arcs properly embedded in D.
   If there is a simple closed curve of S ∩ D, there is one that is innermost in D.
It bounds a disc D′ in M ′ that is disjoint from P ′ . Since we are assuming that P ′
is essential, ∂D′ bounds a disc D′′ in ∂M ′ that is disjoint from the pattern. This
disc is a subset of S. We can remove D′ from D and replace it by a parallel copy
of D′′ . The result is violating for P if and only if D was violating. However, its
intersection with S contains fewer simple closed curves.
   So, we may assume that D ∩ S is a collection of arcs properly embedded in D.
Say there are n > 0 of them. Then at the endpoints of these arcs, we see 4n points
of intersection with P ′ . We also have at most 3 points of intersection between
∂D and P . So, in the n + 1 discs D\\(D ∩ S), there are at most 4n + 3 points
of intersection with P ′ . Hence, one these discs, D1 say, has at most three points
of intersection with P ′ in its boundary. Necessarily, it has at least two points of
intersection with P ′ . We are assuming that P ′ is essential. Hence, ∂D1 bounds
a disc D2 in ∂M ′ . This disc D2 intersects P ′ either in a single arc or a tripod.
Therefore, D2 ∩ S is a disc. We may remove D1 from D and replace it by D2 ∩ S,
and then perform a further small isotopy to give a disc D3 properly embedded in
M that is transverse to P and that has fewer components of intersection with S.
This is a violating disc for M if and only if D was.
   So we may assume that D is disjoint from S and hence lies in M ′ . Since P ′ is
essential, ∂D bounds a disc D′ in ∂M ′ intersecting P ′ in the empty set, an arc or a
tripod. Since ∂D′ is disjoint from S, all of D′ must be disjoint from S. So, D′ lies
in ∂M . So, D was not a violating disc, and so we have proved that P is essential.
   We now show that S is incompressible. Consider a disc D embedded in the
interior of M such that D ∩ S = ∂D. Then D is properly embedded in M ′ and
is disjoint from the pattern P ′ . Since P ′ is essential, ∂D bounds a disc in ∂M ′
disjoint from P ′ . This is a disc in S. So D is not a compression disc for S.
   We now show that S is pattern-incompressible. Let D be a disc embedded in
M such that D ∩ S is an arc in ∂D, and the remainder of ∂D is an arc in ∂M
intersecting P at most once. Then D lies in M ′ and intersects P ′ at least twice and
at most three times. Since P ′ is essential, ∂D bounds a disc D′ in ∂M ′ intersecting
P ′ in an arc or a tripod. The intersection between D′ and S is a disc separated
off by D ∩ S that intersects P at most once. So D is not a pattern-compression
disc.                                                                               □
Lemma 3.3 (Irreducible and essential pattern pulls back). Let
                                          S
                                         → (M ′ , P ′ )
                                 (M, P ) −
be a decomposition and suppose that M ′ is irreducible and P ′ is essential. Then M
is irreducible and P is essential.
Proof. The fact that P is essential is a result of Lemma 3.2. We need to show that
M is irreducible. Consider a 2-sphere S 2 properly embedded in M . This intersects
S in a collection of simple closed curves. An innermost one bounds a disc D′ in
M ′ that is disjoint from P ′ . Since P ′ is essential, ∂D′ bounds a disc D′′ in S and
because M ′ is irreducible, we may isotope D′′ across to D′ and then a little further,
to remove this curve of S 2 ∩ S. So we may assume that S is disjoint from S 2 , and
hence S 2 lies in M ′ . As M ′ is irreducible, S 2 bounds a ball in M ′ and this is also
a ball in M .                                                                         □

    In many circumstances, it is also the case that when P is essential and M is
irreducible, then P ′ is also essential and M ′ is also irreducible. However, in order
to guarantee this, we require that the decomposing surface S is incompressible and
pattern-incompressible. We now investigate how to arrange this.
    When we pattern-compress a surface, we would like to say that the new surface
is, in some sense, simpler than the original one. We formalise this as follows, using
the following measure of complexity.
Definition 3.4. The pattern-complexity χp (S) of a properly embedded surface S
is defined to be −4χ(S) + |S ∩ P |.
  A useful feature of pattern-complexity is the following immediate result.
Lemma 3.5. If S is a compact connected orientable surface satisfying χp (S) < 0,
then S is a sphere or disc intersecting the pattern in at most three points.
Lemma 3.6 (Pattern-compression simplifies). Let S be a properly embedded con-
nected orientable surface that is non-separating. Let S ′ be obtained from S by
pattern-compressing or compressing. Then at least one component of S ′ is also
non-separating and has smaller pattern-complexity than S.
Proof. The lemma is straightforward and well known in the case of a compression.
So suppose that we pattern-compress S. We decrease −4χ(S) by 4 and increase
|S ∩ P | by at most 2. So χp certainly goes down by at least 2. Note that at
least one component of S ′ is non-separating. Thus, the lemma is proved in the
case where S ′ is connected. However, we need to consider the possibility that we
have created a separating component S1′ with negative pattern-complexity. Suppose
that this occurs. Let S2′ be the other component of S ′ which is necessarily non-
separating. Note that S1′ is a disc and it must intersect P at least twice by the
definition of a pattern-compression. If S1′ has three points of intersection with
P , then |S2′ ∩ P | < |S ∩ P | and hence χp (S2′ ) < χp (S). If S1′ has two points of
intersection with P , then both of these must lie in S by the definition of a pattern-
compression. Hence, again χp (S2′ ) < χp (S).                                       □
Corollary 3.7 (Simplest surface pattern-incompressible). Let (M, P ) be a com-
pact orientable 3-manifold with a boundary pattern. Let S be a properly embedded
connected orientable surface that is non-separating and that has smallest pattern-
complexity among all such surfaces. Then S is pattern-incompressible and incom-
pressible.
Lemma 3.8 (Essential pushes forward). Let M be a compact orientable 3-manifold
with essential boundary pattern P . Let S be a connected pattern-incompressible
incompressible surface properly embedded in M , which is not a disc intersecting P
in at most 3 points. Then M \\S inherits an essential boundary pattern P ′ .
Proof. Let D be a disc properly embedded in M \\S with ∂D ∩ P ′ at most three
points. The curve ∂D may run through parts of ∂(M \\S) coming from ∂M and
parts coming from S. Note however the points where it swaps must be points of
∂D ∩ P ′ , and that at most one side of any point of ∂D ∩ P ′ lies in S. Hence, at
most one arc or simple closed curve of ∂D\\P ′ lies in S.
  Case 1. ∂D is disjoint from S.
  Then ∂D ⊂ ∂M . Since P is essential, ∂D bounds a disc D′ in ∂M intersecting
P in the empty set, an arc or a tripod. If S intersects D′ , then pick a simple closed
      INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                 11


curve of S ∩ D′ innermost in D′ . The disc this bounds cannot be a compression
disc for S. Hence, S must be a disc. By assumption, S intersects P in at least 4
points. Hence, it intersects some edge of P ∩ D′ more than once. We can therefore
find a pattern-compression disc for S, which is contrary to assumption. Hence, D′
is disjoint from S, and therefore lies in ∂(M \\S). This verifies that D is not a
violating disc for P ′ .
  Case 2. ∂D lies in S.
  By the incompressibility of S, ∂D bounds a disc D′ in S. This is a disc in
∂(M \\S) disjoint from P ′ . So D is not violating.
  Case 3. ∂D intersects S in an arc.
   Then ∂D\\S intersects P at most once. Since D is not a pattern-compression
disc for S, D ∩ S separates off a disc D1 of S intersecting P in at most one point.
Then, D ∪ D1 is a disc properly embedded in M , intersecting P in at most two
points. Since P is essential, there is therefore a disc D2 in ∂M with ∂D2 = ∂(D∪D1 )
and with D2 ∩ P either empty or an arc. Therefore, D1 ∪ D2 is a disc in ∂(M \\S)
intersecting P ′ in an arc or a tripod.

of disjoint non-parallel properly embedded incompressible boundary-incompressible
surfaces in a compact 3-manifold. In the following section, we give an alternative
proof. Although longer than the proof in [12], it will be more useful because it will
lead to a good bound on the length of the resulting hierarchy.

                  4. Handle structures and normal surfaces
   Our algorithm deals with partial hierarchies. The way that the surfaces in these
partial hierarchies are encoded is as normal surfaces. Normal surface theory takes
place in a 3-manifold equipped either with a triangulation or with a handle struc-
ture. We mostly use handle structures, as they behave better than triangulations
when the 3-manifold is decomposed along a normal surface. Normal surfaces in
handle structures have been used in the theory of hierarchies since the work of
Haken [4] and Waldhausen [22]. One of their particular uses is as a method for
controlling the length of hierarchies, in other words the number of decompositions
until the manifold has been cut up into balls.
Definition 4.1. In this paper, a handle structure on a 3-manifold or a 2-manifold

    (1) each i-handle Di × Dk−i (k = 2 or 3) intersects the handles of lower index

Definition 4.10. Let H be a handle structure of a compact orientable 3-manifold

Lemma 4.11 (Normalising a surface). Let M be a compact orientable 3-manifold
with boundary pattern P . Let S be a compact orientable surface properly embedded
in M transverse to P that is non-trivial in H2 (M, ∂M ). Then, after applying a
sequence of isotopies preserving P , compressions and pattern-compressions, some
component of the resulting surface is normal and either non-separating or a violating
disc. Moreover, if S is standard in H, then the weight and extended weight of the
resulting normal surface are at most the weight and extended weight of S.

0-handle H0 in anything other than discs, then we may modify it, keeping S ∩ ∂H0
unchanged but ensuring that each of these curves bounds a disc in H0 . This process
is achieved by a sequence of isotopies and compressions.
    We may further assume that S has no component that is a boundary-parallel disc,
as we could remove this component and the surface would still be homologically
non-trivial.
    Suppose that S is not normal. Then by Lemma 4.9, S has a simplifying disc.
The first and second types of simplifying disc can be used to pattern-isotope S to
reduce its weight without increasing |S ∩ H1 |. The third type of simplifying disc is
a potential pattern-compression disc for S. It satisfies (1) and (2) in Definition 3.1,
but possibly not (3). We can pattern-compress S along this disc. If S was a violating
disc, then some component of the resulting surface is again a violating disc, and
we work with this instead. If S was non-separating, but the pattern-compression
produces a violating disc, then we work with this violating disc instead. However,
if the pattern-compression creates a component that is a boundary parallel disc,
but not a violating disc, then discard it. The resulting surface has smaller extended
weight and no greater weight than S. On the other hand, if the pattern-compression
does not create a violating disc or a boundary-parallel disc, then some component
of the resulting surface is non-separating, has smaller χp and no greater weight
or extended weight. The fourth type of simplifying disc specifies an isotopy of S,
which reduces the number of components of S ∩ H1 and that leaves the weight of
S unchanged. At each stage, we are either reducing χp or leaving it unchanged
and reducing extended weight. So this process terminates. The resulting surface
is normal, connected, and orientable. Moreover, it is either non-separating or a
violating disc.                                                                      □

Proposition 5.4 (Create admissible handle structure). Let (M, P ) be a

of F. It will strictly decrease the number of 1-handles of H at each stage, and so
it is guaranteed to terminate. Suppose that there is a 0-handle D of F that either
has negative index or has zero index and intersects P . Then D has at most one arc
of intersection with F 1 . (See Figures 9 and 10.)

the arc lies in a 2-handle H2 of H, and we may remove H1 ∪ H2 from the handle
structure. There may be one or two arcs of P running along H1 . If so, then we
need to replace each component of F 1 ∩ H2 with one or two arcs of P .

whether ∂D bounds a disc D′ in ∂M intersecting P in either the empty set, an
arc or a tripod. If so, D ∪ D′ bounds a 3-ball in M , by irreducibility. We remove
this submanifold bounded by D ∪ D′ , and we insert into D the boundary pattern
that was in D′ . On the other hand, if ∂D does not bound such a disc D′ , then D

properly embedded disc in H0 , disjoint from F, that also forms a violating disc
and intersecting P fewer times. Hence, we may assume that D is normal, and the
algorithm terminates with this disc. Suppose now that D has zero index, and hence
is a square. If it is a trivial square, it bounds a disc D′ in ∂M intersecting P as in
(1) or (2) of Lemma 3.10. We can then remove the submanifold bounded by D ∪D′ ,
and insert into D the boundary pattern that was in D′ . If D is a non-trivial square,
then we decompose along it.

bound a disc in ∂H0 intersecting P in the empty set, an arc or a tripod. There is
always such a curve C when ∂H0 \\(F ∪ P ) is not a union of discs. In that case, we

pick C to be boundary parallel in a component of ∂H0 \\(F ∪ P ) that is not a disc.

embedded in H0 bounded by C is a violating disc. On the other hand, if there is
such a disc D′ , then we can remove the submanifold bounded by D ∪ D′ and insert

the squares are all disjoint from each other, and any violating disc that is produced

Theorem 6.2 (Build non-separating surface). There is an algorithm that
takes, as its input, a triangulation T or handle structure H for a compact orientable
3-manifold M with boundary pattern P , and with b1 (M ) > 0. In the case where
∂M is non-empty, we also assume that H has no 3-handles. The algorithm builds
a connected, non-separating, properly embedded, oriented, normal surface S with
weight at most c|T | or extended weight at most c|H| . Here, c is a constant that is
universal in the case of a triangulation. In the case of a handle structure, c depends
on the number N , which is the maximum, over each 0-handle of H, of the number
of components of intersection between the 0-handle and each of the following: the
1-handles, the 2-handles, the vertices of P and the edges of P . The running time
is bounded above by a polynomial function of |T | or |H|, where the polynomial is
universal for triangulations and depends on N in the case of handle structures.
Proof. Let us initially assume that M is given via a triangulation T , since this case
is more straightforward.
   We are assuming that H 1 (M ) is non-zero. Any non-zero element of this group
can be represented by a simplicial 1-cocycle, and indeed such a 1-cocycle is unique
if we require that its evaluation on each edge in some given maximal tree in the
1-skeleton is zero. So, one can set up a system of linear equations, with a vari-
able for each oriented edge not in this tree, and an equation specifying that the
total evaluation around each face is zero. Then any non-zero real solution to these
equations represents a non-trivial element of H 1 (M ; R). In fact, one can ensure
that the coefficients of this element are rational, by imposing enough extra con-
straints specifying that some edges have zero evaluation, until the solution space is
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                    27


1-dimensional, and then requiring that the sum of the coefficients is 1. Then we can
multiply through by a suitable integer to obtain a non-zero solution with integer
coefficients, which is a 1-cocycle representing a non-trivial element of H 1 (M ).
    The size of the co-ordinates of this solution are bounded above by 212|T | for the
following reason. The system of equations has at most n = E − V + 1 variables,
where E and V are the number of edges of T . This is because a maximal tree has
V − 1 edges in it. Note that E − V + 1 ≤ E ≤ 6|T |. There is an equation for
each face, as well as an equation that requires that the sum of the coefficients is 1.
We also have extra conditions that require that some edges evaluate to zero. These
equations are combined into a single matrix equation Av = w, where A represents
the above equations, v is the solution, and w is the vector with all zeroes except the
final entry, which is 1. We are assuming that the kernel of A is zero, and hence the
rank of A is equal to the number of variables. There is a square submatrix B of A
with the same number of columns of A having the same rank. Thus B is invertible.
We have Bv = w′ , where w′ consists of some of the entries of w, and so all entries
are zero apart from one. Then v = B −1 w′ . Note that B −1 = (det(B))−1 adj(B),
where det(B) is the determinant of B and adj(B) is the adjugate matrix. We can
obtain an integral solution by multiplying through by det(B). Each co-ordinate of
this integral solution is an entry of the adjugate matrix, which is the determinant
of a minor of B. The size   √ of this  is at most the product of the l2 norms of the
rows, which is at most 3 n ≤ 2 ≤ 212|T | , as required. Note that the procedure
                          n        2n

used to produce this solution runs in polynomial time, since it involves computing
(n − 1)2 determinants.
    From this, one can build a properly embedded surface representing an element
of H2 (M, ∂M ) dual to this 1-cocycle c, as follows. First, for each edge e of the
triangulation, place a collection of |c(e)| points along the interior of the edge. These
will be the intersection points between e and the surface. Pick a circular ordering
on the edges of each face. Since the evaluation of c around the face is zero, we may
join the points in the edges in pairs by a collection of disjoint normal arcs, so that
for each arc, one endpoint lies on an edge with positive evaluation and one endpoint
lies on an edge with negative evaluation. These arcs form the intersection between
the surface and the face. In the boundary of each tetrahedron, these arcs combine
to form a collection of normal curves, each with length three or four, and we may
fill these in with normal triangles and squares. The result is a compact oriented
properly embedded surface dual to c. Any component of this surface can be used
as the required surface S. Such a component can be found using the algorithm of
Agol, Hass and Thurston [1, Corollary 17].
    We now consider the case where M comes equipped with a handle structure
H. When ∂M is non-empty, we are assuming that H has no 3-handles. This
determines a cell structure on M , as follows. Each handle becomes a 3-cell. The
2-cells arise from the components of intersection between handles, as well as the
components of intersection between a handle and ∂M \\P . Some of these surfaces
might not be discs, in which case we decompose them along arcs forming 1-cells,
until they become discs. We will explain how to do this below. The remaining
1-cells arise from the components of intersection between any three handles, as well
as the components of intersection between pairs of handles and ∂M \\P , and also
the components of intersection between a handle and an edge of P . Finally, the
0-cells arise as follows: the components of intersection between four handles; the
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                     28


components of intersection between any three handles and ∂M \\P ; the components
of intersection between any two handles and P ; the vertices of P . One arrangement
when non-disc components of intersection between handles arises is when a 1-handle
of H is disjoint from H2 and P . In that case, we insert a single vertical 1-cell running
along the 1-handle. The other scenario where non-disc components arise is in the
boundary of a 0-handle, since the intersection between the 0-handle and ∂M \\P
or H3 may have components other than discs. In that case, we insert properly
embedded 1-cells into the planar surface, but without adding any new 0-cells.
   We now pick a maximal tree in the 1-skeleton of this cell structure. Our approach
will depend on whether M is closed or has non-empty boundary. When M is closed,
we pick a maximal tree in the 1-skeleton of each 3-handle. The union of these trees
forms a forest in M . In each 1-handle disjoint from the 2-handles, we also add
the new vertical 1-cell to this forest. We then extend this to a maximal tree in
all of M . The effect of this is that, for each 1-cocycle c that is zero on the edges
in the maximal tree, the evaluation of each 1-cell in each 3-handle is zero. This is
automatic for 1-cells in the maximal tree. But for any other 1-cell in a 3-handle, the
evaluation of c on that edge is equal to the evaluation of the loop that runs along
the edge, then through the maximal tree back to the start of the edge. This loop lies
entirely in the 3-handle, and so is homotopically trivial, and hence its evaluation
is zero, as claimed. When M has non-empty boundary, we pick a maximal tree
in the 1-skeleton of each 2-handle and again any new vertical edges in a 1-handle
are included in this forest. We also add each component of P ∩ H1 to this forest.
Furthermore, for each disc component of H1 ∩ ∂M , we pick one of its arcs of
intersection with H0 and we declare that all but one of the 1-cells in that arc lies
in the forest. We again extend this to a maximal tree in M . This has the effect
that the evaluation of each 1-cell in each 2-handle is actually zero.
   As above, such cocycles c are solutions to a system of linear equations. The
variables correspond to the oriented 1-cells not in the maximal tree, and there is
an equation for each 2-cell. The argument above also gives that there is a non-zero
integral cocycle with the property that its value on each 1-cell has modulus that is
bounded above by an exponential function of |H|, where the exponent only depends
on the uniform type of H.
   We now construct a surface S that is dual to this 1-cocycle c. For each 1-cell e, we
insert |c(e)| points into its interior. These will be the points of intersection between
S and e. Each 2-cell is a polygon, and we have specified the intersection between S
and the boundary of this polygon. Since the total evaluation of the oriented edges
in this boundary is zero, we can insert transversely oriented arcs into the interior
of the polygon joining these points. Note that the 1-cells that are vertical in the
1-handles have zero evaluation, for the following reason. Each such 1-cell is either in
the boundary of a 3-handle, or in the intersection between a 2-handle and ∂M , or is
an inserted 1-cell in a 1-handle disjoint from the 2-handles. For each such 1-cell, its
evaluation is zero. Each 2-cell in the vertical boundary of a 1-handle has four sides,
and we have just shown that the two vertical sides have zero evaluation. Hence, the
remaining two sides have the same evaluation. So the arcs that we insert into such
a 2-cell can be isotoped to be vertical. We can also ensure that the arcs in the two
polygons at the ends of this 1-handle correspond via the product structure. In the
boundary of each handle, these arcs patch together to form closed curves. We can
insert discs into the handle bounded by these curves. The result is a transversely
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                   29


oriented, properly embedded surface dual to c. It is standard because it is disjoint
from the 3-handles and respects the product structure of the other handles of H.
For 1-handles, we have already arranged this. For 2-handles in a closed 3-manifold
M , this follows from the fact that the surface misses the 3-handles and intersects
each 1-cell in points of the same sign. When M has non-empty boundary, the
surface misses the 2-handles. Note also that all the points of intersection between
the surface and any arc component of H0 ∩ H1 ∩ ∂M have the same sign.
   We need to arrange that this surface is in fact normal. We observe that when
M is closed, we have in fact already arranged this. When M is closed, the only
normality condition that is not automatic is (3) of Definition 4.6. However, for
each disc that we have inserted into a 0-handle, it runs over each component of F 1
at most once. For if it ran over a component of F 1 more than once, then there
would be two arcs of intersection in that component of F 1 with opposite transverse
orientations. However, all the arcs of intersection between S and any component
of F 1 are compatibly oriented.
   We now deal with the case where M has non-empty boundary. Each component
of S ∩ H0 intersects each component of ∂F 0 \\(P ∪ F 1 ) and each component of
intersection between the edges of P and H0 at most once, since S intersects each
such component in points of the same sign. (This latter property is convenient at
this stage, but we will not maintain it in the modifications that we make below.)
So this results in finitely many types of disc in S ∩ H0 . For each such disc type D,
we define its complexity to be |D ∩ P |2 + |D ∩ F 1 |2 . This quantity is bounded above
by a constant depending only on the type of H. The complexity of S is equal to the
sum of the complexities of its disc types. Note that in this sum, we only consider
one disc of S ∩ H0 of each type in each 0-handle. Hence, the complexity is bounded
above by a constant times |H0 |, where the constant only depends on the uniform
type of H.
   We now examine the normality conditions (1)-(7) from Definition 4.6. Conditions
(1) and (2) are automatically satisfied. In our setting, S is disjoint from the 2-
handles, and so (3) and (5) are satisfied. If a curve of S∩∂H0 intersects a component
of ∂H0 − (F ∪ P ) in more than one arc, contradicting (4), then we can pattern
compress such a disc. We can pattern compress all discs of this type. For each such
disc, this creates two discs D1 and D2 . Discs of these types may already exist in S,
but this does not affect our argument. The points of intersection between D and P
are shared out between D1 and D2 . The same is true for D ∩ F 1 . Hence, the total
complexity of S decreases. Note that when a pattern-compression is performed,
then the class that S represents in H2 (M, ∂M ) is unchanged. A similar argument
works when (6) in the definition of normality is violated. We pattern compress the
disc D to discs D1 and D2 . This introduces two points of intersection with P , but
the complexity of these discs still decreases. Finally note that condition (7) must
be satisfied, since if it were not, then we obtain two points of intersection between
S and a component of H1 ∩ H0 ∩ ∂M that are oriented towards or away from each
other, contrary to our construction. Thus we have constructed a normal surface.
Any component of this is our required normal surface.                                □

It is a famous example of a 3-manifold admitting a hyperbolic structure with to-
tally geodesic boundary. Totally geodesic boundary components of a hyperbolic
3-manifold are always incompressible. However, the application of our algorithm
gives a straightforward alternative proof of this.
    The first decomposing surface is a thrice-punctured sphere as shown in (ii) of
Figure 12. The exterior of this surface, with its boundary pattern, is shown in
(iii). The next surface is the four discs shown in red in (iv). The exterior of these

checked that they are essential. Hence, the empty boundary pattern for the tripus
manifold is essential. In other words, the boundary is incompressible. Furthermore,
the manifold is irreducible.
    A skeptical reader may object that we have not quite followed the algorithm
from Section 6, in several ways. We have not specified an initial handle structure H
for the manifold. Of course we could have done so, and we could have realised the
first surface as normal, and we could have decomposed H along it, thereby forming
a new handle structure H2 , and so on. Although a computer implementation would
have used handle structures, we have chosen to avoid them in this example, for ease
of exposition.

union of discs which is separating, whereas the surfaces in Step (6) are required to
be connected and non-separating. However, we could instead have chosen three of
the four discs, and decomposed along of each of these in turn. These are connected
and non-separating. The resulting manifold would have been a single 3-ball. Its
boundary pattern would have been essential, by an application of Lemma 3.2 and
because decomposition along the fourth disc gives an essential pattern. We chose
to decompose along four discs rather than three, again for ease of visualisation and
exposition.

to leave the hierarchy construction loop. So suppose instead that we had initially

                                     9. The number of steps
    There are various steps in the algorithm from Theorem 1.3 that are not described
with enough precision to be able to estimate their running time. So, we will initially
content ourselves with an analysis of the number of times that the algorithm goes
through any of the steps in Section 6. We do this in terms of two quantities:
     (1) the maximal length L of any of the hierarchies; thus the variable ℓ will be
          at most L throughout;
     (2) the maximal pattern-complexity g of any decomposing surface.
At present, we will not attempt to bound these two quantities. But we can bound
the number of steps in the algorithm in terms of L and g.
    When the algorithm is in the hierarchy construction loop, namely Steps (2)-(8),
it continues until a violating disc is found or until it reaches a manifold Mℓ+1 with
b1 (Mℓ+1 ) = 0. The number of times it can iterate this loop is at most L. By
this stage, we have produced a list of surfaces S1 , . . . , Sℓ with pattern-complexity
χp (S1 ), . . . , χp (Sℓ ). It may be the case that the final surface Sℓ has negative pattern-
complexity, in which case it is a violating disc (because it is assumed to be connected
and non-separating), and we do not include it in this list. The algorithm then
proceeds through the surface simplification process, namely Steps (9)-(15). If it
does not terminate here, then we pattern-compress or compress some Sj and thereby
reduce its pattern-complexity by at least one. We discard the later surfaces and
return to Step (7) to create new ones.

      10. A refined algorithm using interior parallelity bundles
    We saw in Proposition 9.1 that the quantity L, which provides an upper bound
to the length of any of the partial hierarchies that we construct, is key to control-
ling the running time of the algorithm. With our current algorithm, it is not so
straightforward to give good estimates on L. This is because the proof at the end of
Section 5 that the hierarchies terminate uses the ordered pairs (cq (H), m(H)). Al-
though lexicographical ordering on these pairs is enough to establish termination,
it does not establish how quickly this happens. Now under many circumstances
cq (H) strictly decreases. The quantity m(H) only becomes relevant when H has a
non-separating vertical surface in its interior parallelity bundle. Therefore, in this
section, we show how we may remove every component of the interior parallelity
bundle that is not an I-bundle over a disc. This will lead to a somewhat more
complicated algorithm. But it will mean that we can provide a linear bound on
L in terms of the initial q-complexity of H. The first change that we make to the
algorithm is as follows.
First modification to the algorithm: After Step (2), once the handle structure has
been made admissible, we then do the following:
   (2′ ) Replace each component of the interior parallelity bundle that is an I-
         bundle over a disc by a single 2-handle.
  (2′′ ) Cut along the vertical boundary components of the remaining I-bundles and
         further decompose these using squares into 3-balls with essential boundary
         patterns.
   This ensures that the resulting 3-manifold has interior parallelity bundle that
consists solely of 2-handles and hence cannot contain a non-separating vertical sur-
face. Therefore, by Proposition 5.9, the q-complexity cq (Hi ) of the handle structure
Hi strictly decreases when we decompose along a connected non-separating normal
surface Si .
   However, this algorithm has an extra complication. In the previous algorithm
described in Section 6, if a non-trivial square was found to be pattern-compressible,
this led to a violating disc. This is in turn was used to simplify an earlier surface
in the hierarchy. Hence, pattern-compressed squares never survive in the hierarchy.
But what about the annuli that are used in step (2′′ )? What if these are compressed
or pattern-compressed? We now explain how to deal with this situation.

                    11. Handle structures of uniform type
   Our algorithm starts with a handle structure H. We then decompose this handle
structure along a sequence of surfaces. Suppose that each of the 0-handles of the
initial handle structure H falls into one of finitely many ‘types’. For example, if H
had been dual to an ideal triangulation, then for each 0-handle H, its intersection
with F is a thickened version of the complete graph on 4 vertices. If H had been
dual to a triangulation, then for each 0-handle H, H ∩ F is a thickened version of
a subgraph of the complete graph on 4 vertices. We want to maintain this local
finiteness as we proceed down the hierarchy. In this section, we show how to achieve
this.
Definition 11.1. Let H and H ′ be 0-handles in handle structures H and H′ for
(M, P ) and (M ′ , P ′ ). We say that they have the same type if there is a homeo-
morphism H → H ′ sending H ∩ F 0 (H) homeomorphically to H ′ ∩ F 0 (H′ ), sending
homeomorphically H ∩F 1 (H) to H ′ ∩F 1 (H′ ), and sending H ∩P homeomorphically
to H ′ ∩ P ′ .
Definition 11.2. We say that a handle structure H has uniform type if there is a
finite list of handle types such that any 0-handle of H is one of these types.
    Of course, this definition only really makes sense if the list of handle types is
given in advance, and then the requirement that H is of uniform type forces its
0-handles to conform to this list of types. But this slightly imprecise wording is
useful, because it will be convenient occasionally to enlarge our finite list of types.
An example is given below in Theorem 11.3.
    It will be important that we only use handle structures of uniform type. This is
for many reasons. For example, if we wish to write down a normal surface, we will
typically do so using its normal vector, and it is therefore important that we can
list the different types of normal disc that can arise within a handle.
    The following result ensures that the handle structures in our partial hierarchies
have uniform type.
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                     39


Theorem 11.3 (Maintaining uniform type). Let H be a handle structure of uni-
form type for a compact orientable irreducible 3-manifold M with boundary pattern
P . Let H′ be the handle structure of a 3-manifold M ′ with boundary pattern P ′
obtained from H by a finite sequence of the following moves:
    (1) decomposition along a normal surface that is disjoint from the interior par-
         allelity bundle, no component of which is a boundary-parallel disc, and ap-
         plied to an admissible handle structure;
    (2) application of Create admissible handle structure in Proposition
         5.4;
    (3) decomposition along vertical boundary components of the interior parallelity
         bundle;
    (4) application of (Pattern-)compressible vertical boundary in Propo-
         sition 10.1.
Then H′ is of uniform type, although possibly with a larger set of handle types.
Moreover, when H′ is admissible and its interior parallelity bundle consists of I-
bundles over discs, then for each 0-handle H of H, there are only finitely many
possibilities for the following subsets:
    (1) the intersection between H and the 0-handles of H′ ;
    (2) H ∩ F 0 (H′ );
    (3) H ∩ F 1 (H′ );
    (4) H ∩ P ′ ,
up to the following operations:
    (1) isotopy of H preserving H ∩ H1 , H ∩ H2 and H ∩ P ;
    (2) removing a small collar neighbourhod of a subset of ∂M ′ ;
    (3) re-embedding H′ ∩ H in H in a way that leaves F 0 (H′ ), F 1 (H′ ) and P ′ ∩

location of the 2-handles of H′ . We are specifying their attaching locus via H ∩
F 0 (H′ ) and H ∩F 1 (H′ ), but we do not specify where the remainder of the 2-handles
lie.
    The re-embedding of H′ ∩ H might require some further explanation. Suppose
that we had applied a sequence of modifications to H, giving the handle structure
H′ , and then suppose that we made some further modifications to give another
handle structure H′′ . Suppose that H′ ∩ H and H′′ ∩ H differ solely by a Dehn
twist about a disc properly embedded in M ′ and lying in H′ ∩ H. Then H′ ∩ H
and H′′ ∩ H would differ by operation (3) above, and so, for the purposes of this
proposition, would be viewed as equivalent.
Proof. We define a temporary notion of complexity for a 0-handle H of a handle
structure, called t-complexity. It will be a list of three integers. The first element of
the triple is the sum of the indices of the components of H ∩ F with positive index.
The second element is |H ∩ F 1 |. The third element is |H ∩ F 0 |. We compare two
such triples in the usual way, using lexicographical ordering. The set of triples is
then well-ordered.
   We will prove by induction on t-complexity that a 0-handle type H gives rise to
only finitely many handle types of H′ ∩ H under a sequence of the operations in (1)-
(4) above. Moreover, we will show that the union of the 0-handles of H′ ∩ H with
positive index embed in H in one of only finitely many ways, up to the equivalence
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                     40


described in (1)-(3) at the end of the proposition. This will also establish the second
part of the proposition, because when H′ is admissible and its interior parallelity
bundle consists of I-bundles over discs, then all its 0-handles have positive index.
   The induction starts with H having t-complexity (0, 0, 0). Then H is an isolated
0-handle and none of the procedures in (1)-(4) affect it.
   We now prove the inductive step. We consider when one of procedures (1)-(4) is
applied to a 0-handle H. We may assume that the result is not a single 0-handle
H ′ that is the same type as H and where H ′ is obtained from H by removing a
small collar on a subset of ∂H, as in this case there is nothing to prove.
   In the procedure Create admissible handle structure, there are three
types of operation that are performed. One type is where we collapse a 1-handle
and 2-handle pair, where the 1-handle has at most two arcs of P running along
it. The effect on H ∩ F 0 is possibly to remove some components of H ∩ F 0 with
non-positive index. The effect on H ∩F 1 is to remove some components and replace
them by at most two parallel arcs of P ∩ H. This does not increase the index of
any component of H ∩ F. If H is modified, then it reduces at least one of |H ∩ F 1 |
and |H ∩ F 0 |, and it does not increase either of them. There are only finitely such
modifications that can be applied to H. Hence, the induction is proved in this case.
   The second type of operation in Create admissible handle structure is
the removal of isolated 0-handles of F with non-positive index. Each such operation
does not affect the index of the other components of F ∩ H. It leaves |H ∩ F 1 |
unchanged and it reduces |H ∩ F 0 |. Hence, again the inductive step is proved.
   The third type of operation in Create admissible handle structure is when
there is a simple closed curve C in ∂H\\F that intersects the pattern at most three
times. It is assumed that C does not bound a disc in ∂H but that it does bound a
disc in ∂M intersecting P in the empty set, an arc or a tripod. When ∂H\\(F ∪ P )
is not a union of discs, then C is chosen to be boundary parallel in a component of
∂H\\(F ∪ P ) that is not a disc. Hence, in that case, there are only finitely many
possibilities for C. On the other hand, when ∂H\\(F ∪ P ) is a union of discs, then
again there are only finitely many possibilities for C. Now C bounds a properly
embedded disc D in H, and the operation that is performed is to remove the 3-ball
separated off by D, and possibly to insert into D an arc or tripod. The effect of this
on H is to remove some components of F and to possibly insert some new pattern.
This does not increase the index of the components of H ∩ F, it does not increase
|H ∩ F 1 |, and it strictly reduces |H ∩ F 0 |. Hence again the inductive step is proved.
   The effect when we decompose along vertical boundary components of the in-
terior parallelity bundle is similar to the case of Create admissible handle
structure. The effect is to replace some components of H ∩ F 1 by two parallel
arcs of H ∩ P . This leaves index unchanged, but reduces |H ∩ F 1 |. It also removes
some components of H ∩ F 0 that are disjoint from P and that intersect F 1 in two
arcs, and replaces them by two arcs of P . Again, this leaves index unchanged, and
reduces |H ∩ F 0 |.
   In (Pattern-)compressible vertical boundary, submanifolds of the man-
ifold are removed. The effect on H is either to remove it entirely (in which case
there is nothing to prove) or possibly to remove some components of F 1 and re-
place them with an arc of P , and possibly to remove some components of F 0 that
intersect F 1 in two arcs and are disjoint from P , and replace them with an arc of
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                  41


P . As argued above, this does not increase the index of any component of F or
|H ∩ F 0 |, and it strictly reduces |H ∩ F 1 |.
   We now examine what happens when H is decomposed along a normal surface.
We are assuming that the handle structure is admissible in this case. Hence, every
0-handle of H ∩ F 0 has non-negative index. So, H has non-negative index. Fur-
thermore, if it has zero index, then it is an interior parallelity handle, and we are
assuming that the normal surface is disjoint from the interior parallelity bundle.
So, we may assume that the index of H is positive.
   The normal surface intersects H in discs. We can decompose along these one at
a time. So we focus on one such disc D. Note that there are only finitely normal
disc types in H. This disc D divides H into two 0-handles H0 and H1 , both of
which have non-negative index, by Proposition 5.5. The sum of these indices is
I(H). Hence, if they are both positive, then H0 and H1 have smaller index, and
so we are done by induction in this case. So suppose that one 0-handle H0 has
zero index and the other handle H1 has positive index. Since D is normal, each
component of F 1 ∩ H is either divided into two by D or lies wholly in one of the
handles H0 or H1 . If one component of F 1 ∩ H ends up wholly in H0 , then H1 has
smaller t-complexity than H, and we are again done by induction.
   If ∂D lies wholly in F ∩ H, then the component of H0 ∩ F incident to ∂D has
zero index and so must be an annulus disjoint from the pattern. Hence, in this
case, H1 is a copy of H and, given H, there are only finitely many possible types
for H0 .
   Suppose that ∂D is disjoint from F ∩ H. It is therefore properly embedded in
M . Since we are assuming that the decomposing surface has no component that
is a boundary-parallel disc, D must separate components of F ∩ H. Hence, in this
case, H1 has smaller index than H, which is contrary to assumption.
   So suppose that ∂D intersects F ∩ H in arcs. These must be boundary-parallel
in F .

Remark 11.5. The above proposition is crucial to the efficient certification that a
boundary pattern P for a compact orientable 3-manifold M is essential, as required
by Theorem 1.6. When M is irreducible, this is achieved by giving an essential
hierarchy for (M, P ). Now (M, P ) is given to us by means of a triangulation T ,
and so Theorem 11.3 guarantees that the resulting handle structures for all the
manifolds in the hierarchy are of uniform type. This is important at many points.
For example, in Proposition 12.1 below, a constant k depends on the type of the
handles. Hence, for all the handle structures that we consider, k is in fact bounded
above by a universal constant independent of M and the hierarchy.
   Another crucial conclusion from Theorem 11.3 is that when the handle structure
H′ for a manifold in our hierarchy is admissible and its interior parallelity bundle
consists of I-bundles over discs, then the intersection between the 0-handles of H′
and any tetrahedron of T is one of only finitely many types, where again this list of
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                    43


types is universal. In particular, the number of 0-handles of H′ is bounded above
by a universal constant times the number of tetrahedra of T . This is important
because many quantities depend on |H′ |, for example again in Proposition 12.1. It
is also central to the efficiency of our algorithm. For example, when a manifold in
the hierarchy is created and all its boundary components are 2-spheres, then we
need to examine whether the boundary pattern in these 2-sphere is essential. We
can do so efficiently, since the handle structure on this manifold has uniform type
and its number of handles is bounded above by a universal constant times |T |.
   Theorem 11.3 is an analogue of results known for sutured manifold hierarchies
(see [13, Proposition 6.3] and [16, Theorem 6.1]).

                   12. Fundamental decomposing surfaces
   Our goal in this section is to control the extended weight of our decomposing
surfaces.
Proposition 12.1 (Extended weight bound). Let M be a compact orientable irre-
ducible 3-manifold, other than a 3-ball, with non-empty boundary and an essential
boundary pattern P . Let H be a handle structure of (M, P ) of uniform type. Then
there is a constant k (depending only on the handle types of H) with the following
property. There is a compact orientable connected non-separating normal surface S
properly embedded in M with smallest pattern complexity among all such surfaces
and with extended weight at most k |H| .
    Our proof of this fact relies heavily on work of Tollefson and Wang [21]. A fairly
minor complication is that they worked with triangulations rather than handle
structures. Starting from H, it is straightforward to build a triangulation T of M
in which P is simplicial, satisfying the following conditions:
   (i) the number |T | of tetrahedra is at most c1 |H|, where c1 is a constant depending
       only on the handle types of H;
  (ii) any incompressible, pattern-incompressible normal surface S in T can be iso-
       toped, leaving P invariant, to form a standard surface in H with extended
       weight at most c2 w(S), where w(S) is the weight of S in T and c2 is a con-
       stant depending only on the handle types of H.
Hence, we now work with a triangulation T satisfying these conditions.
    We now recall the projective normal solution space associated with T . There are
7|T | types of normal triangles and squares in T . We assign a variable xi to each
type. When S is a normal surface, these variables count the number of triangles
and squares of each type in S, giving a list of 7|T | non-negative integers for S
called its normal surface vector and denoted (S). Such vectors satisfy a system of
linear constraints called the matching equations. There are three such equations for
each face of T with tetrahedra on both sides. For each such face, the triangles and
squares in the tetrahedron on one side of the face must patch together correctly
with the triangles and squares in the tetrahedron on the other side. The triangles
and squares on one side intersect the face in normal arcs, which come in three
different types. The matching equations assert that for each such normal arc type,
the number of triangles and squares on one side intersecting the face in arcs of
that form is equal to the number of triangles and squares on the other side also
intersecting that face in arcs of that form. The projective normal solution space is
the subset of R7|T | subject to the matching equations, the conditions xi ≥ 0 for each
      INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                  44

                      P
i, and the equation      xi = 1. It is a convex compact polyhedron. Each normal
surface S determines a pointP in the projective normal solution space by scaling all
its co-ordinates xi so that     xi = 1. The unique closed face of minimal dimension
in the projective normal solution space containing this point is called the face that
carries S and is denoted CS .
    A normal surface is said to be the normal sum of two properly embedded normal
surfaces G and H if its normal vector is (G)+(H). We denote the surface by G+H.
One cannot always form the normal sum of two normal surfaces, but this is possible
if they are both carried by the same face of the projective normal solution space.
A normal surface is said to be fundamental if it cannot be written as the normal
sum of two non-empty normal surfaces.

                    13. Certifying an inessential pattern
   In this section, we establish one part of Theorem 1.6, by showing that Essen-
tial boundary pattern is in co-NP. In the case where the given 3-manifold is
irreducible, this is a consequence of the following result, which we will prove in this
section.
Theorem 13.1 (Fundamental violating disc). Let M be a compact orientable ir-
reducible 3-manifold with a boundary pattern P . Let T be a triangulation for M in
which P is simplicial. If P is inessential, then it has a violating disc that is normal
and fundamental.
  The following result provides a violating disc in an important special case.
Theorem 13.2 (Fundamental compression disc). Let M be a compact orientable
irreducible 3-manifold with pattern P . Let T be a triangulation for M in which
P is simplicial. If ∂M \\P is compressible, then it has a compression disc that is
normal and fundamental.
   This result was proved in [18, Theorem 4.1.13]. Although that theorem does
not explicitly assert that the compression disc is fundamental, this is established
in the proof. So, in our proof of Theorem 13.1, we may assume that ∂M \\P is
incompressible. In the terminology of Matveev [18], this means that (M, P ) is
boundary-irreducible.
   We recall some of the standard terminology from normal surface theory. Let S
be a normal surface that is the normal sum of two surfaces S1 and S2 . We may
pattern-isotope one of the surfaces, preserving its normality throughout, so that
afterwards S1 and S2 intersect transversely in a collection of simple closed curves
and properly embedded arcs. Then each component of S1 \\S2 and S2 \\S1 is a
patch. It is a clean disc patch if it is a disc that is disjoint from the pattern and is
either disjoint from ∂M or intersects ∂M in a single arc.
       INCOMPRESSIBLE SURFACES, HIERARCHIES AND UNKNOT RECOGNITION                    46


    A summation S = S1 + S2 is in reduced form if there do not exist normal
surfaces S1′ and S2′ where each Si′ is pattern-isotopic to Si , and S = S1′ + S2′ , with
|S1′ ∩ S2′ | < |S1 ∩ S2 |.
    Let S be a compact surface properly embedded in M . A clean boundary-
compression disc for S is a disc embedded in M such that ∂D is the concatenation
of two arcs α and β, where

    (1) α = D ∩ S does not separate off a disc from S that is disjoint from P ;
    (2) β = D ∩ ∂M is disjoint from P .

When S has no clean boundary-compression disc, it is said to be cleanly boundary-
incompressible.
    Note that a violating disc that intersects P as few times as possible is cleanly
boundary-incompressible.
    When S is a normal sum of two normal surfaces S1 and S2 in general position, it
is well known (see for example [18, Section 3.2.2]) that S is obtained from S1 ∪S2 by
resolving the intersections S1 ∩ S2 as follows. A regular neighbourhood of S1 ∩ S2 is
removed from S1 and S2 . This regular neighbourhood is a collection of discs, annuli
and Möbius bands. At each component of S1 ∩ S2 , a pair of discs or annuli are
inserted, lying in a regular neighbourhood of that component of S1 ∩ S2 . These are
known as trace discs and annuli. This procedure is called a switch. There are two
possible ways to perform a switch. Only one choice of switch at each component
of S1 ∩ S2 leads to the normal surface S. These are called regular switches. The
other type of switch is an irregular switch. If a switch is made at each component
of S1 ∩ S2 and at least one of these switches is irregular, then the resulting surface
S ′ admits a disc D in some face of T , such that D ∩ S ′ is an arc in ∂D, and the
remainder of ∂D lies in an edge of T . When D is disjoint from ∂M , this leads
to an isotopy that reduces the weight of S ′ . When D intersects ∂M but not P ,
then it is a potential clean boundary-compression disc for S ′ . If it is not a clean
boundary-compression disc then, assuming ∂M \\P is incompressible, there is an
isotopy of S ′ leaving P fixed, that reduces its weight. On the other hand, when D
intersects P , then a small isotopy of D pushing it off the face makes it a potential
clean boundary-compression disc. There are two possible directions in which to
push D off the face and if neither direction gives a clean boundary-compression
disc, then, again assuming ∂M \\P is incompressible, S ′ must have been a disc
parallel to a disc in ∂M intersecting P in a single arc. This observation leads to
the following result.

                    14. Certifying an essential hierarchy
   In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem 1.6, by showing that Essen-
tial boundary pattern is in NP. Thus, we are given a triangulation T of a
compact orientable 3-manifold M with essential pattern P that is simplicial, and
we must provide a certificate, verifiable in polynomial time, that establishes that
P is indeed essential. We may assume that ∂M is non-empty, as otherwise there
is nothing to prove. Using the techniques at the end of the previous section, it
is not hard to reduce to the case where M is irreducible. We will describe this
reduction in more detail at the end of this section, but for the moment, suppose
that M is irreducible. Then, by Theorem 2.5, we know that (M, P ) admits an
essential hierarchy. Moreover, the existence of such a hierarchy guarantees that P
is essential. So, our goal is to write down such a hierarchy and prove that it is
essential. We know by Proposition 10.2 that there is such a hierarchy with length
that is linearly bounded in terms of the number of tetrahedra in T . Furthermore,
by Theorem 11.3, the intermediate 3-manifolds have handle structures of uniform
type. However, this only controls the local complexity of each handle, not the total
number of handles. Thus, it is not immediately clear how to encode the handle
structures of these manifolds. Nevertheless, we will establish the following result.

hierarchy for (M, P ) that may be described by an amount of data that is at most
a polynomial function of |H| and this hierarchy can be verified as essential by a
polynomial-time algorithm. Furthermore, for each decomposition
