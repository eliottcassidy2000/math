# Bounded bijections from Fitting decomposition on symmetry orbits

2026-10-20. PROVED elementary finite theorem. This is a structural algorithmic supplement to the session's circulant necklace construction. The general result concerns a linear map with one-dimensional kernel, not tournament types directly.

## 1. Routing leaves to branch vertices

Let f:Q→Q be a function on a finite set, with every vertex having 0, 1, or 2 incoming edges. Call the indegree-zero vertices sources and the indegree-two vertices branches. At each branch b designate exactly one incoming edge p(b)→b as cut. Delete those edges.

The remaining directed graph has indegree and outdegree at most one. Its source vertices are exactly the original sources, since cutting at a branch lowers its indegree from two to one. Its terminal vertices are exactly the designated predecessors p(b), one for each branch: a vertex has only one outgoing edge, so it cannot be designated for two different branches.

Each source follows a finite path ending at a terminal. It cannot enter a directed cycle, since that would give a vertex two remaining incoming edges. Conversely tracing the unique remaining predecessor of a terminal ends at a source; a backwards cycle cannot lead to a terminal under a function. Thus

    source s -> terminal p(b) -> branch b

is a bijection, with the displayed backward traversal its inverse. Cyclic components with no endpoints are irrelevant. This is an explicit path bijection, not a pairing by ranks. Any invariant constant along original edges is preserved.

## 2. Linear maps modulo commuting symmetries

Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over F_2, let D:V→V be linear with dim ker D=1, and let G be a finite subgroup of GL(V) commuting with D. D induces a function f on Q=V/G. Each f-fiber has size at most two: every orbit mapping to [y] has a representative mapping exactly to y, and D^{-1}(y) has either zero or two vectors. The stabilizer G_y can merge those two preimages, producing indegree one. Hence the routing theorem applies.

Use the Fitting decomposition

    V = N direct-sum W,  N=ker D^e,  W=im D^e,

where e is large enough that the kernels and images stabilize; choose the least such positive e. D is nilpotent on N and invertible on W. Since ker D has dimension one, its nilpotent part is one Jordan block of size e, although the proof only needs D^e N=0. Both summands are G-invariant.

An orbit is periodic under f exactly when it lies in W. The forward implication follows because if D^k v=g v, then D^{k ord(g)}v=v, which kills the nilpotent component; the reverse implication follows from the finiteness and invertibility of D on W. Every orbit reaches W within e steps.

For y in W, its two vector preimages are w and w+k, where w in W is the unique stable preimage and 0≠k in ker D⊆N. They lie in different G-orbits because one is stable and the other is not. Thus every periodic orbit vertex is a branch, with exactly one stable and one unstable predecessor.

## 3. The bounded cutting rule

At each periodic branch, cut its unique unstable incoming edge and keep its stable incoming edge. At other branches use any deterministic rule, for example lexicographic order of canonical representatives.

A path starting at a source stops at a cut edge before entering W. All sources are outside im D, so in particular outside W. After at most e applications of D it would enter W, and that entering edge is cut. Therefore the source-to-branch algorithm uses at most e forward applications. The inverse traverses at most e predecessor edges, using the two-preimage solver to find each predecessor and detect the source. There is no need to enumerate or traverse a long cycle in the invertible summand.

The distinction is useful: an arbitrary branch-cutting rule proves a finite bijection, but could route a path through a long periodic component. Fitting-aware cutting pays only the nilpotent depth. Neither conclusion asserts that computing G-orbit representatives is easy for an arbitrary group.

## 4. Binary cyclic words

Take V=F_2^m, G generated by cyclic shift R, and D=I+R. Write m=e u with e=2^{v_2(m)} and u odd. In F_2[x]/(x^m-1),

    x^m-1 = (x^u-1)^e,

and x+1 occurs to multiplicity e. Thus the nilpotent depth is exactly e, independently of the possibly long invertible recurrence cycles.

Stable membership has a short test: W is the ideal generated by (x+1)^e=x^e+1. Reduce the word polynomial modulo x^e+1, equivalently XOR its length-e blocks. The result is zero exactly for stable words. This is invariant under rotation. Solving D z=y is a cumulative-XOR operation; when solvable the two solutions differ by the all-one word. Canonical cyclic rotations then identify the one or two predecessor necklaces.

The session's necklace proof identifies source necklaces with odd total weight and branch necklaces with even-weight primitive root. This theorem upgrades that source/branch bijection to at most 2^{v_2(m)} derivative steps, with no enumeration of all necklaces needed per input. Each step can use straightforward quadratic-time canonical rotations and linear-time XOR. A direct implementation therefore takes O(2^{v_2(m)} m^2) bit operations, apart from elementary input/output encoding. Faster minimum-rotation algorithms can improve that bound but are not needed here.

For odd m the depth is one: every source is sent directly to its derivative, which is a branch. The general algorithm is an integration-and-routing extension of this simple case. If m=(p-1)/2 for an odd prime p, the step bound becomes 2^{v_2(p-1)-1}. This remains a necklace bijection until the separate prime-circulant identification is invoked.

## 5. Scope and reusable connection

This is where the previous session's LFSR primary-factor analysis becomes constructive: the repeated x+1 factor controls the running time of an orbit bijection, while the odd/invertible factors do not. The source and branch sets are different observables of a noninvertible dynamical system. The cycle decomposition is used to remove an algorithmic obstruction, not merely to count states.

The routing lemma is an elementary path argument. The cyclic derivative is the classical binary difference/Lempel operation; no claim is made that the operator itself is new. The session-specific point is its use, together with the invariant stable subspace and an explicit cut rule, in a structural source/branch correspondence relevant to circulant tournaments and sign-even graphs.
