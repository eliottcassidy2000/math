# A fixed reversal budget cannot follow a fixed prime alphabet

2026-11-27. This merges the [bounded backward-arc compiler](../constructive_path_compiler_backward_arc_budget_20261127/README.md), the [three-arc inverse census](../third_chain_three_arc_inverse_20261127/README.md), and [Session 302's two-run substitution, one-gap parity and S-unit argument](../session302_ternary_ray_birth_20261113/README.md). The new step is a **simultaneous parity lemma for every gap and every fixed arc budget**, rather than an extrapolation from the census.

**Theorem.** Fix a nonnegative integer b and a finite set P of primes. There are only finitely many distinct Hamiltonian-path counts with all prime factors in P that can occur in tournaments having at most b backward arcs in some order. In particular for the attained chain h_t=7·3^t, t≥2, the minimum backward-arc budget b(h_t) tends to infinity. The theorem does not locate the last exponent at any fixed budget and does not restrict unrestricted Chan–Pak realizability.

## 1. More than one gap: parity of the coefficient table

Take an order with at most b backward arcs. Mark their endpoints as individually named exceptional vertices. All other vertices occur in transitive gaps between consecutive marked vertices, plus transitive padding at the ends. The marked order and backward pairs form a finite pattern bank for fixed b. Drop leading and trailing padding, which cannot change H. Choose which gaps are empty; for this proof a positive gap remains positive throughout its chart. Positive gaps are modules: any vertex outside a gap sees its vertices uniformly.

A path skeleton lists the marked vertices once each and substitutes one occurrence of a gap label for each maximal positive-length visit to that gap. Two consecutive occurrences cannot carry the same label. Every cross-arc in the skeleton must be legal. A gap of length l visited k times fills in exactly k!S(l,k) ways, where S(l,k) is a Stirling number of the second kind. Indeed the vertices are assigned surjectively to the k ordered visit slots, and in each slot their internal forward order is forced. Thus, writing a_k for the number of legal skeletons with visit vector k, the complete count is

    H(l) = sum_k a_k prod_i k_i! S(l_i,k_i).

Each excursion between two consecutive visits to a given gap contains a marked vertex. Otherwise the intervening ordinary gaps only move in forward order and cannot return to it. More precisely a return must cross that gap backward along one of the b reversed pairs; each such pair is used at most once in a path. Consequently k_i≤b+1, so these are finite sums.

**New parity lemma.** If any k_i≥3 then a_k is even. Choose the first gap whose visit count is at least three. Swap the first two excursions between its visits. Both excursions start and end against the same module, so this preserves all incidences. It also preserves the entire visit vector, including for other gaps: the excursions' boundaries are occurrences of that chosen gap, and swapping cannot merge two visits to some other gap at a boundary. The excursions contain disjoint nonempty sets of the individually named marked vertices, so they are different. The swap is a fixed-point-free involution. This extends Session 302's one-module argument to the multivariate response needed for the inverse problem.

Expand the Stirling factors by inclusion–exclusion into the integer-base form H(l)=sum_v c_v prod_i v_i^(l_i), dropping base zero at positive lengths. All coefficients are integers. Pairing removes every skeleton with some k_i≥3 modulo 2, and k_i=2 gives 2^(l_i)−2, whose sole odd-base coefficient is even. Thus the part of the coefficient table supported on **all-odd base vectors** has exactly one nonzero coefficient modulo 2, at (1,…,1). Its coefficient is odd because the skeleton with exactly one visit per positive gap counts the Hamiltonian paths of the tournament with all those gaps contracted to one vertex, and [Rédei's parity theorem](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.05569) applies. This is an exact statement about the coefficients, strictly stronger than knowing H(l) is odd when evaluated at integer lengths.

## 2. Fixed prime support cannot absorb that odd part

Suppose infinitely many different P-smooth heights occur at budget b. Pass to a fixed endpoint pattern and fixed zero gaps. Along a subsequence every remaining length is either constant or tends to infinity, and there is a fixed p in P for which v_p(H) tends to infinity. The latter choice is possible because distinct positive integers supported on P cannot all have every p-adic exponent bounded.

Write each equality as a sum of signed terms, including the term −H. Every term is in one fixed finitely generated multiplicative subgroup of the rationals: take the primes dividing the finitely many positive bases and nonzero coefficients, the primes in P, and the sign. Partition the terms of each equality into minimal nonempty vanishing subsums, and pass to a fixed partition (only finitely many partitions exist). Within any one block the ratios of its terms take only finitely many values. Indeed divide by the negative of one term, obtaining a nondegenerate unit equation, and apply [Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2002/155-3/p04). Hence the differences of v_p within a block are bounded.

It follows that all terms with **bounded** p-adic valuation must make up an entire union of vanishing blocks: a source term is bounded if and only if none of its bases in growing gaps is divisible by p, and every other term (including −H) tends to infinite valuation. Yet the sum of the bounded terms is **odd**. At positive gap lengths the even-base vectors vanish modulo two. The only remaining odd-base coefficient modulo two is at the all-ones vector, and it is in the bounded group. This contradicts vanishing. Neither the source terms nor their discrepancies were assumed nondegenerate as a whole; that is why partitioning, then using the parity of the bounded-valuation group, is needed.

In particular, for each fixed prime bound Y and fixed reversal budget b, all sufficiently large heights in that budget have a prime divisor above Y. On 7·3^t the same statement says that no fixed budget, **including three, four, or any other constant**, can furnish infinitely many terms. This rules out arbitrary moving gaps, not merely affine rays. It gives no effective last exponent or growth rate for the necessary budget. In the other direction the earlier 15,309=7·3^7 three-arc witness (0,12),(1,4),(2,8) has a zero-defect rightmost root: vertex 12 has sole exit to 0, and removing 12 makes 11 a sink. The [ordered Busch overlap](../session324_busch_inverse_and_gap_structure_20261113/BUSCH_INVERSE.md) therefore gives a strong H=7·3^t witness on 13+2(t−7)=2t−1 vertices using at most t−4 reversals for t≥7. Attach a right triangle at the current final vertex and move the root to the newly appended final vertex; this uses one additional reversed pair each time and preserves the zero-defect hypothesis. So the strong chain remains constructive, but the bounded-cost dream fails.

## 3. Which older information does this preserve?

- The [fixed two/three bank](../chanpak_two_three_archive_20261115/INDEPENDENT_CLOCKS.md) showed how ratios lock in minimal subsums. Session 302's dyadic chart or single transitive replacement could not follow the entire ray. The new parity argument lets one use an arbitrary finite set of fillers whose *ordinary* vertices move in arbitrarily many different ways across the fixed order (but still at fixed reversal budget). In particular it does not rely on any gap growing at a predetermined rate.
- The one-apex rail 13·2^a+11 in [Session 302](../session302_ternary_ray_birth_20261113/README.md) has arbitrarily high 3-divisibility because 2 generates the units modulo 3^j. Its exact prime factors nevertheless cannot stay in any fixed finite palette along infinitely many growing indices. Thus unbounded local v_3 is compatible with the theorem; it is keeping the 3-free part fixed that fails.
- Equal heights do not preserve reversal costs. The [Paley repair work](../chanpak_absolute_descent_20261122/PALEY-REPAIR.md) uses the seven-vertex H=189 core. It needs **seven** reversals in every order. A regular tournament on seven vertices sends six arcs backward across each of the cuts after positions 3 and 4. If only six reversals occurred in total, all would cross both cuts, so the first vertex would beat the next three and lose to the last three. Its out-neighborhood would then be transitive, since no backward arc is supported on those next three positions. But every Paley out-neighborhood is a cyclic triangle (translate the out-neighborhood {1,2,4} of 0). The order (4,1,5,2,6,3,0) attains seven. In contrast the [eight-vertex witness](../third_chain_three_arc_inverse_20261127/README.md) attains the same 189 at the global minimum of four. The small-order and small-reversal inverse problems genuinely choose different witnesses.
