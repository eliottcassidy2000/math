# The valuation clock is locally complete at every odd modulus

**Proved.** Prescribe that the zero-corridor lengths are repetitions
of \(4,2,5,3,7\), with arbitrary positive gap widths. For every odd
modulus \(m\) and every residue \(n\bmod m\), there is a physical
first-terrace tournament of this form whose ordinary Hamiltonian
count is \(n\bmod m\). This is not exact absolute-target coverage:
the spectra of every subfamily here have the established
\(O(X^{0.952})\) bound. In particular even infinitely many prime
targets are missed. This gives a local-to-global obstruction
for the entire indicated five-letter clock, not just for the
much freer dyadic lifting template.

The selection of these five corridor labels is an experiment suggested
by the historical [valuation chain](https://github.com/eliottcassidy2000/math/blob/095ef14e1e88351526125b56f2679ed5d7d40cbe/05-knowledge/results/session32_square_monster_20261023/VALUATION_CYCLE_AND_QUOTIENT.md).
That chain is \(13\to2\to5\to3\to7\to4\), with a graph quotient
identifying 4 and 13. Here 4 is used as the first letter; replacing it
by 13 is a different physical operation and forces an exact factor.
No equality of clocks in arithmetic or group actions is asserted.

## 1. Three physical full-clock variants

Use the [terrace transfer](https://github.com/eliottcassidy2000/math/blob/edf094e6bc983f5e8c8599aecdd2e0c00783773c/05-knowledge/results/chanpak_frame_decoder_pressure_20261114/RESETS_AND_DECODER.md).
Use column states, \(C_q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}e_q&f_q\\f_q&g_q\end{smallmatrix}\right)\),
and \(D_y=\mathrm{diag}(1,y)\).
A positive width \(r\) licenses \(y=2^r\), acting after its corridor:
\((a,b)^t\mapsto D_y C_q(a,b)^t\).
The seed is \((1,y_0)^t\) and the scalar output is \(a+b\).
The prescribed ordered capacities have the following determinants:
\[
 \begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
 q&4&2&5&3&7&13\\\hline
 \det C_q&-4&3&8&-1&-7&0.
 \end{array}
\]
The active product is \(-672=-32\cdot21\); 13 is excluded
from the active loop.

For any odd modulus, the residue 1 is realized by a positive
width, namely the order of 2, and 2 is realized by width one.
Take as physical modular variants \(A,B,C\) the full-clock words
with all five width-powers 1, with only the final width-power 2,
and with only the penultimate width-power 2, respectively. Write
\(D=D_2\). Thus
\[
 A=C_7C_3C_5C_2C_4,\qquad B=DA,\qquad
 C=C_7D C_3C_5C_2C_4.
\]
When \(C_7\) is invertible,
\(CA^{-1}=C_7 D C_7^{-1}\). The finite monoid of invertible
matrices generated by \(A,B,C\) is a group: inverse powers can
always be replaced by positive powers. This does not allow
matrix inverses over the positive integers; it only realizes
the stated residues by actual positive-width words.

## 2. Good primes and their powers

Let \(p\) be odd, \(p\notin\{3,7\}\), and first work over
\(\mathbb F_p\). The projective image of the generated group contains
both \(D\) and \(C_7 D C_7^{-1}\).
Outside \(p\in\{5,17,19,31\}\) their orders exceed 5
(use the prime divisors of \(2^j-1\), \(1\le j\le5\)), and their
fixed-line pairs are disjoint. Indeed the lines of the former are
the two coordinate axes; the lines of the latter are the two
column lines of \(C_7=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}57&68\\68&81\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
These columns have no zero coordinate away from the listed primes
and the already excluded primes. There can be no common fixed
point, even over the algebraic closure, and no common invariant
pair: an element of order \(>5\) preserving a pair fixes it
pointwise after squaring, so it must be its own fixed-line pair.

By Dickson's subgroup classification
([Faber, Theorems C and D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1112.1999)), this
projective image contains \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\):
the cyclic and dihedral cases preserve a pair, the Borel cases
fix a point, and the exceptional \(A_4,S_4,A_5\) groups have no
element of order \(>5\). At prime field there are no proper-field
\(\mathrm{PSL}\) or \(\mathrm{PGL}\) cases. The four exceptions
are discharged by the exact projective breadth-first computation
in the replay, with orders \(120,2448,6840,14880\), respectively.
Orders equal to half the full projective group imply the subgroup
is \(\mathrm{PSL}_2\): the projective commutator is
\(\mathrm{PSL}_2\), so every index-two subgroup contains it.

For clarity, \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) is perfect over
\(\mathbb Z/p^k\) for these primes. Its elementary upper and
lower unipotents generate it by elimination over the local
ring, and each is a commutator with
\(\mathrm{diag}(2,2^{-1})\), because 3 and 4 are units.
Consequently its projective image over the field is perfect,
and is also exactly the derived group of \(\mathrm{PGL}_2\)
(the determinant-square quotient is abelian).
The commutator of our generated group lies in
\(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\) and maps onto
\(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\). A lift of any nonidentity
projective involution has square \(-I\): its square is scalar;
square \(I\) would force either a scalar lift or determinant
\(-1\). The commutator therefore includes the kernel \(\{\pm I\}\)
and is the full \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\).

Here is the corresponding prime-power lift. A subgroup of
\(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z/p^k)\) surjecting mod \(p\) is the whole
group. Given a lift \(V\) of \(I+E_{12}\), expansion yields
\(V^p\equiv I+pE_{12}\pmod{p^2}\). The correction from writing
\(V=I+E_{12}+pX\) is, after division by \(p\), a sum of polynomials
of degree at most 2 in the \(p\) successive indices, so vanishes
mod \(p\) for \(p\ge5\). For \(j\ge1\), the \(p\)-th
power maps \(I+p^j X\pmod{p^{j+1}}\) to \(I+p^{j+1}X
\pmod{p^{j+2}}\).
The conjugates of \(E_{12}\) under the field group span
all traceless matrices: use the swap for \(E_{21}\) and a
lower unipotent to get a nonzero diagonal part. Therefore the
subgroup covers every successive trace-zero principal congruence
layer, proving the assertion by induction. Apply this to the
commutator of the generated group modulo \(p^k\), which already
surjects onto \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\).

For a good part \(g\) with several prime powers the same
commutator gives the full product of these \(\mathrm{SL}_2\)
groups, not just separate projection onto each. Each factor is
perfect and has only the nonabelian simple quotient
\(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\); its principal kernel is a
\(p\)-group, so any nonabelian simple quotient factors through
the reduced group, where the central sign dies. These simple
quotients have distinct orders at distinct primes. By Goursat's
lemma a proper subdirect product between one factor and a product
of the others would force a common nontrivial quotient, hence a
common simple quotient. A simple quotient of a direct product
comes from a factor, since the images of different factors
commute. Induction excludes all such correlations. We conclude
that the good group \(G\) contains the full product and that its
derived group is exactly that product.

## 3. Split a rank-one compressing loop off the bad part

Write the odd modulus as a good part \(g\) coprime to 21 and
a bad part \(b=3^\alpha7^\beta\); enlarge \(b\) if necessary
to make \(\alpha\ge2,\ \beta\ge1\), then reduce at the end.
The all-1 variant \(A\) has determinant divisible by each bad
prime, and its traces are 2 mod 3 and 3 mod 7. Its eigenvalues
0 and the nonzero trace are distinct modulo each bad prime,
so elementary root lifting gives two eigenvalues mod each
bad prime power, one nonunit and one unit. The difference of
the two roots is a unit, and the associated interpolation
projectors split the module into two free rank-one lines.
Choose a sufficiently large positive power \(E=A^L\) that kills
the nonunit eigenvalue and maps the unit eigenvalue to 1 on
both bad factors, and is the identity on the good part.
Choose \(L\) a large multiple of the relevant finite orders.
Concretely this means \(E\) is a bad rank-one idempotent and a
good identity, and remains a physical clock word. Its unique
idempotent reductions in this bad cyclic semigroup are
\[
 E\bmod3=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
 E\bmod7=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\4&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
 E\bmod63=\begin{pmatrix}12&51\\53&52\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The last is \(A^6\bmod63\), as multiplication verifies.

Compress the three variants to \(EAE, EBE, ECE\). On the bad
rank-one lines their scalars are respectively \(2,1,2\bmod3\)
and \(3,5,5\bmod7\); these are all units. Thus they generate
a finite group with identity \(E\) in the local corner
monoid, on which their bad projection is abelian. Their good
projection generates exactly \(G\). The commutator of the
compressed group therefore projects onto the full product of
good \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) groups and acts as \(E\) on bad.
Again every word in this finite group can be written with
positive blocks only. This gives an independent knob: choose
any desired determinant-one action on the good state after
first applying \(E\); the bad state \(E(1,y_0)^t\) remains
unchanged.

## 4. Terminal discharge on the bad part, with the seed width allowed

For terminal widths \(y_1,\ldots,y_5\), consider the
multiaffine polynomial
\[
 P(y_0,\ldots,y_5)
 =(1,1)D_{y_5}C_7D_{y_4}C_3D_{y_3}C_5D_{y_2}C_2
   D_{y_1}C_4E(1,y_0)^t.
\]
For each residue modulo 63 the accompanying **finite exact**
replay exhibits a choice in \(\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}^{6}\)
for which the polynomial has the desired residue, at least one
width derivative is a unit mod 3, and at least one width derivative
is a unit mod 7. The two derivative sites need not be the same.
It matters that the initial width \(y_0\) is also available for
this test.

All chosen powers can be varied by multiplication with
\(64^{t_j}\), keeping the same residues mod 63 and using only
positive width exponents. The generated subgroup on
\((\mathbb Z/3^\alpha)^\times\) is exactly \(1+9\mathbb Z/3^\alpha\)
because \(v_3(64-1)=2\); on \((\mathbb Z/7^\beta)^\times\) it is
\(1+7\mathbb Z/7^\beta\) because \(v_7(64-1)=1\).
For example the asserted orders follow inductively by the
binomial \(p\)-th-power valuation increase for a principal unit.
The orders are coprime powers of 3 and 7. Work at each prime
separately, fixing the other widths, and choose a new unit width
at the unit-derivative site by solving the *exact affine equation*
in that width. The solution is congruent to its prescribed starting
value mod 9 or mod 7, respectively. For each coordinate the CRT
on the two exponent orders synchronizes these choices, yielding
the desired bad residue with witnesses built from positive powers.

Finally allow this selected seed width on the good part as well.
The seed \((1,y_0)^t\) is primitive at every good prime power.
After specifying the terminal widths, the terminal full block
is invertible on good. Choose a primitive desired output vector
\((1,n-1)^t\) on good, take its inverse under this terminal block,
and use the compressed commutator to carry the actual seed to that
inverse. \(\mathrm{SL}_2\) is transitive on primitive vectors
over a local ring, by completing each to a determinant-one
basis. The bad residue is preserved by this independent middle
choice, and the terminal vector's scalar readout matches both
parts. This completes simultaneous odd-modulus coverage by the
specified five-clock alone.

## 5. This is not a Chan–Pak proof

The construction works in finite matrix groups where inverse
powers are returned as potentially very long positive words.
Neither the order of modular witnesses nor their real scalar
readout is bounded near the desired integer. Every actual word
still lies on the minimum-triangle terrace, where the convergent
inverse-power series at \(s=0.952\) forbids cofiniteness even
among primes (use Euler's divergence of the prime reciprocal
sum). The valuation clock therefore gives a rigorous case in which
a perfect modular local test, simultaneously at every odd modulus,
cannot establish physical exact-target return. Its transferable
ingredient is instead the separation of exact reset, modular group,
and size-controlled positive representative.
