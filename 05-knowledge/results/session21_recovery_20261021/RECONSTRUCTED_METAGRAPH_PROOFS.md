## 2. Compact closure of strong h=3 iff R5

### 2.1 Cycle-cut quotient and the index-two obstruction

Choose a Hamilton cycle of a strong tournament, with successor permutation R.
Let q be the index of Aut(T) intersect <R> in <R>, and write n=qm. The
intersection is generated by R^q and has odd order m. Indeed, a tournament
has no nonidentity involutory automorphism because a transposed pair would
have its arc reversed; an even-order group would contain such an involution.

Two cuts of the marked cycle represent the same Hamilton-word orbit exactly
when their relative cyclic shift is an automorphism. Thus the cuts occupy
exactly q orbits, all closed, and q<=h. A word is closed when its last vertex
beats its first; closure is invariant under automorphisms.

Suppose q=2, so n=2m with m odd. For m>=3 the step-two rotation has even and
odd shores A and B, each a regular induced tournament of size m. If every
vertex of A beats k vertices of B, the full scores on the two shores are

    s_A=(m-1)/2+k,       s_B=(m-1)/2+m-k.

These differ because m is odd. Consequently **every** automorphism preserves
the two shores, not merely the automorphisms of the chosen marked cycle.
Each induced regular tournament is strong: a proper source SCC would have
average full outdegree greater than the regular degree. Hence each has a
Hamilton cycle. The marked alternating cycle supplies cross arcs both ways.
Cutting and concatenating the two internal cycles at a cross arc in each
direction gives words of patterns A^mB^m and B^mA^m. Together with the two
alternating cuts, the four patterns are

    (AB)^m, (BA)^m, A^mB^m, B^mA^m.

They are distinct for m>1 and invariant under all automorphisms, so h>=4.
The omitted m=1 case is n=2, where no tournament is strong.

If h=3, then q is one of 1,2,3. The q=2 case is now impossible. If q=3 and
n>=7, the three closed cut orbits plus the open path from Benhocine–Wojda
give a fourth orbit. If q=3 and n<7, oddness of n/3 forces n=3; the strong
C3 has h=1. It remains to exclude q=1 odd circulants of order at least seven.

### 2.2 Half-supported prefix obstruction

Let Q=uP be two Hamilton words. Suppose u has even order and is supported
in a first r-position set S of P, with n>=2r. Then S is disjoint from its
positional reversal rho_P(S).

- The words cannot be in the same orbit, since u would be an even-order
  automorphism.
- They cannot both be blue: rho_Q rho_P equals
  u(rho_P u^(-1) rho_P), a product of disjoint permutations with the same
  even order, yet would be an automorphism.
- Their orbits cannot be converse/reversal mates. That would make u rho_P
  an anti-automorphism, whose square u(rho_P u rho_P) is an even-order
  automorphism by the same disjoint-support argument.

Therefore a clique of three words with this pairwise property has at most
one blue orbit and at least two black orbits that are distinct and not
mates. Their mates give two further black orbits, so Delta>=4.

In particular, suppose S carries a directed four-cycle, three vertices of
S beat v outside S, and T-S is strong. A Hamilton word of T-S can start at
v. Cutting the four-cycle after each of three exits and appending that
common word gives the required clique: relative permutations are nonidentity
powers of a four-cycle. For n>=8 the reflected supports are disjoint, so
Delta>=4.

### 2.3 The hand local exit/entry lemma

Every strong self-converse tournament of order at least six contains a
directed four-cycle with an external vertex beaten by at least three cycle
vertices. Moon supplies a strong five-set and then a strong four-set. Label
the latter by 0->1->2->3->0 with chords 2->0 and 3->1. If the fifth vertex4
has other than two outneighbors in this set, there is an exit or its dual
entry. For four of the six balanced choices there are explicit exits:

    4 beats {0,1}: cycle (1,2,3,4), terminal0;
    4 beats {0,2}: cycle (1,4,2,3), terminal0;
    4 beats {0,3}: cycle (1,2,4,3), terminal0;
    4 beats {1,2}: cycle (0,4,2,3), terminal1.

The other two choices give regular R5 and Q5=a->C3->b->a. Every four-set of
R5 is strong, so any additional vertex has a majority direction toward a
four-set, yielding an entry or exit.

In Q5, each {a,b,x,y} for two triangle vertices is strong. Unless an
external vertex v has exactly two neighbors in each direction on every
such set, there is already an entry/exit. In the balanced case, writing
t_x=[x->v] and p=#{a,b}->v gives p+t_x+t_y=2 for all triangle pairs.
All t_x are equal. Either C3->v and v->{a,b}, or {a,b}->v and v->C3.
For x->y in C3, the first case has cycle (a,x,y,v) with x,y,v beating b;
the second has cycle (a,v,x,b) with a,v,x beating y. Thus there is an exit.
Any entry previously found is converted to an exit by an ambient
anti-automorphism.

Here kappa denotes **vertex connectivity**, the minimum number of deleted
vertices leaving a nonstrong tournament; a singleton is considered strong.
It is not a prefix parameter. For n>=8, kappa>=5 would allow deletion of
the exit four-set while preserving strongness, forcing Delta>=4. Thus
self-converse Delta<=2 implies kappa<=4 at these orders.

## 4. Exact order18 recurrence despite unknown centers

Write p(z)=1-z-z^3 and u(z)=p(z^2). The common denominator in (1) is

    Q(z)=p(z)^2 u(z)^2,

and the numerator is

    N(z)=[z^5 u(z)^2-z^5 p(z)^2u(z)]/2
         +R6(z)p(z)^2u(z)+z^10p(z)^2(1+z+z^3).       (2)

The half term is integral because u-p^2 is even coefficientwise. From the
weaker bound deg R6<=4096, deg N<=4108, giving the homogeneous recurrence

    sum_{j=0}^{18} Q_j g_{n-j}=0

for every n>=4109. The multiple-of-16 exclusion improves deg R6 to4095,
deg N to4107 and the safe onset to **n>=4108**. This is not an earliest-onset
claim. Missing R6 coefficients still leave an unknown initial segment.

### No denominator cancellation, for any finite R6

The cubic p is squarefree and has nonzero roots. Hence u=p(z^2) is squarefree
as well. If p(z)=0, then z+z^3=1, so

    u(z)=1-z^2-z^6=2z^4 != 0.

Thus p and u have no common root. At a p-root the numerator (2) is
z^5u(z)^2/2, which is nonzero.

At a u-root, the numerator is z^10p(z)^2(1+z+z^3). Either p(z)=0 or
1+z+z^3=0 would give z+z^3=+1 or-1. Squaring again forces u(z)=2z^4!=0,
a contradiction. No u-root cancels either, independently of R6.

Consequently Q is the **exact reduced denominator**, with nine distinct
double poles. Any eventual homogeneous constant-coefficient recurrence
supplies a denominator after deleting finitely many initial terms; it must
retain every pole with its order. The minimal eventual recurrence order is
therefore **exactly18**, over Q or C. This says nothing about minimal
variable-coefficient recurrences.

## 5. Dominant asymptotic and density consequences

Let rho be the unique positive root of p, so 0<rho<1 and rho+rho^3=1.
Define

    C=rho^3/[2(1+3rho^2)^2],
    kappa=(4+6rho^2)/(1+3rho^2).

Then the prior proved corollary was

    g_n=C(n-kappa)rho^(-n)+O(n rho^(-n/2)),
    g_n ~ C n rho^(-n).                               (3)

The implied constant can depend on the fixed finite R6. This is not uniform
over arbitrary unbounded choices of its coefficients.

For w=1-z/rho, use rho+rho^3=1 to obtain

    p(z)=rho(1+3rho^2)w-3rho^3w^2+rho^3w^3.

The principal part of z^5/[2p(z)^2] is

    C w^(-2)+C[-5+6rho^2/(1+3rho^2)]w^(-1).

Since `[z^n](1-z/rho)^(-2)=(n+1)rho^(-n)`, this gives exactly the dominant
term in (3). The two other roots of p are conjugate and have modulus
rho^(-1/2)>1. The nearest other poles are therefore at +/-sqrt(rho), of
order at most two. Partial fractions give the error; a polynomial part
changes only finitely many coefficients. The R6 term has no pole at rho,
so it changes neither C nor the constant offset kappa.

In particular g_n/n! tends to zero. If m_n counts all converse-merged
tournament classes, then the elementary orbit bound is

    m_n >= 2^[n(n-1)/2]/(2n!).

Hence g_n/m_n <=2g_n n!/2^[n(n-1)/2] tends to zero: the numerator's logarithm
is O(n log n), while the denominator's is quadratic. These statements concern
only the specified Q-degree-three stratum, not all bounded-degree strata or
the whole degree distribution.
