# Additive prime-power quasi-periodicity for strong tournaments

**Status: PROVED; independently audited in Session25.**

For every odd prime p, every r>=1, and every integer n>=2r-1, put epsilon=(2/p) and P=phi(p^r)=p^(r-1)(p-1). Then

s(n+P) = epsilon s(n) (mod p^r),
D(n+P) = epsilon D(n) (mod p^r).

Here s(n) counts strongly connected labelled tournaments, with s(1)=1; c(n)=2^((n-1)/2) for odd n and0 for even n; and D(n)=s(n)-c(n).

This proves the additive higher-prime-power statement posed as HYP-9358(d′) in incoming commit3596c5d0. It is separate from the multiplicative dilation theorem. The threshold2r-1 is sufficient; no claim that it is minimal for each p,r is made. For example the independent checks at p=3,r=3 have no failures at n=3 or4, below the sufficient threshold5.

## 1. Coefficient ring and Fourier decomposition

Let R_0=Z/p^r Z. Work throughout in its unramified quadratic extension R. Since 2(p-1) divides p^2-1, this extension contains all L=2(p-1) roots of unity. (The base ring alone does not contain all L roots, even when epsilon=1.) Their differences are units whenever the roots are distinct, and L is a unit.

Let omega be the Teichmüller lift of2 modulo p. Then omega^(p-1)=1, omega^((p-1)/2)=epsilon, and2=omega(1+pu) for some u in R_0. Define

t_0(n)=omega^(n(n-1)/2), for n>=0.

It has period L and satisfies t_0(n+p-1)=epsilon t_0(n). Finite Fourier inversion over R gives

t_0(n)=sum_(alpha^L=1) c_alpha alpha^n,
c_alpha=L^(-1) sum_(k=0)^(L-1) t_0(k) alpha^(-k).

The shift identity forces c_alpha=0 unless alpha^(p-1)=epsilon: Fourier inversion is valid because L is a unit, and the two possible values of alpha^(p-1) are±1, whose difference2 is a unit. Also sum c_alpha=t_0(0)=1.

Choose theta with theta^(p-1)=epsilon. Every supported alpha is theta times a (p-1)st root of unity in R_0. Thus all supported exponents lie in the finite additive cyclic subgroup

G=theta R_0 of R,

which has order p^r. This closure under addition is essential; the set of Fourier unit nodes alone is not additively closed.

## 2. An invertible finite group-algebra element

In the group algebra R[G], let A=sum c_alpha [alpha]. Its augmentation is1. Modulo p, the group algebra is that of a cyclic p-group over a field. Its augmentation ideal is nilpotent: if z is a generator, (z-1)^(p^r)=z^(p^r)-1=0. Hence the reduction of A is a unit.

Lift its inverse to an element B of R[G]. Then AB=1+pC for some C, and1+pC is a unit by the finite geometric series, since p^r=0. Therefore A itself is a unit in R[G]. In particular A^(-1) is a finite R-linear combination of [beta] with beta still in G.

This step requires neither an injective exponential map nor a field of characteristic zero.

## 3. Hurwitz sequences and the degree/valuation filtration

Let H(R) be the ring of sequences f=(f_n)_(n>=0), with binomial convolution

(f*g)_n=sum_(k=0)^n binom(n,k) f_k g_(n-k).

Its unit e is delta_0. For beta in R write E_beta=(beta^n)_n, with0^0=1. Then E_alpha*E_beta=E_(alpha+beta), so [beta] maps to E_beta by a ring homomorphism. We use the same symbol A for the image of A; it is the sequence t_0, and its inverse is still an image of a group-algebra element.

Write delta_d for the sequence equal to1 at index d and0 elsewhere. Then

(delta_d*E_beta)_n=binom(n,d) beta^(n-d) for n>=d,
delta_d*delta_e=binom(d+e,d) delta_(d+e).

Define F to be the finite-sum R-submodule generated by terms

p^j delta_d*E_beta, with0<=j<r,0<=d<=2j,beta in G.

It is a subring: multiplying two terms adds j and d, with d+e<=2(j+k); if j+k>=r the term vanishes. Multiplication by a grade-zero group-algebra image preserves every filtration bound.

## 4. Integer-valued Newton expansion places T in F

The sequence T_n=2^(n(n-1)/2) satisfies

T_n=t_0(n) sum_(j=0)^(r-1) (pu)^j binom(n(n-1)/2,j) (mod p^r).

For each j, the rational polynomial f_j(n)=binom(n(n-1)/2,j) has degree at most2j and integer values at all nonnegative integers. Newton expansion therefore gives

f_j(n)=sum_(d=0)^(2j) a_(j,d) binom(n,d), with a_(j,d) in Z.

Explicitly a_(j,d) is the dth forward difference at0, so integrality is immediate. This avoids dividing by j! in R, which would be invalid when p divides j!.

Multiplying the Fourier expansion by binom(n,d) gives

binom(n,d) alpha^n = alpha^d (delta_d*E_alpha)_n.

Thus T=A+U belongs to F, where U is a sum of terms with j>=1. The finite geometric inverse

T^(-1)=sum_(k=0)^(r-1) (-A^(-1)U)^k * A^(-1)

also belongs to F: A^(-1) is grade zero; each factor U has positive p-adic grade; total derivative degree is at most twice total grade; and terms with grade at least r vanish.

The ordered strong-component decomposition gives (e-S)*T=e, where S_0=0 and S_n=s(n) for n>=1. The constant coefficient T_0=1 ensures its inverse in H(R) is unique. Hence S=e-T^(-1) belongs to F.

## 5. Every filtered term obeys the shift law in the stated tail

Consider p^j delta_d*E_beta with d<=2j and n>=2r-1. Then n>d, so all exponents below are nonnegative.

### Nonunit beta

Write beta=theta b, b in R_0. If p divides b, beta lies in pR. Its coefficient at n has valuation at least

j+n-d >= n-j >= (2r-1)-(r-1)=r.

Thus the term vanishes modulo p^r at both n and n+P. The case beta=0 is included, since n>d.

### Unit beta

If b is a unit, Euler's theorem in R_0 gives b^P=1. Since theta^(p-1)=epsilon and p^(r-1) is odd,

beta^P=epsilon.

It remains to show p^j[binom(n+P,d)-binom(n,d)]=0 modulo p^r. For j=0, d=0 and this is immediate. For j>=1 use Vandermonde:

binom(n+P,d)-binom(n,d)=sum_(l=1)^d binom(P,l) binom(n,d-l).

The identity l*binom(P,l)=P*binom(P-1,l-1) gives

v_p(binom(P,l)) >= r-1-v_p(l).

For l>P the binomial is zero, so the bound is still valid. Because l<=d<=2j and p is odd,

v_p(l)<=floor(log_p(2j))<=j-1,

where the last inequality follows from p^j>2j for every j>=1. Therefore each summand after multiplication by p^j is divisible by p^r.

Combining the unchanged binomial factor with beta^P=epsilon proves the shift law for every filtered term. Since S lies in F,

s(n+P)=epsilon s(n) modulo p^r for n>=2r-1.

The inclusion R_0 into its unramified extension is faithful, so this is the ordinary integer congruence modulo p^r, not merely a weaker statement after extension.

## 6. Circulant target and discrepancy

The shift P is even. For even n, both c-values vanish. For odd n,

c(n+P)/c(n)=2^(P/2)
 =omega^(P/2)(1+pu)^(P/2)=epsilon modulo p^r.

Here omega^(P/2)=epsilon, and P/2 is divisible by p^(r-1), so the principal-unit factor is1 modulo p^r. Thus c has the same law, and subtraction proves the asserted law for D. QED.

## 7. Scoped algebraic corollaries

### Arbitrary unit base and ordered-component inverse

Replace2 by any integer a coprime to p. Let T_a(n)=a^(n(n-1)/2), let epsilon=(a/p), and define S_a=e-T_a^(-1) in the Hurwitz ring. Then for every n>=2r-1,

S_a(n+phi(p^r))=epsilon S_a(n) modulo p^r.

The proof is unchanged: use the Teichmüller lift omega of a and write a=omega(1+pu); the same Fourier support, Newton degree bounds, and inversion argument apply. There is no requirement that a be positive for this algebraic statement. For the strong semicomplete-digraph interpretation a=3, require p!=3.

### Closure under explicitly permitted operations

For fixed p,r,a and its group G, the filtered algebra F above is closed under:
- addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication by R;
- Hurwitz convolution multiplication;
- the sequence shift Delta f(n)=f(n+1);
- inversion of an element whose constant coefficient f(0) is a unit.

For the shift, use the exact identity

Delta(delta_d*E_beta)=delta_(d-1)*E_beta+beta delta_d*E_beta,

with delta_(-1)=0. Both resulting degrees stay at most2j.

For inversion, choose the grade-zero group-algebra part A_0. Since all higher-grade terms vanish modulo p, its augmentation is f(0) modulo p and is a unit. The cyclic p-group-algebra argument makes A_0 invertible. The remainder has positive grade, so the same finite geometric expansion preserves F. This reasoning does not require uniqueness of a filtered representation.

Thus every finite expression obtained from T_a using these operations, and only inverses with unit constant coefficient, obeys the same sufficient tail law with multiplier (a/p). Scalar constants here mean scalar multiples of the Hurwitz unit delta_0. This is not closure under arbitrary added sequences, Hadamard products, or formal integration/logarithms. In particular the all-ones sequence must not be treated as the scalar unit.

This is a precise sufficient mechanism for the relevant closed family, not a classification of every sequence with quasi-periodicity or of its minimal threshold.

### Connected labelled graphs

Let b(n) count connected labelled simple graphs and T=T_2. Rooting the component containing a distinguished vertex gives the Hurwitz identity

Delta b=(Delta T)*T^(-1),

where the zeroth entry on the left is b(1). The right side lies in F. Therefore

b(n+phi(p^r))=(2/p)b(n) modulo p^r, for n>=2r.

This sufficient bound follows by shifting the filtered-sequence index once. It neither denies nor replaces sharper thresholds available from incoming proofs or the literature. At r=1 it gives the familiar exact tail beginning at n=2. No claim is made that ordered component decomposition is necessary for additive quasi-periodicity.

## 8. Full prime-power CRT criterion for the aggregate discrepancy

Here D(n) always means the aggregate s(n)-c(n), not a metagraph polynomial or any individual tournament statistic.

Let n>=4 be composite. For each odd prime power p^r exactly dividing n, define

t_(p,r)=2r-1 + ((n-(2r-1)) mod phi(p^r)),

where the remainder is in0..phi(p^r)-1. Then

n divides D(n) if and only if p^r divides D(t_(p,r)) for every odd p^r exactly dividing n.

There is no squarefree or cube-free restriction.

Proof: The chosen representative satisfies

2r-1 <= t_(p,r) <= phi(p^r)+2r-2 <= p^r <= n.

For the middle inequality use2r-2<=p^(r-1), valid for all odd p and r>=1. Thus n=t_(p,r)+k phi(p^r) with k>=0. Repeated additive quasi-periodicity gives

D(n)=epsilon^k D(t_(p,r)) modulo p^r.

The multiplier is a unit, so vanishing is equivalent. If2^a exactly divides n, the subgroup of order2^a in the rotations of the n labels acts freely on all tournaments: any nontrivial stabilizer contains the half-turn, which would swap endpoints of an edge. Its action on the strong subset is also free. Hence2^a divides s(n), and c(n)=0 for even n, so the2-primary condition is automatic. CRT completes the proof. For a pure power of2 the list of odd-prime conditions is empty, correctly giving a pass.

For n=p^r, the representative is p^(r-1), since p^(r-1)>=2r-1 and p^r-p^(r-1)=phi(p^r). This recovers the prime-square criterion and its higher-level predecessor relation. It does not say p^r divides D(p) for every passing p^r.

Finally, for any m>=1, the lower index mp^(r-1)>=2r-1, and mp^r differs from it by m phi(p^r). Iterating the additive theorem therefore gives the multiplicative law with multiplier epsilon^m. The separate contraction proof remains an independent combinatorial route to that consequence.
