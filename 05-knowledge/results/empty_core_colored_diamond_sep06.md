## 1. Statement

Let `Gamma` be a rank-two lattice, let `H` be any proper additive subgroup
of `Gamma`, and let `K=-K` be a convex set containing two independent lattice
vectors. Assume `K` contains finitely many primitive lattice directions;
a compact convex body suffices. A direction means a primitive vector modulo
sign. A primitive vector in `K` is **live** iff it is outside `H`; the complete
live direction set, not a selected subset, is counted. Let its size be `r>=2`.

**Theorem (adjacent-live determinant bound).** For any two angularly
consecutive signed live primitive vectors `u,v` in `K`, oriented so that
`det_Gamma(u,v)>0`,

```text
det_Gamma(u,v)<=r-1.                               (1)
```

More precisely, either the two vectors are consecutive in the complete
primitive lattice fan and their determinant is one, or exactly one invisible
primitive vector `w` separates them. In the latter case, writing
`k=det_Gamma(u,v)`, the segment `[u,-v]` contains the `k+1` pairwise
nonparallel live primitive vectors

```text
u-j*w, 0<=j<=k.                                    (2)
```

**Corollary (the diamond test).** If `u,v` are independent primitive live
vectors and `|det_Gamma(u,v)|>=3`, then

```text
conv{u,v,-u,-v}
```

contains at least four live primitive directions. In particular, a symmetric
convex body with exactly three live directions has every pair determinant
at most two. With exactly two live directions, their determinant is one.

**Corollary (three-direction circuits).** With exactly three live directions,
the absolute coefficients in their primitive integer circuit, sorted and
divided by their common gcd, are exactly one of

```text
(1,1,1), (1,1,2).                                  (3)
```

Both types occur with `Gamma=Z^2` and `H={(x,y):x=0 mod3}`. The assertions
are not specific to modulus three: any proper subgroup works, including
noncyclic finite-index sublattices.

## 2. The empty angular wedge has determinant one

First observe that primitive division preserves both `K` and liveness.
If `x=m*p` lies in `K`, with `p` primitive and `m>=1`, then `p` lies in
the segment `[0,x]` because `0 in K`. If `x` is outside `H`, then `p` is
also outside `H`, since `p in H` would imply `m*p in H`.

Take two angularly consecutive primitive vectors `p,q` among **all**
primitive vectors in `K`, with angle less than pi and positive determinant
`D=det_Gamma(p,q)`. Suppose `D>1`. The half-open fundamental parallelogram

```text
{alpha*p+beta*q:0<=alpha,beta<1}
```

contains a nonzero point `x` of `Gamma`, because the sublattice generated by
`p,q` has index `D`. Its coefficients satisfy `alpha,beta>0`: a zero
coefficient would put a lattice point strictly between zero and one of the
primitive vectors. If `alpha+beta<=1`, use `x`; otherwise use `p+q-x`.
The selected nonzero lattice point has both coefficients positive and their
sum at most one. It therefore lies in the triangle `conv{0,p,q} subset K`
and strictly between the two ray directions. Primitive division supplies
another primitive direction in the same wedge, a contradiction.

Hence consecutive primitive directions have determinant one. This argument
is elementary and uses no Pick theorem or external lattice-point input beyond
the determinant-index identity.

If the original convex set is not bounded, replace it for this argument by
the convex hull of its finitely many signed primitive lattice points. This
is a compact symmetric subset and preserves exactly the primitive direction
set. Central symmetry and two independent vectors ensure that each adjacent
fan angle is strictly less than pi.

## 3. An invisible separator gives a live arithmetic segment

Two consecutive vectors in the complete primitive fan cannot both lie in
`H`: their determinant is one, so they form a lattice basis, and a subgroup
containing them would equal `Gamma`. It follows that consecutive **live**
vectors `u,v` have either zero or one invisible primitive vector between
them. If there is none, their determinant is one.

Otherwise let the unique separator be `w in H`. The empty-wedge result gives

```text
det_Gamma(u,w)=det_Gamma(w,v)=1.
```

Writing `v` in the unimodular basis `(u,w)` therefore gives

```text
v=k*w-u, k=det_Gamma(u,v)>=1.                       (4)
```

The endpoint `-v=u-k*w` belongs to `K` by central symmetry. Convexity puts
every vector in (2) in `[u,-v] subset K`. Each such vector is primitive,
because its determinant with `w` is one up to sign. Each is outside `H`,
because it differs from the live vector `u` by an element of `H`.
Finally, for `i!=j`,

```text
det_Gamma(u-i*w,u-j*w)=i-j !=0,
```

so the vectors yield distinct unoriented directions. Hence `r>=k+1`,
which proves (1), including its direct-adjacency case since `r>=2`.

If `r<=3`, every pair of distinct unoriented live directions is consecutive
after choosing signs: there are at most three directions in a half-circle.
Thus (1) applies to every pair. Taking `K` to be the diamond proves its
corollary by contradiction.

## 4. Circuit coefficients and equality controls

For three primitive vectors `a,b,c`, the primitive circuit coefficients
are the three signed pair determinants divided by their common gcd. When
all absolute pair determinants lie in `{1,2}`, their normalized multiset
is one of `(1,1,1),(1,1,2),(1,2,2)` before using primitivity.
The last pattern is impossible: the pair having determinant one is a
unimodular basis, and the other two determinants being even say that the
third vector has both coordinates even in that basis. This contradicts
primitivity. This proves (3); it does not require a choice of circuit signs.

For explicit realizations under `f(x,y)=x mod3`, take the symmetric hulls
of the following primitive direction lists:

```text
(1,0),(1,1),(2,1):    (1,0)+(1,1)=(2,1),
(1,0),(1,1),(1,2):    (1,0)+(1,2)=2*(1,1).
```

Each hull has exactly the three listed live directions, giving circuit
types `(1,1,1)` and `(1,1,2)` respectively. The second hull is the determinant
two diamond, so the determinant bound for three directions is attained.
For sharpness at determinant three, the diamond on `(1,0),(1,3)` has
exactly the four live directions `(1,0),(1,1),(1,2),(1,3)`.

Central symmetry is load-bearing. The triangle with vertices
`(0,0),(1,0),(1,3)`, with liveness `x+y!=0 mod3`, has exactly three live
primitive directions `(1,0),(1,1),(1,3)`, but their circuit type is
`(1,2,3)` and one pair determinant is three. The missing implication is
`-v in K`, which is needed for the replacement segment.

Completeness is also load-bearing. Selecting three live directions from a
larger convex body does not license the bound: the constructive proof must
be allowed to add every primitive live vector it forces. The theorem bounds
primitive directions, not the number or weights of their integer multiples.
