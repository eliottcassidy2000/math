# How small can a chiral one-apex-invisible core be?

Here chirality means not isomorphic to the converse. The one-apex reversal criterion uses $D_m(v)$, the difference between the number of allowed-empty ordered $m$-slot path covers whose distinguished path starts versus ends at $v$. Invisibility under every homogeneous host with one individualized apex is equivalent to $D_m(v)=0$ for all $m,v$.

## The 21-vertex Cayley example is minimal among vertex-transitive tournaments

Every tournament automorphism group has odd order: by Cauchy's theorem, if its order were even, an involution would swap at least one pair of vertices and reverse their uniquely oriented connecting edge. A Cayley tournament of an abelian group is self-converse by inversion.

Let $T$ be vertex-transitive of odd order below 21. The only composite cases beyond order one are 9 and 15. At prime order $p$, Cauchy's theorem gives an automorphism that is a $p$-cycle, so $T$ is a Cayley tournament of a cyclic group.

* **Order 9.** Let $G$ be a transitive automorphism group and take a Sylow 3-subgroup $P$ containing a Sylow 3-subgroup of a point stabilizer. Orbit–stabilizer then makes $P$ transitive. Choose a central element $z$ of order three in $P$. It has no fixed points, because centrality and transitivity would make any fixed point force it to fix all points. Thus its orbits are three blocks of size three. Choose $g\in P$ cycling the three blocks. If $g$ has order nine, it is a regular cycle; otherwise it has order three, commutes with $z$, and $\langle g,z\rangle$ is regular abelian. Either case gives an anti-isomorphism.
* **Order 15.** The odd-order theorem of [Feit and Thompson](https://doi.org/10.2140/pjm.1963.13.775) makes $G$ solvable. Choose a nontrivial minimal normal subgroup $M$, necessarily elementary abelian of prime exponent $p$. Its orbits are normal blocks of equal size, not singletons since $G$ acts faithfully. Their size is therefore $p\in\{3,5\}$, and the number of blocks is the other prime $q$. On each block, $M$ acts by translations on a copy of $\mathbb F_p$, through a nonzero linear coordinate functional $\phi_i$. Two blocks are equivalent when their functionals are proportional. This is a $G$-invariant equivalence (the coordinate kernels agree), so transitivity and primality of $q$ make it universal or discrete.

  If discrete, projection to any two coordinates has rank two. Their vertices can be independently translated, so all inter-block arcs are uniform. The tournament is a lexicographic product of its prime-order transitive quotient and its common prime-order transitive block, where “transitive” here refers to the automorphism action. Both factors are cyclic Cayley tournaments, and the product is self-converse by reversing each factor. If the equivalence is universal, all coordinates are proportional, so faithfulness makes $M$ cyclic of order $p$. There is a $q$-power-order element $g$ inducing a $q$-cycle on blocks (lift such a cycle in the quotient and discard the prime-to-$q$ part of the lift). Since $q$ does not divide $p-1$, $g$ centralizes $M$. Then $g^q$ fixes the blocks and commutes there with a regular $p$-cycle, so its order divides $p$. As it has $q$-power order it is trivial. Thus $M$ and $g$ generate a regular abelian group of order 15, and again inversion reverses the tournament.

The explicit 21-vertex Cayley example therefore gives the first possible order by this vertex-transitive route. This does **not** rule out smaller non-vertex-transitive balanced cores.

## Independent exact order-nine exclusion without transitivity

A balanced tournament is regular: in the binomial-basis expansion of $D_m(v)$, the coefficient of $\binom{m-1}{n-2}$ uses $n-1$ nonempty slots. The start-minus-end difference is zero unless the distinguished path occupies two vertices and every other nonempty slot is a singleton. The coefficient is $(n-2)!\,(2d^+(v)-(n-1))$. Hence even orders have no balanced tournaments, and the order-nine search only needs regular tournaments.

The [finite replay](regular-balanced-scan.cpp) normalizes the neighbors beaten by vertex 0 to labels 1–4, enumerates every remaining orientation subject to regular outdegree four, and evaluates distinguished start and end Hamilton counts by subset DP. Of 46,144 normalized tournaments, precisely 1,216 have $D_1(v)=0$ at every vertex. Every one of those is self-converse by direct relabeling backtracking. Thus there is no chiral balanced order-nine core, even without any automorphism hypothesis. The labeled total before normalizing is $\binom84\cdot46144=3230080$.

Orders 11, 13, 15, 17 and 19 in the non-vertex-transitive case are not settled here.

## Strong regular collision families with no anchor vertices

The 21-vertex construction also gives regular hosts, which the ordinal list with a closing triangle does not. Let $k=2m+1\ge3$ and let $C_k$ be the carousel tournament on cyclically ordered vertices, with $i\to i+d$ for $1\le d\le m$. Substitute one copy of the 21-vertex chiral core or its converse at every quotient vertex. Every resulting tournament is strong and regular, with outdegree $21m+10=(21k-1)/2$. In any additional outside host whose vertices except a single apex are uniform toward each replacement block (in particular any one-apex homogeneous context for the entire new core), the two choices at any one block give the same fixed-label path count. Replace blocks one at a time to show all the resulting cores have the same full one-apex response.

The replacements are recoverable up to rotation. First, the carousel is prime (has no nontrivial proper homogeneous module): if a module contains $i$ and $i+d$ with $1\le d\le m$, every vertex strictly between them on that cyclic interval distinguishes them, so it belongs to the module. Hence there is an adjacent pair $i,i+1$. The vertex $i+m+1$ distinguishes that pair and belongs to the module as well. Applying the same interval argument to $(i+1,i+m+1)$ and $(i+m+1,i)$ fills both remaining intervals. Second, projection of any module of a blow-up to its intersected quotient blocks is a module of the quotient, since a block outside the projection is uniform to all the module's vertices. If the projection is all quotient blocks but one block is only partially included, a missing vertex in that block sees selected vertices in both directions, since the quotient has both in- and out-neighbors. Therefore each module of cardinality 21 is exactly one replacement block. An isomorphism must map the blocks to blocks, and its action on the carousel is a rotation: the unique source in the transitive out-neighborhood of $i$ is $i+1$, so every automorphism preserves the successor operation. Conversely, a rotation preserving the orientation word induces an isomorphism.

The number of distinct cores is thus exactly the binary necklace count
$$\frac1k\sum_{d\mid k}\varphi(d)2^{k/d}\ge\frac{2^k}{k}.$$
All have order $21k$. This is a regular, fixed-label one-apex collision family, not merely a family with the same unmarked Hamilton count. The quotient and modular argument applies to any equal-order regular strong nonisomorphic pair satisfying the same fixed-label one-apex equivalence.
