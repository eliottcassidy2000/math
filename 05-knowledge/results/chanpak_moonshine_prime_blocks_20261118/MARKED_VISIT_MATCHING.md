# Exact visit cap at a marked tournament vertex

Let x be a marked vertex of a tournament, O its outneighbors and I its inneighbors. Form the bipartite graph with sides O and I and an edge uv exactly when the tournament contains u→v with u in O and v in I. Let ν be its maximum matching size.

**Theorem.** After substituting a transitive block of positive width m for x, the maximum number of runs of that block in a Hamiltonian path is exactly min(m,1+ν). In particular the two-visit cutoff is not a degree restriction: it holds precisely when the bipartite graph is empty or is a star, including a one-edge star.

**Proof.** Compress the block to copies of x separated by nonempty words of ordinary vertices. Each interval between consecutive copies starts in O and ends in I. It therefore contains an edge O→I. Those intervals are vertex-disjoint, so the edges selected from them constitute a matching. This gives the upper bound. Conversely, from any matching of size k≤m−1 form the word x,u1,v1,x,…,uk,vk,x. It already respects the required arcs. Insert each unlisted exterior vertex into the word by the usual tournament path insertion: prepend or append if possible, and otherwise find the first transition from an existing vertex that beats the new vertex to one that loses to it. This preserves all copies of x with nonempty runs between them. Give each x run at least one clone and fill each run in ascending order. This attains the bound. Finally a nonempty bipartite graph of matching number one is a star: all edge pairs meet, and two edges meeting at one endpoint force every further edge to use that endpoint. □

For the family 38·2^j+1 this makes the two-visit interface test exact, without assuming the mark itself has degree one in either direction. In this interface H(2)−H(1)=2c₂, and H(m)=H(1)+c₂(2^m−2), so H(2)=2H(1)−1 is exactly the intercept-one test. A finite search of degree-one marks alone cannot settle this condition. The star case with center u also exposes a second distinguished vertex: deleting u separates the remaining exterior and the mark into the ordinal sequence I → x → (O minus u) if u is in O, or (I minus u) → x → O if u is in I. Thus an arbitrary two-visit host can be generated as an apex over that ordinal sequence; the internal tournaments in the outside blocks need not be transitive.

## A larger divisor clock

The original [LRC-inspired clock](LRC14_DEFECT_CLOCK.md) has period 27,720 and 4,716 surviving exponent residues. Instead of adding further factors with periods dividing that fixed modulus, add q=101 and q=131. Their respective periods and divisibility residues are (100,53) and (130,29), verified by modular exponentiation. The joint period is 1,801,800. Exactly 285,318 residues survive, of which 84,216 are 1 modulo 3 (the potentially sevenfold axis). The surviving fraction drops from 17.013% to 15.835%. These are filters for a *least* counterexample, not general tournament witnesses. Nothing here changes the earlier lower bound j≥365, or proves that the target at exponent 365 is missing.

Replay the clock by lifting each surviving residue of the old period into the new period and discarding residues 53 mod 100 and 29 mod 130. The run bound above is a structural proof, not a finite search.
