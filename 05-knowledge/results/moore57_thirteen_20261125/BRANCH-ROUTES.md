# The full square-sum join around every Moore root

**PROVED conditional implication.** Let \(G\) be any degree-57 Moore graph of diameter two. Take **any** vertex \(o\), and assign labels \(L_1,\ldots,L_{25},R_1,\ldots,R_{32}\) to its 57 neighbors in any order. There is a simultaneous routing of all edges of
\[
                        Q_{25}\vee Q_{32}
\]
as paths of exactly three edges between these branch vertices. All 1,756 internal vertices are distinct and lie at distance two from \(o\). Here \(Q_n\) joins unequal labels whose sum is square and the join inserts all 800 cross-part edges. Thus 32 and 46 internal square-sum adjacencies and all 800 crossing adjacencies coexist in one subdivision, on 1,813 vertices. Adding \(o\) gives the same subdivision with a common vertex adjacent to every branch vertex. This is a subgraph statement; unused ambient edges may join its vertices.

## The inputs supplied by the Moore equations

For a branch vertex \(u\), its 56 neighbors other than \(o\) form its set of slots. These 57 sets are disjoint by the uniqueness of two-step paths. Between any two different sets the graph induces a perfect matching. Indeed a slot of \(u\) has a unique common neighbor with \(v\), which has to lie in the slots of \(v\); uniqueness in the reverse direction gives the bijection. To route \(uv\), use a slot of \(u\) and its image in the matching to \(v\). No branch may use the same slot twice. The proof below works for **arbitrary** pairwise matchings, including systems that cannot extend to a Moore graph. [Inherited branch-matching coordinates](../moore57_gewirtz_exact_cover_20261124/moore-paired-branches.md)

For the cross-part edges alone, each request conflicts with at most \(31+24=55\) earlier requests and there are 56 choices. Retaining every internal square-sum neighbor costs enough choices to defeat that simple bound. The remedy uses the unique leaf 18 of \(Q_{25}\), which is adjacent to 7, and the degree-two label 8, adjacent to 1 and 17.

1. Prescribe a slot at \(L_8\) for \(L_8L_1\). In the matching to \(R_1\) that slot corresponds to a particular slot at \(R_1\). Prescribe this latter slot for \(L_2R_1\), using its unique inverse at \(L_2\). Make the analogous two prescriptions for \(L_{18}L_7\) and \(L_3R_2\). The four prescribed routes use different slots at every common endpoint. Finish all internal edges of \(Q_{25}\) greedily. Its maximum degree is four, so even with the two early crossing routes there are many available choices.
2. Process crossing routes one left row at a time: first rows 2 and 3, then the remaining rows in decreasing internal degree, except that rows 8 and 18 are last in that order. On row \(i\) in this sequence each right label supplies a list of admissible slots at the left vertex. For the first two rows there are 31 outstanding requests and at least 49 choices per request. From index \(i=3\) through 23, the internal degree \(d_i\) obeys \(i+d_i\le25\) (from the square edges, whose degrees are at most 4, with one vertex of degree 1, eleven of degree 2, eleven of degree 3 and two of degree 4). The list sizes are at least \(56-d_i-(i-1)\ge32\). Hall's theorem gives distinct slots for all requests.
3. The last two rows have internal degrees two and one, respectively. Every list has at least 31 slots. For row 8 at \(R_1\) and row 18 at \(R_2\) the two earlier exclusions in step 1 now coincide, so one list on each row has at least 32 slots. Hall's condition follows: a proper subset has at most 31 requests and the full family contains the larger list. This is why independently counting all excluded slots would miss the routing.
4. Every right vertex has now used exactly 25 slots. Add the internal edges of \(Q_{32}\), doing all edges with two degree-four endpoints first. The degree-four labels are \(1,3,4,5,6,7\); their induced edges are \(1\! -\!3,3\!-\!6,4\!-\!5\). During this short first stage at most two prior internal incidences can conflict. On every subsequent edge the sum of endpoint degrees is at most seven. At most five earlier internal incidences and fifty crossing incidences forbid a choice. A slot pair remains.

The [executable decoder](branch_routes.py) accepts both directions of each matching and returns the two selected slots for every request. Running `python3 -B branch_routes.py` checks the edge counts and the placement on identity and shuffled matchings. The universal assertion follows from Hall's argument.

## Ordinary odd cycles generated by this chart

The square-sum path at 25 and cycle at 32 each supply a path segment of any requested order up to their respective sizes. For every \(3\le j\le57\), take two nonempty such segments with orders summing to \(j\), then join their ends crosswise. This is a simple cycle of length \(j\) in the join, including when one segment has just one vertex. The routed subgraph thus contains simple cycles of lengths \(3j\) throughout that interval. Similarly any segment of \(j\) edges of a spanning path of the join gives a cycle of length \(3j+2\) through \(o\), for \(1\le j\le56\). These specify two explicit ranges of native cycle lengths at degree 57. They concern the vertices and edges present in the chart; chords elsewhere in the full graph are allowed.

For the original path and cycle thresholds see [Gerbicz's own recorded result](https://oeis.org/A090461/internal). For a different use of the same numbers, a hypothetical **equitable equal cut of all 3,250 vertices** has 25 crossing and 32 internal neighbors per vertex. Our choice of 25 and 32 labels in a single neighborhood requires no such cut. [Prior crossing-block analysis](../moore_fixed_order_spectra_20261124/SQUARE-REFLECTION-CROSSING.md)

## A prescribed native cycle of every length from 5 to 456

**PROVED conditional implication.** Every vertex in a degree-57 Moore graph lies on simple cycles of every integer length from 5 through 456. More precisely, one can prescribe the cyclic order of up to 57 branch vertices and vary the number of slots traversed between successive branch vertices independently within the following bounds. These cycles may have chords; the 13-cycle contact analysis uses an induced cycle instead.

Take any \(3\le j\le57\) neighbors of a root in any cyclic order \(u_0,\ldots,u_{j-1}\), and set \(k=\min(7,j)\). Between \(u_i,u_{i+1}\) choose any \(2\le r_i\le k\) slot sets among the cyclic interval \(i,\ldots,i+k-1\), including both endpoint sets. Visit the chosen sets in any order starting at \(i\) and ending at \(i+1\). The matchings compose to give 56 possible routes; each visits the designated slot sets once, and for each particular visited set the starting slot maps bijectively onto it. Each set belongs to at most \(k\) route intervals. At the time of choosing a route, at most \(k-1\) slots in each of its sets could have been used earlier. Hence at most \(r_i(k-1)\le42<56\) starting slots are forbidden. The chosen routes are internally disjoint, and yield a cycle through all prescribed branch vertices, in the prescribed order, of length \(j+\sum_i r_i\).

For fixed \(j\) these lengths fill the integer interval \([3j,j(1+\min(7,j))]\). The intervals overlap from 9 through 456. For lengths 5 through 60, fix a root and two of its neighbors and close a path between them via the root. The path can use any number from 2 through 57 of the distinct slot sets, since no earlier route has consumed any slot. For the longer lengths any specified vertex can be included by choosing one of its neighbors as root. This supplies, in particular, every possible odd **cycle length** from 23 to 455 in a degree-57 candidate while preserving actual vertex placement. Whether a positive odd integer is a **Hamilton-path count of a tournament** requires an additional construction. No step of this routing transfers a missing integer count between those two problems.
