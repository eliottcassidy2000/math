# An induced Petersen graph with one edge missing detects a generating triple

**CITED.** Two-neighbor percolation adjoins every vertex adjacent to at least two current vertices. In a diameter-two Moore graph this is closure under the unique midpoint of a nonadjacent pair. In the hypothetical degree-57 graph its minimum seed size is 3 or 4. An independent triple with no common neighbor either generates a Petersen or generates the whole graph. The minimum is 4 precisely when the induced Petersen count reaches its upper bound of 266,266,000. The corresponding nonstar triples in Hoffman–Singleton all generate Petersens. [Ibrahim et al., Propositions 4.15–4.16 and Theorem 4.18](https://ajc.maths.uq.edu.au/pdf/93/ajc_v93_p060.pdf)

**PROVED local test.** For any one such nonstar triple, three rounds of taking specified midpoints reduce its fate to the presence of **one undirected adjacency** on ten vertices, as follows. The three pair midpoints first form an induced hexagon with the original vertices in alternate positions. They are distinct, and a fourth edge among these six would give a triangle, a square, or a common neighbor of all three seeds. Conversely either alternating triple of any induced hexagon is an independent triple without a common neighbor, since each of its pairs already has a different midpoint.

Take the unique midpoint of each of the three opposite pairs of the hexagon. Call these new vertices \(y_1,y_2,y_3\). A vertex outside an induced hexagon can touch at most two of its vertices: its contacts must have cyclic distance at least three. Thus the three midpoints are distinct and have precisely the specified contacts. Two of them cannot be adjacent because their supports contain two adjacent hexagon vertices, making a square. Finally take the midpoint \(z\) of \(y_1,y_2\). It is new and has no contact with the hexagon. For contacts already touching \(y_1\) or \(y_2\) this would give a triangle. Each of the other two hexagon sites has a hexagon neighbor touching \(y_1\), so contact there would give a square. Therefore the ten vertices form either a Petersen or a Petersen with the edge \(zy_3\) missing. Filling this edge gives the usual cubic Petersen on an induced hexagon, its three opposite-pair midpoints and their common neighbor. A ten-vertex Petersen has diameter two, so its vertices are closed in the ambient graph.

If the edge is absent, the ten-vertex set cannot be contained in another Petersen, since it already has the order of that graph. The cited dichotomy forces the original triple to generate all 3250 vertices. Conversely an induced Petersen with one edge missing supplies a generating triple: designate either degree-two vertex as \(z\), the other as \(y_3\), and take the two neighbors of \(z\) as \(y_1,y_2\). The remaining six form the required hexagon, as follows directly from the Petersen model (or its transitivity on ordered edges). Thus the minimum seed size is 3 precisely when this ten-vertex induced pattern occurs.

**PROVED counting identity.** Let \(p\) count unlabelled induced Petersen vertex sets in a degree-57 input and \(e\) count unlabelled induced Petersen-minus-one-edge vertex sets. Then
\[
                         e=15(266266000-p).
\]
There are 5,325,320,000 independent nonstar triples. This is obtained by subtracting \(v\binom d3\) from the independent triples in the triangle-free graph; the latter number is \(\binom v3-(vd/2)(v-2)+v\binom d2\), with \(v=3250,d=57\). The nonstar triples occur twice per induced hexagon. A successful hexagon lies inside its unique closed Petersen, and each Petersen has ten hexagons. Hence the failing hexagons number \(10(266266000-p)\). Each failure gives three different incomplete Petersen sets, one per choice of the pair for the last midpoint. Those last midpoints are distinct, since equality would make one common neighbor of all three. Conversely every incomplete Petersen has exactly two such presentations, according to its choice of degree-two vertex \(z\); once this endpoint is chosen the other three exterior roles and the hexagon set are determined. Double counting proves the identity.

One incomplete Petersen would force at least fourteen others. Counting these patterns tests the exact deficit in Petersen saturation and the existence of a three-site generator without growing the whole closure of each triple. The actual deficit and the existence of the ambient graph remain open. The [four-site extension calculation](FOUR-SEEDS.md) bounds the number of possible larger proper closures through a fixed Petersen.
