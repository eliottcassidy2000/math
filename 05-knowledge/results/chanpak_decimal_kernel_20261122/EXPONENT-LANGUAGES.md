# Which Euclid exponents can an exact output grammar recognize?

**PROVED.** Let a context-free grammar generate words on the four synchronous binary columns \((0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\). Regard each word as two most-significant-bit-first nonnegative integer readings. Then the set of exponents \(p\ge2\) for which the grammar generates the canonical equal-width pair \((E_p-1,E_p+1)\) is **effectively ultimately periodic**. From the grammar one can decide whether 61 occurs, find the first missed exponent, and decide whether all sufficiently large exponents occur. There is a physical one-arc conclusion when each accepted word carries a construction of the two readings on opposite orientations of one edge. The statement also works for any grammar whose effective intersection with the regular restriction used below has an effectively semilinear letter-count set.

The arithmetic is unusually economical. The binary words for the two readings are \(1^{p-1}0\,1^{p-1}\) and \(1^p0^{p-2}1\). Write \(A=(1,1), B=(0,1), C=(1,0)\). Their paired word is exactly
\[
                         A^{p-1} B C^{p-2} A .
\]
Intersect the output grammar with the regular pattern \(A^+ B C^* A\). Now the letter counts determine the word uniquely. The target counts on \((A,B,C)\) are \((p,1,p-2)\). Compute the Parikh image of the intersection, constrain it to this line, and project onto the first coordinate. Effective semilinearity, closure under linear equality and projection, and eventual periodicity in one integer variable give the assertion. Any accepted exponent comes with an ordinary grammar-membership computation for recovering a derivation. [Parikh, *On Context-Free Languages*](https://doi.org/10.1145/321356.321364) · [Esparza, Ganty, Kiefer and Luttenberger, constructive automaton proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.3825)

In particular an infinite accepted set contains infinitely many **composite exponents**: from a positive term \(r>1\) in an infinite arithmetic progression of period \(d\), the terms \(r(1+jd)\) are composite for all \(j>0\). An exact grammar of this kind cannot select infinitely many centers exclusively at Mersenne-prime exponents. This holds regardless of how many even perfect numbers exist.

For a deterministic automaton with \(s\) states the exponent set is periodic from \(p=s+2\), with a period dividing \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,s)\). Each of the two repeated letters is a function on the finite state set. After at most \(s\) steps every orbit lies on a cycle. At each individually accepted \(p\ge s+2\), combining the two particular cycle lengths gives a whole forward progression of successes with period at most \(s^2\). The double-track requirement matters. Two grammars independently accepting the two scalar integers could produce unrelated tournaments.

The concurrent finite-carry construction compiles a common-base affine grammar to a finite machine. When the binary digits describe an actual shared physical edit, this gives an exact exponent selector. In contrast the Farey controller reads the *address* at fixed modular precision; its input language is different. Our [absolute chart verifier](ABSOLUTE-WORDS.md) uses growing integer tapes. For unrestricted Turing output machines even deciding whether the single paired word at exponent 61 is ever printed is undecidable: simulate a given computation and print that word exactly when it halts. This last assertion concerns arbitrary machines, with no claim of undecidability for tournaments.
