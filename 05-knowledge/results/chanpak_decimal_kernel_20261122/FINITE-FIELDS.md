# Which finite-field readings of a module can actually occur?

**PROVED.** For each odd prime \(p\), every ordinary Hamilton count obtained by substituting a nonempty tournament \(A\) into a hole in a tournament is determined modulo \(p\) by its slot polynomial as a function on \(\mathbb F_p\). Conversely these contextual readings determine the function. At fixed vertex parity only \((p-1)/2\) field coordinates are needed. For the primes **3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19 simultaneously**, every vector of values in those coordinates, including arbitrary zeros, is realized by a single actual tournament of the specified parity. The first assertion specializes the [integral arithmetic interface](../chanpak_symmetry_interfaces_20261115/OBSERVABLES.md). The joint realizability assertion uses explicit small tournament generators.

## Compression and the converse

Writing \(B_r(A)\) for ordered covers by \(r\) nonempty paths, the count of any host \(S[A]\) equals \(\sum_r c_r(S)B_r(A)\). Compress each maximal consecutive visit to the module into one symbol; the resulting exterior word accepts all ordered covers of the module of the appropriate size. Permuting the slots shows \(r!\mid B_r\), while inclusion–exclusion expresses \(B_r\) as the \(r\)-th forward difference at zero of the ordered optional-slot polynomial \(Q_A(j)\). Thus only values on the field can contribute modulo \(p\). Conversely the successive hosts that wrap the module in a cyclic triangle with two singleton vertices give triangular readings of \(Q_A(1),\dots,Q_A(p-1)\), with diagonal entries \(0!,\dots,(p-2)!\), all units. The zero-arity value of a nonempty tournament is zero. For two modules of the same parity, the first \((p-1)/2\) of these readings suffice, as \(Q_A(-j)=(-1)^{|A|}Q_A(j)\). [Triangular physical readings](../chanpak_arc_fiber_grammar_20261122/SQUARE-EARS.md)

Grouping positive powers of \(j\) by their exponent modulo \(p-1\) expresses every host count as a linear combination modulo \(p\) of the same host with transitive modules of orders \(1,\ldots,p-1\). At \(p=3\) a single term suffices: replace \(A\) by a singleton in odd order or a transitive pair in even order, and multiply the resulting scalar by \(H(A)\). Hence **every module of height divisible by three forces every containing tournament to have height divisible by three**. At \(p=5\), universal divisibility is equivalent to both \(H(A)\) and \(Q_A(2)\) being divisible by five. In particular an arc leading from \(E_{61}-1\) to \(E_{61}+1\) must meet each such module in its lower host. The two endpoints can meet at most two pairwise disjoint modules of this kind. [Strong lower families and their boundary arcs](../chanpak_e61_fibonacci_target_20261122/NEW-LOWER.md)

On any tree describing recursive substitutions, the zeros of the local quotient weights modulo three must lie on the tree path joining the two endpoints of a successful flip. The strict ancestor condition can exclude an edit even when it repairs the only three-divisible proper module. [Tree-path criterion and exponent-61 example](MODULE-PATH.md)

## Joint realization by a finite alphabet

First choose even parity. At each prime take representatives \(1,\dots,(p-1)/2\) of the sign pairs in the nonzero field. Values at these coordinates determine any even function vanishing at zero. An ordinal sum multiplies the coordinate vectors entrywise. Use all catalogued tournaments through order eight as seeds, padding each odd-order seed by one singleton to make it even.

On the nonzero coordinates choose a primitive root of each prime field and take discrete logarithms. The question whether the all-unit seed vectors generate the whole product of cyclic groups of orders \(p-1\) is elementary integer linear algebra. For each prime \(q\) dividing some \(p-1\), reduce modulo \(q\), keeping only the coordinates whose group order is divisible by \(q\). Full rank for each \(q\) is equivalent to generation: any nontrivial quotient of the desired finite abelian group would have a cyclic quotient of prime order. The semigroup of actual products realizes every such group element, since inverses are positive powers. Its identity is also realized by a positive power of any unit seed.

For the seven primes above, the replay gives full ranks **34, 18, 5** at \(q=2,3,5\), respectively. For each one of the 34 coordinates it also finds a seed with zero precisely there, with all other coordinates units. Multiplying the selected zero-producing seeds gives any desired support. A unit vector corrects every surviving value. One final ordinal singleton transfers the result to odd parity, since multiplication by \(j\) is invertible on all chosen coordinates. This proves surjectivity at both parities. The same method alone gives full realizability at 31 and 41 individually. In particular finite-field information alone places no further restrictions on these vectors at unrestricted order. The final tournament can be large and need not be strong.

## A phase match that detects a physical obstruction at 19

Start with the six-vertex lower tournament with rows \((2,32,11,19,7,29)\) and flip the arc between vertices 1 and 2. Replace vertex 0 by an arbitrary nonempty module \(A\). Writing \(H=H(A),\ Q=Q_A(2)\), the two full counts are
\[
          L=3H+12Q,\qquad U=-H+15Q .
\]
The one-run coefficients of the two exterior words are 27 and 29. The two-run coefficients are 12 and 15, respectively, and both higher coefficients vanish. Thus the desired residues \((E_p-1,E_p+1)\) impose \(19H=E_p-9\) and \(57Q=4E_p+2\) wherever the relevant factors are invertible, as follows by taking the two indicated linear combinations. For any odd exponent at least three, \(E_p\equiv1\pmod3\), so that \(H\equiv1\pmod3\) suffices for both counts there. At 5, 7, 11, 13 and 17 solve the two linear equations for \(H,Q\); 1 and 2 are distinct sign-pair coordinates at each of these primes. Joint realizability above therefore gives a single physical marked pair matching the desired endpoints **simultaneously modulo \(3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\)** for every such exponent. Nevertheless at 19 the required equation demands \(E_p\equiv9\pmod{19}\), whereas \(E_{61}\equiv15\pmod{19}\).

For a short explicit exponent-61 control at moduli 3, 5, 7 and 13, take \(A\) to be the ordinal sum of one singleton and the two order-eight tournaments with rows
\[
 (6,12,24,65,139,31,55,111),\qquad (6,136,42,49,135,83,31,109).
\]
Then \(H=81{,}253,\ Q=44{,}342{,}528\), giving the counts 532,354,095 and 665,056,667 on 22 vertices. Their residues are 0 and 2 modulo 1,365. The absolute gap in this one-port example is \(3Q-4H\). Splitting full paths proves \(Q\ge(|A|+1)H\), so at order at least two the gap already exceeds two. This locates both the first failing prime in the displayed example of lattice constraints (19 for the six-prime existence result) and the direct positive obstruction to an exact lift.

In fact **all** unit lifts by substituting an arbitrary single module into any inactive vertex of a primitive \(27\to29\) pair already come from transitive modules. Using the [four primitive seeds and their compressed words](../chanpak_e61_fibonacci_target_20261122/SEED-LIFTS.md), the complete list of coefficient triples of the difference on \((B_1,B_2,B_3)\) is
\[
 (2,0,0),\ (2,1,0),\ (2,3,0),\ (2,6,0),\ (2,-3,0),\ (2,-5,-2).
\]
All higher coefficients vanish. A positive second coefficient excludes any nonsingleton. A zero second coefficient forces \(H=1\), which characterizes transitive tournaments. In the last two cases, for \(n\ge3\) we have \(B_2\ge(n-1)H\), so the signed difference is at most \(-4H\). At \(n=2\) the module is transitive. Thus even reversing the direction of comparison cannot provide a new nontransitive single-module lift. Any success from these seeds using homogeneous substitutions must use **at least two nontransitive modules or one nontransitive module together with other nontrivial substitutions** at exponent 61. The independent arbitrary-transitive-width obstruction still applies.
