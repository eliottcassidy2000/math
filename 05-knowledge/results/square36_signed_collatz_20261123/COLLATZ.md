# Eighteen children modulo 36 and a twelve-cycle under alternating signs

On positive odd integers put \(U_s(n)=(3n+s)/2^{v_2(3n+s)}\), where \(s=\pm1\). Choosing the sign from the current residue and choosing it from the time give different exact results. Here the mod-4 character is \(\chi(n)=1\) at \(1\bmod4\) and \(-1\) at \(3\bmod4\). For odd primes these classes are the split and inert cases in the Gaussian integers. The dynamics uses the character on *all* odd inputs.

## The sign chosen from the input

**PROVED.** Each positive odd \(n\) has exactly one sign with at least two divisions by two, namely \(s=\chi(n)\). Put \(D(n)=U_{\chi(n)}(n)\). Then \(D(n)-1\le 3(n-1)/4\), so every positive odd integer reaches 1. Removing the loop at 1 gives a rooted tree. For the other choice the valuation is exactly one. In the coordinate \(n=2u+1\) this other map is \(u\mapsto\lceil3u/2\rceil\), which increases strictly at every positive \(u\). Both assertions follow by substituting the two odd classes modulo four.

**Exact inverse.** An odd multiple of 3 has no children. For any other positive odd parent \(y\), let \(\epsilon=1\) or \(-1\) match \(y\bmod3\). At each exponent \(a\ge2\) there is exactly one preimage with valuation \(a\), namely
\[
 x_a=\frac{2^a y-(-1)^a\epsilon}{3}.
\]
Here and in the modular statements the inverse at the root includes its self-preimage \(x_2=1\). The numerator is divisible by three for exactly the displayed sign. The quotient is odd and positive, and \(3x_a+(-1)^a\epsilon=2^a y\) has valuation exactly \(a\). Since \(a\ge2\), reduction modulo four already enforces the input's sign rule.

**The ternary selector.** For every \(k\ge1\), the exponents with \(3^k\mid x_a\) occupy one residue modulo \(3^k\). In particular, one third of the exponents give children which are leaves. To choose the residue, solve
\[
               (-2)^a y\equiv\epsilon\pmod{3^{k+1}}.
\]
The element \(-2=1-3\) generates the group of residues congruent to 1 modulo 3 at this precision. Indeed \(v_3((-2)^j-1)=1+v_3(j)\) for positive \(j\), by the elementary lifting-the-exponent identity. Thus the residue lifts by one of three choices at each digit. More generally, for any positive odd \(m\), the condition \(m\mid x_a\) has no solutions if \(\gcd(y,m)>1\). Otherwise it is the discrete logarithm of \(\epsilon y^{-1}\) to base \(-2\) modulo \(3m\). A solution exists exactly when this element belongs to the cyclic subgroup generated by \(-2\), and fixes \(a\) modulo its order. This is the same distinction between invertible odd phases and valuation data that enters the repository's rational Collatz constructions. [Earlier rational-cycle congruence construction](../prime_brackets_collatz_2311_20261122/RATIONAL-CYCLES.md)

**Every odd residue modulo 36 occurs exactly once in eighteen consecutive exponents.** More generally, take \(b\ge2\), \(k\ge1\) and \(a\ge b\). Modulo \(2^b3^k\), the children cover exactly the two classes \(x\equiv\pm\epsilon/3\pmod{2^b}\), each with every ternary residue. Each occurs once per \(2\cdot3^k\) consecutive exponents. In fact \(w_a=(-1)^a x_a\) satisfies
\[
 3w_a=(-2)^a y-\epsilon .
\]
Modulo \(2^b\), \(w_a=-\epsilon/3\) once \(a\ge b\). Modulo \(3^k\), the sequence \(w_a\) visits every residue once per \(3^k\) by the preceding subgroup calculation. Specifying the parity of \(a\) fixes the sign and imposes no condition on \(a\bmod3^k\). The two binary classes exhaust the odd classes when \(b=2\). Taking \(k=2\) gives 36.

**Fixing the child modulo 36 leaves exactly two possibilities modulo 37** when \(37\nmid y\). The exponent is fixed modulo 18 and \(-2\) has order 36 modulo 37, as follows from its powers at 12 and 18. The two possibilities occur at \(a,a+18\), with the same parity. Because \((-2)^{18}=-1\bmod37\) their predicted sum is
\[
                  x_a+x_{a+18}\equiv-2(-1)^a\epsilon/3\pmod{37}.
\]
They differ because \(y\) is a unit. For parent 1 and child class \(1\bmod36\), the only possible classes modulo 37 are 1 and 23. They occur already at exponents 2 and 20. This gives a simultaneous conditional prediction from the two marginal clocks.

## The sign chosen from the time

Set \((n_{i+1},s_{i+1})=(U_{s_i}(n_i),-s_i)\). The signed coordinate \(z_i=s_i n_i\) conjugates this phase map to \(z\mapsto(-3z-1)/2^{v_2(3z+1)}\) on nonzero odd integers. Even periods refer to returns of the full state.

**PROVED.** Starting at \((n,s)\), the exact initial number of successive steps with valuation one is \(v_2(5n+s)-1\). For a single-division step the identity \(2(5n_{i+1}+s_{i+1})=3(5n_i+s_i)\) drops this valuation by one. Conversely \(4\mid5n_i+s_i\) is equivalent to \(3n_i+s_i\equiv2\pmod4\). Thus runs of any specified finite length exist by a single dyadic congruence. Time alternation can produce arbitrarily long growing runs.

A more general local calculation makes the limitation precise. Given any externally fixed signs and any sequence of positive valuations \(a_0,\ldots,a_{d-1}\), the starting odds realizing it form one residue class modulo \(2^{1+\sum a_i}\). Invert \(n_i=(2^{a_i}n_{i+1}-s_i)/3\) in the 2-adic integers, letting the last state range over all odds. Each inverse takes an odd argument to an odd argument and the composite slope is \(2^{\sum a_i}/3^d\). These local cylinders have relative density \(2^{-\sum a_i}\) among the odd integers for either the constant or the alternating schedule. The periodic points on positive integers can nevertheless be different.

**FINITE-EXACT.** Among positive phase cycles of primitive period at most 4,096, exactly two occur up to cyclic rotation. One has \(n=1\) in both phases. The other starts at \((13,-1)\) and its successive odd inputs are
\[
13,19,29,43,65,97,73,109,41,61,23,17.
\]
Its valuation word is \((1,1,1,1,1,2,1,3,1,3,2,2)\). Eleven entries are prime and the remaining entry is \(65=5\cdot13\).

**Why a finite computation covers all sizes.** For a period \(d\), put \(A=\sum_i v_2(3n_i+s_i)\) and \(m=\min_i n_i\). Multiplication around the cycle gives \(2^A=\prod_i(3+s_i/n_i)\). Exactly half of the signs are positive, so
\[
             (9m-3)^d\ \le\ 2^{2A}m^d\ \le\ (9m+3)^d .
\]
After division by \(m^d\), the interval shrinks monotonically as \(m\) increases. Set \(A_0=\lfloor\log_2(3^d)\rfloor\), computed by integer bit length. If at a chosen cutoff \(m=M\) both neighboring integers \(A_0,A_0+1\) place their powers on opposite sides outside this interval, no integer \(A\) can work at any \(m\ge M\). For every even \(d\le4096\), integer powers certify this at \(M=2{,}538{,}899\). Searching smaller odd minima in both phases then suffices. Stop a trial when it first drops below its proposed minimum, first returns to the starting phase state, or reaches the period bound. The positive cycle problem at unrestricted period and the global orbit problem remain open here.

**Comparison with the fixed minus sign.** Its three classical positive cycles, accelerated to odd inputs, are \((1)\), \((5,7)\), and \((17,25,37,55,41,61,91)\). Negation identifies them with the three known negative cycles of the usual plus-sign map. The length-12 alternating cycle actually shares the directed minus edge \(41\to61\) with the last of these and then takes the plus edge \(61\to23\). By comparison, the modulo-four rule sends \(7\to5\to1\) and \(21\to1\). These calculations specify the common directed edges and where each selector changes the continuation. [Bell and Lagarias, §1](https://www.impan.pl/shop/en/publication/transaction/download/product/82190)
