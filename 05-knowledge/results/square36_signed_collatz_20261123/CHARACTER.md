# Three quadratic characters detect the square-sum graph and transport tournament counts

**PROVED with finite-exact witnesses.** Three inert primes detect every edge of the square-sum graph on the first 36 integers. They also orient a majority tournament on the same labels. For a common word of signed Collatz branches, the parity of the total number of halvings chooses between two possible Hamilton-path counts of that tournament. One legal branch realizes both a 13-to-11 flip and a 13-to-15 flip on different five-element subsets.

The inherited [square-sum reflection theorem, THM-4510](../../../01-canon/theorems/THM-4510-sum-graphs-as-reflection-orbits-pythagorean-zigzags.md) keeps the actual sum. The [Heegner contact code](../chanpak_heegner_selector_20261122/HEEGNER.md) uses quadratic characters for prescribed orientations. The [signed-cycle clock](../prime_brackets_collatz_2311_20261122/README.md) keeps the number of halvings. The [square-sum port obstruction](../../../session211/square-sum-port-bridge/PROOF.md) rules out the direct transfer of a square-sum path count to twice that count plus one. Here one set of observable signs reads sums for the undirected graph and differences for the tournament.

## A sharp three-character range

Let \(Q_n\) be the simple graph on \(1,\dots,n\) whose distinct vertices are adjacent when their sum is a square. The attainable pair sums fill \([3,2n-1]\) for \(n\ge2\). A set of odd primes detects \(Q_n\) if a sum is a square exactly when it is a quadratic residue, including zero, at every prime in the set. Write a negative Legendre sign as bit one.

**PROVED.** Three primes congruent to 3 modulo 4 suffice for every \(Q_n\) through \(n=71\). Two arbitrary odd primes fail from \(n=16\), and three arbitrary odd primes fail from \(n=72\). Thus exactly three are necessary and sufficient for \(16\le n\le71\).

For the lower bounds, extend the character bit at its own prime by choosing either bit on that prime generator and extending additively on square classes. Every original rejection persists because it came from a prime coprime to the rejected integer. Consequently every nonsquare in the window must have nonzero image in the resulting binary vector space. At sums through 30, all seven nonzero square classes generated by 2, 3 and 5 occur (use 8 for the class of 2). Their images must be linearly independent on those three generators. With just three characters we may therefore use the colors of 2, 3 and 5 as basis vectors. At sums through 143, for each \(q=7,11,13\) the products of \(q\) with \(1,2,3,5,6,10\) all occur. This leaves each of the three prime colors only the colors of 15 and 30. The three sums \(77,91,143\) require the colors of 7, 11 and 13 to be pairwise distinct, which is impossible.

For the upper bound assign independent colors to 2, 3 and 5, give 7 the color of 30 and every prime from 11 to 139 the color of 15. The squarefree part of a nonsquare at most 142 contains at most one prime from 11 upward. If none occurs, the least nontrivial kernel element on \(2,3,5,7\) is 210. If one such prime \(q\) occurs without 7, cancellation requires the extra factor 15, giving at least 165. With 7, it requires the extra factor 2, giving at least 154. Hence the code accepts exactly the squares through 142. For each bit, choose a prime \(p>142\), \(p\equiv3\pmod4\), with the specified signs \((q/p)\) on prime generators. Choose \(p\bmod8\) for the sign at 2. At each odd generator, quadratic reciprocity permits both signs by choosing a nonzero residue for \(p\bmod q\). CRT and Dirichlet give infinitely many primes realizing each row. [Prior use of this prime realization, THM-5125](../../../01-canon/theorems/THM-5125-residue-tournament-model-heegner-domination-and-cycle-gas-identities.md)

**FINITE-EXACT at 36.** The least possible largest inert prime in a three-prime detector is 331. All seven triples attaining it consist of 283, 331 and one of \(79,103,107,131,151,223,251\). Restricting to the seven odd Heegner primes requires five, and the complete list of minimal-cardinality choices is

\[
 (3,7,11,19,67),\quad(3,7,19,43,67),\quad
 (3,11,19,43,67),\quad(7,11,19,67,163).
\]

The finite test intersects the masks of nonsquare sums in \([3,71]\) accepted at each prime. On \(P=(79,283,331)\) a square-sum path has an equivalent description in three Paley tournaments: send its ordered labels to \(z_j=(-1)^{j-1}x_j\). In every layer the differences \(z_{j+1}-z_j=(-1)^j(x_j+x_{j+1})\) have the prescribed alternating signs exactly when all its sums are integer squares. The least prime exceeds 72, so signed distinct labels cannot collide in a layer. A Hamilton path uses exactly one signed copy of each original label with sign fixed by position.

## How the multipliers act on a majority tournament

Use \(\chi(v)=((v/79),(v/283),(v/331))\), again writing negative entries as ones. Directly,
\[
   \chi(2)=011,\qquad \chi(3)=\chi(-1)=111,\qquad \chi(-3)=000.
\]
The three primes are inert for the Gaussian field and split for the Eisenstein field. The first assertion uses \(p\equiv3\pmod4\), the second \(p\equiv1\pmod3\). This explains why multiplication by 3 reverses each Paley layer. At a general odd modulus, the split/inert multiplication table in the question is exactly the character of the odd integer modulo 4. For composite moduli this terminology describes the character of the product. For instance 21 is split in this vocabulary although its two prime factors are inert.

For finite integer labels distinct modulo each prime in \(P\), orient \(x\to y\) when a majority of the three signs on \(y-x\) are positive. Every pair has a direction since all three primes are 3 modulo 4. A prescribed signed odd-to-odd itinerary of \(m\) steps, with signs \(\epsilon_i\in\{-1,1\}\) and exact positive valuations \(k_i\), acts affinely with multiplier \(3^m/2^A\), where \(A=\sum k_i\). Its action on **pair differences** adds the bit word
\[
                       (m\bmod2)111\;\mathbin{\oplus}\;(A\bmod2)011 .
\]
The signs and their order enter the translation, which cancels in every difference. For sums the center must travel with the labels: \(g(x)+g(y)-2g(0)=(3^m/2^A)(x+y)\).

**PROVED.** For every such label set and every prescribed common itinerary, the majority tournament's Hamilton count is one of two specified integers chosen by \(A\bmod2\). At even \(A\) it equals the original count. At odd \(A\) it equals the count after twisting just the 79-layer, the bit word \(100\). Indeed \(111\) takes the entire tournament to its converse, preserving the count by path reversal, and \(011\oplus111=100\). The two counts can coincide. A prescribed itinerary can be physically synchronized on copies of any given label set. Choose an odd residue \(r\pmod{2^{A+1}}\) realizing the given signs and valuations. Such a residue follows backwards from a final odd class: invert 3 modulo each successive power of two in \(3x_i=2^{k_i}x_{i+1}-\epsilon_i\). Start the copies at \(2^{2b}x+r\) with \(2b\ge A+1\). The input multiplier is a square in each layer. A sufficiently large positive representative of \(r\) keeps a prescribed finite itinerary positive when required.

| Itinerary on odd integers | Odd steps | Total halvings | Twist before converse |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| \(3x+1\) starting at 1 | 1 | 2 | 111 |
| \(3x-1\) starting at 1 | 1 | 1 | 100 |

The three known minus cycles select the same count after allowing path reversal. Negation gives the corresponding three known plus cycles on negative integers. Their full ordered itineraries distinguish cycles that give the same count. The cycle list is not asserted to be exhaustive. ## Exact single-arc witnesses

For a legal common single step, start the minus map at \(4x+1\); the odd output is \(6x+1\). Alternatively start the plus map at \(4x+3\), with odd output \(6x+5\). Both act identically on differences. The input majority tournament equals that on the original labels, while the output twists the 79-layer. An arc changes precisely when its other two layer signs disagree. The following counts are by the endpoint subset recurrence. Labels in the changed pair refer to the original set.

| Original labels | Before | After | Physical changes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| \(1,3,5,6,7\) | 13 | 11 | pair \(1,6\) |
| \(1,3,5,6,9\) | 13 | 15 | pair \(1,6\) |
| \(1,\ldots,9\) | 2831 | 3159 | six pairs with differences 5 or 7 |

The two sources of height 13 are **isomorphic**. An isomorphism from the first labeling to the second sends the labels \(1,3,5,6,7\) to \(5,1,3,9,6\) in that order. Transported back to the first labeling, the downward flip uses \(\{1,6\}\) and the upward flip uses \(\{3,7\}\). Thus one ordinary tournament class supports two different arithmetic coordinate assignments which give opposite signed exits. Holding a coordinate assignment fixed retains only its two counts.

The three values 11, 13 and 15 have minimum order five, with respective minimum isomorphism multiplicities **1, 1, 2**. The labeled census on all \(2^{10}\) five-vertex tournaments gives masses 120, 120 and 64. On at most four vertices every count is at most five (the four-vertex census suffices by adjoining a universal sink). An automorphism acts freely on directed Hamilton paths, so the order of the group at height 11 or 13 divides both that prime and \(5!\), and is one. At height 15 the displayed upper witness has automorphism order 3 and degree multiset \(1,2,2,2,3\). The regular cyclic tournament on \(\mathbb Z/5\) with forward differences 1 and 2 has height 15 and automorphism order 5. Their orbit sizes \(40+24=64\) exhaust the mass.
