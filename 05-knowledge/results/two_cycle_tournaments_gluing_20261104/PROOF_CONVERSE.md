## 1. Notation

`Y` is a strong tournament of order `n`. A vertex `c` is a *cut vertex* if `Y − c` is not strong; then the strong components of `Y − c` are linearly ordered, `Y − c = Q₁ ⇒ Q₂ ⇒ ⋯ ⇒ Q_r` (`r ≥ 2`, every arc between different components goes forward), each `Q_i` a single vertex or a strong tournament of order `≥ 3`. For a tournament `Q` and `x, y ∈ V(Q)`: `h(Q)` = number of Hamiltonian paths of `Q`, `s_x(Q)` = those starting at `x`, `e_y(Q)` = those ending at `y` (`h = s = e = 1` for a single vertex). For a cut vertex `c` put

`S(c) = Σ_{x ∈ Q₁, c → x} s_x(Q₁)`, `E(c) = Σ_{y ∈ Q_r, y → c} e_y(Q_r)`, `M(c) = Π_{1 < i < r} h(Q_i)`.

`T^p` (Shelor insertion at `p`): `T` plus a new vertex beating every vertex except `p`, with `p →` new vertex. `T_x` (sole-exit attachment at `x`): `T` plus a new vertex `w` with `w → x` and every other vertex `→ w`. Facts used throughout: `hc(T^p) = e_p(T)` and `hc(T_x) = s_x(T)` (a Hamiltonian cycle of `T^p` is `p → v → x ⇝ p` with `x ⇝ p` a Hamiltonian path of `T` ending at `p`, and conversely; dually for `T_x`); and the **extension lemma** (opus-S74 §2, THM-4877 (1)): if `T′` is strong and `v` has both in- and out-neighbours in `T′` then `hc(T′) ≤ hc(T′ + v)`.

A *gluing* is `A ⊕_s B` or `A ⊕_{s → s′} B` as in README §0 / THM-4877 (2): `A` uniquely Hamiltonian rooted at a sole entry `s` (or at two consecutive sole entries `s → s′`), `B` uniquely Hamiltonian rooted at a sole exit `s` (or two consecutive sole exits `s → s′`), identified at the root, every cross arc from `B` to `A` except `a_k → b₁` (`a_k` = the predecessor of the root on `A`'s cycle, `b₁` = the successor of the root on `B`'s cycle). The class of gluings is closed under taking the converse: `(A ⊕_s B)^op = B^op ⊕_s A^op` (in `B^op`, `s` is a sole entry with predecessor `b₁`; in `A^op`, `s` is a sole exit with successor `a_k`; the exceptional arc `b₁ → a_k` is the one required).

## 2. Lemma A (cut-vertex product law)

**Lemma A.** If `c` is a cut vertex of the strong tournament `Y`, then `hc(Y) = S(c) · M(c) · E(c)`.

*Proof.* Every Hamiltonian cycle of `Y` passes through `c` once, `… y → c → x …`, and the rest of it is a Hamiltonian path `P` of `Y − c` from `x` to `y`. Since all arcs between different components of `Y − c` go forward, once `P` leaves a component it never returns to it or to an earlier one, so `P` is `P₁ P₂ ⋯ P_r` with `P_i` a Hamiltonian path of `Q_i`, `x ∈ Q₁` and `y ∈ Q_r`; conversely any such concatenation with `c → x` and `y → c` is a Hamiltonian cycle (consecutive components are joined by forward arcs). Counting: `Σ_{x} s_x(Q₁)` over the out-neighbours `x` of `c` in `Q₁`, times `h(Q_i)` for each middle component, times `Σ_y e_y(Q_r)` over the in-neighbours `y` of `c` in `Q_r`. ∎

Two consequences for `hc(Y) = 2`: every middle component is a single vertex (a strong tournament of order `m ≥ 3` has at least `m` Hamiltonian paths, the rotations of a Hamiltonian cycle, so `h(Q_i) ≥ 3` would force `hc ≥ 3`), and `{S(c), E(c)} = {1, 2}`. Also `S(c) ≥ 1` and `E(c) ≥ 1` force `c` to have an out-neighbour in `Q₁` and an in-neighbour in `Q_r`.

## 3. Lemma B (cut vertices exist)

**Lemma B.** Every tournament of order `n ≥ 6` with exactly two Hamiltonian cycles has a cut vertex.

*Proof.* For `6 ≤ n ≤ 16` this is the census (status paragraph). For `n ≥ 7` it follows from the theorem of Thomassen (1980), in the explicit form of Busch (2006, Corollary 2): a 2-connected tournament of order `n` has at least `β^{n/3 − 1}` Hamiltonian cycles with `β = ∛5 ≈ 1.710`, which exceeds 2 from `n = 7` (`β^{4/3} ≈ 2.05`); a tournament with exactly two Hamiltonian cycles of order `≥ 7` is therefore not 2-connected, i.e. has a cut vertex (it is strong, having a Hamiltonian cycle). ∎

(Only the existence for `n ≥ 17` depends on Thomassen–Busch; the sidecar records that the printed exponent is typeset ambiguously and that every reading gives three cycles from order 14.)

## 4. Lemma C (two in-neighbours: the last piece is uniquely Hamiltonian and the pair is a gluing pattern)

**Lemma C.** Let `hc(Y) = 2`, let `c` be a cut vertex with `S(c) = 1`, and suppose `c` has two in-neighbours `y, y′` in `Q_r` (so `e_y(Q_r) = e_{y′}(Q_r) = 1` and `E(c) = 2`). Then:
1. `Q_r` is uniquely Hamiltonian.
2. With `y₀` the non-critical sole entry of `Q_r` (THM-4868 (C)) and `y₀−, y₀−−` its first and second predecessors on the cycle of `Q_r`, the pair `{y, y′}` is `{y₀, y₀−}`, or `{y₀−, y₀−−}` with `y₀−` a sole entry, or `{y₀, y₀−−}` with `y₀−` a sole entry.
3. In the first two cases `Y = A ⊕_s B` with `A = Q_r`, `s` the sole entry of the pair (`y₀`, resp. `y₀−`), `a_k` the other element, `B = Y[(V − Q_r) ∪ {s}]`, `b₁ = c`. In the third case `Y = A ⊕_{s → s′} B` with `A = Q_r`, `s = y₀−`, `s′ = y₀`, `a_k = y₀−−`, `B = Y[(V − Q_r) ∪ {s, s′}]`, `b₁ = c`.

*Proof.* (1) `|Q_r| ≥ 3` (a single vertex gives `E = 1`), so `Q_r` is strong; `hc(Q_r^y) = e_y(Q_r) = 1`, and the extension lemma applied to `Q_r^y = Q_r + v` (`v` has the in-neighbour `y` and out-neighbours everywhere else) gives `hc(Q_r) ≤ 1`.
(2) By THM-4868 (G) (kind-pasteur, PROVED; stated for uniquely Hamiltonian tournaments of order `≥ 4`), the vertices `p` of `Q_r` with `e_p(Q_r) = 1` are exactly `y₀`, `y₀−`, and `y₀−−` when `y₀−` is a sole entry. Two distinct elements of this set form one of the three listed pairs. For `|Q_r| = 3` (`Q_r` the 3-cycle) every vertex is a sole entry with `e = 1` and any pair is `{z, z−}`, the first case.
(3) First two cases: `s` is a sole entry of `A = Q_r`, so its unique in-neighbour in `A` is its cycle predecessor, which is the other element `a_k` of the pair; `A` is uniquely Hamiltonian with cycle `s a₁ ⋯ a_k s`. Put `U = V − Q_r = Q₁ ∪ {q₂, …, q_{r−1}} ∪ {c}` and `B = Y[U ∪ {s}]`. Arcs: every vertex of `Q₁ ∪ {q_i}` beats every vertex of `Q_r` (forward arcs); `c` beats every vertex of `Q_r` except its two in-neighbours `s, a_k`. Hence in `B` the only out-arc of `s` is `s → c` (`s` is a sole exit of `B`), and the arcs between `A − s` and `B − s` all go from `B` to `A` except `a_k → c`. `B` is uniquely Hamiltonian: a Hamiltonian cycle of `B` must use `s → c`, then an out-neighbour `x` of `c` in `U − c`, then cover `Q₁ ∪ {q_i}` (whose arcs go `Q₁ ⇒ q₂ ⇒ ⋯`, so the path starts in `Q₁` and ends at `q_{r−1}`, or in `Q₁` if `r = 2`) and return to `s` (every vertex of `U − c` beats `s`); the number of such cycles is `Σ_{x ∈ Q₁, c → x} s_x(Q₁) = S(c) = 1`, and that one cycle is `s c b₂ ⋯ b_l s` with `b₁ = c`. So `Y = A ⊕_s B` by definition.
Third case: `s = y₀−` and `s′ = y₀` are consecutive sole entries of `A = Q_r` (`y₀−` precedes `y₀` on the cycle), `a_k = y₀−−` is the predecessor of `s`, and the in-neighbours of `c` in `Q_r` are `s′` and `a_k`. In `B = Y[U ∪ {s, s′}]`: `s`'s out-arcs are `s → s′` only (`c → s`, no arcs to `U − c`), `s′`'s out-arcs are `s′ → c` only; so `s, s′` are consecutive sole exits of `B`, and a Hamiltonian cycle of `B` is `s s′ c x ⋯ s`, counted by `S(c) = 1`. Cross arcs between `A − {s, s′}` and `B − {s, s′}`: from `B` to `A` except `a_k → c`. So `Y = A ⊕_{s → s′} B`. ∎

## 5. Lemma D (descent)

**Lemma D.** Let `hc(Y) = 2`, let `c` be a cut vertex with `S(c) = 1`, and suppose `c` has exactly one in-neighbour `y` in `Q_r` (so `e_y(Q_r) = 2`). Then `y` is a cut vertex of `Y`, `S(y) = 1`, `E(y) = 2`, and the last component of `Y − y` is a proper subset of `Q_r − {y}`.

*Proof.* Put `U = Q₁ ∪ {q₂, …, q_{r−1}} ∪ {c}`. The only arc from `Q_r` into `U` is `y → c`: vertices of `Q_r` have no arcs into `Q₁ ∪ {q_i}` (forward components), and `c`'s only in-neighbour in `Q_r` is `y`. So in `Y − y` no arc enters `U` from `Q_r − y`, both sets being nonempty (`|Q_r| ≥ 3`): `Y − y` is not strong. Its first strong component `R₁` is the unique source component and reaches every vertex, so it lies in `U` (nothing in `U` is reachable from `Q_r − y`); its last component `R_m` lies in `Q_r − y` (nothing in `Q_r − y` reaches `U`), so `R_m ⊊ Q_r`. By Lemma A, `hc(Y) = S(y) M(y) E(y) = 2`, so `S(y) ≥ 1`. The out-neighbours of `y` in `U` are `c` only, hence `c ∈ R₁` and `S(y) = s_c(R₁)`. Now `R₁ − c ⊆ Q₁ ∪ {q_i}`, and `R₁` is the set of vertices of `U` reachable from `c` and reaching `c`: it is `{c}`, or `{c} ∪ Q₁ ∪ {q₂, …, q_j}` for the last chain vertex `q_j` beating `c` (when `|Q₁| ≥ 3` every vertex of `Q₁` other than the unique out-neighbour `x` of `c` beats `c`, because `S(c) = 1` and every vertex of a strong tournament starts a Hamiltonian path; when `Q₁ = {x}`, the chain segment up to `q_j` is what reaches `c`). A Hamiltonian path of `R₁` starting at `c` continues to an out-neighbour of `c` and then covers `R₁ − c`, whose arcs go `Q₁ ⇒ q₂ ⇒ ⋯ ⇒ q_j`; it must enter `Q₁` first (no chain vertex has an arc into `Q₁`), at `x`, and then it is forced: `s_c(R₁) = s_x(Q₁) · 1 = S(c) = 1` (and `= 1` trivially if `R₁ = {c}`). Hence `S(y) = 1`, `M(y) = 1`, `E(y) = 2`, with the factor 2 on the last component `R_m ⊊ Q_r`. ∎

## 6. The theorem

**Theorem (converse of the gluing theorem).** Every tournament `Y` of order `n ≥ 6` with exactly two Hamiltonian cycles is a vertex gluing or an arc gluing of two rooted uniquely Hamiltonian tournaments.

*Proof.* By Lemma B, `Y` has a cut vertex `c₀`; by Lemma A, `{S(c₀), E(c₀)} = {1, 2}`. If `S(c₀) = 2` replace `Y` by its converse (gluings are converse-closed, and in `Y^op` the same vertex is a cut vertex with the roles of `S` and `E` exchanged); so assume `S(c₀) = 1`, `E(c₀) = 2`. Let `c = c₀`. If `c` has a single in-neighbour `y` in its last component, Lemma D replaces `c` by `y`: a cut vertex with `S = 1`, `E = 2`, and a strictly smaller last component. Since the last component cannot shrink forever, after finitely many steps the current cut vertex `c` has two in-neighbours in its last component, and Lemma C exhibits `Y` as a gluing. ∎

The certificate `descent_certificate.py` follows exactly this proof (first cut vertex, converse if `S = 2`, descent while a single in-neighbour, then Lemma C's pattern and the reconstruction of `A`, `B` and the cross arcs) on every two-cycle class of orders 6–12, checking every intermediate claim; at order 12 the descent takes up to 7 steps.

## 8. The cut-vertex composition as a recursive classification

Lemma A says that tournaments with a cut vertex are *vertex joins* `Q₁ ⇒ ⋯ ⇒ Q_r` closed by `c`, with `hc` multiplicative in the rooted data `(S, h, …, h, E)`; a tournament with `hc ≤ 2` is a chain of a first piece, singletons and a last piece, and the rooted pieces with `s_x = 1`, `s_x = 2`, `e_y = 1`, `e_y = 2` are themselves "uniquely Hamiltonian minus a vertex" or "two-cycle minus a vertex" (`hc(T_x) = s_x(T)`, `hc(T^p) = e_p(T)`). This is the recursion behind the descent, and, combined with THM-4868's description of the `e = 1` vertices, behind the whole classification. For Hamiltonian paths the corresponding law is a two-path-cover sum (each Hamiltonian path of `Y` splits at `c` into a path ending at an in-neighbour of `c` and one starting at an out-neighbour, either possibly empty), i.e. a root-response convolution in the sense of THM-4865 / THM-4866, not a product.
