# Hamiltonian-path spectrum: literature and exact reduction

For a finite tournament T, let H(T) be its number of directed Hamiltonian paths. Chan and Pak, *Computational Complexity of Counting Coincidences*, §6.5, Conjecture 6.2 (2024), explicitly ask whether the spectrum is exactly the positive odd integers excluding 7 and 21, attributing it to bof and Royle: https://www.math.ucla.edu/~pak/papers/DominoPermanent16.pdf. Royle's original report says that all allowed odds **strictly less than 80,557** occur among tournaments on at most 11 vertices: https://mathoverflow.net/questions/232751/the-number-of-hamiltonian-paths-in-a-tournament. Chan–Pak summarize it as up to 80,557; use the original strict boundary unless the endpoint is independently verified. These are finite computational data, not an all-integers theorem.

Rédei's parity theorem gives only odd H. The known exclusion of 7 and 21 follows from strong-component multiplication and Busch's exact strong-tournament minimum, plus a finite check up to order 6. Busch, *A Note on the Number of Hamiltonian Paths in Strong Tournaments*, EJC 13 (2006), N3: https://www.kurims.kyoto-u.ac.jp/EMIS/journals/EJC/Volume_13/PDF/v13i1n3.pdf. For n>=3 the exact minimum is f(3q)=3·5^(q−1), f(3q+1)=5^q, f(3q+2)=9·5^(q−1); the last form has q>=1. In particular f(5)=9 and f(7)=25. On orders through 6, a finite check gives strong counts avoiding 7 and 21. Moon and Yang explicitly state the exact theorem here: https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=78c085212810b466efaf3f903baeb284a695f719&repid=rep1&type=pdf. The 7 and 21 result is not claimed as new; the 2016 discussion already records it.

## Exact two-lane reduction (repository THM-4094)

Let S be the total spectrum and S_str the strong spectrum. Strong components of a tournament form a total order under domination, and every Hamiltonian path traverses them consecutively; therefore H is the product of the component counts. Assuming the known exclusions 7,21, the full spectrum conjecture is equivalent to:

1. Every odd prime p != 7 belongs to S_str.
2. Every 7p, where p is odd prime and p != 3, belongs to S_str.

Necessity: a tournament of count p must have a strong component of count p. A tournament of count 7p must have a strong component of count 7p, since any proper nonunit factorization has a component with count 7, including p=7. Sufficiency: write an allowed odd target N=7^a m with gcd(m,7)=1. For a=0, multiply the prime witnesses. If a>0 is even, use 49^(a/2), then the prime factors of m; for odd a>=3 use 343·49^((a−3)/2), then the prime factors of m. If a=1 and m=3^k, the excluded values force k>=2, so use 63·3^(k−2). Otherwise choose a prime p>=5 dividing m and use 7p times the remaining prime factors. Order joins realize each product. This is a reduction, not a proof that the two infinite lanes are attained.

The requisite 49, 63, 343 witnesses were independently found and verified in `verify_witnesses.py` using literal 8! permutation counting and a transitive-closure strong-connectivity check. Label vertices 0,...,7; for the lexicographically ordered pairs i<j, bit k=1 means i→j and bit 0 means j→i. The respective 28-bit masks are 201564224, 104288510, 136003753. Output in `verify.out`. The two searches use an independent C++ subset-path dynamic program (`find.cpp`), and the verifier uses direct permutations rather than that recurrence.
