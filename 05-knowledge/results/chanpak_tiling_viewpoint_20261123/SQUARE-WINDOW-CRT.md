The prime/composite window has an exact recursive rule with a growing state. In the interior of \((s^2,(s+2)^2)\) for odd \(s\ge3\), any composite odd number has a prime factor at most \(s\), since an odd prime below \(s+2\) is at most \(s\). At \(x=s^2+2j\), mark a prime's lane by \(j\equiv -s^2/2\bmod p\) for every odd \(p\le s\). Taking the complement of the lanes gives all primes in that interior. At \(s=3\) there is exactly one lane, \(j=0\bmod3\). The subsequent window already needs the lane for 5 (for example 35). The finite seed supplies the rule for generating the next sieve data, not a fixed periodic primality word: if a finite modulus identifies a prime \(\ell\), it also identifies all composites \(\ell(1+kt)\) with \(t\) the modulus and \(k\ge1\).

## A sharp exact modular decoder for the graph itself

In \(Q_n\), the undirected graph on \(1,\ldots,n\) with edges exactly when the sum is square, all sums \(3,\ldots,2n-1\) occur. Replace the edge rule by “the sum is a square modulo \(m\).”

* **144 is the smallest modulus that recovers \(Q_{24}\) or \(Q_{25}\) exactly**, even if any modulus is allowed. Modulo 16, the only nonsquare integers in \(3,\ldots,49\) surviving are \(17,20,32,33,41,48\), with residues \(8,2,5,6,5,3\) modulo 9, none square. The same rule gives \(Q_{26}\). Its first false sum is 52, e.g. \(25+27\). For minimality use the nonsquare witnesses \(40,15,24,33,28,22,20,21,32\), all within \(Q_{24}\). Successively deleting each modulus \(2\le m<144\) for which the next witness is a square residue deletes \(58,33,28,11,6,3,1,1,1\) moduli (and modulus 1 trivially accepts every sum). The checker enumerates the half-residue roots \(0,\ldots,\lfloor m/2\rfloor\).
* **Sharp macro radius for the same three primes:** \(16\cdot9\cdot11=1584\) gives the exact graph through \(Q_{48}\), and no smaller modulus supported on \(\{2,3,11\}\) does so (bounded checker). **No powers whatsoever** of these three primes give the exact graph at \(Q_{49}\). The first unavoidable false sum is \(97=48+49\), nonsquare over the integers; it is \(1\bmod 8\), \(1\bmod3\) and \(9\bmod11\). Unit square lifting gives a square root modulo every power of each prime. The modulus 1584 proves no earlier sum is an unavoidable false positive, since it rejects all nonsquares through 96.
* **Growing-prime exact decoder:** for any bound \(S\), each nonsquare \(t\le S\) has an odd valuation \(v_p(t)\). For each prime \(p\le S\), keep the largest odd \(e_p\le\lfloor\log_p S\rfloor\) and test modulo \(p^{e_p+1}\). This exposes that odd valuation for at least one factor of every nonsquare; all squares pass. For any fixed finite collection of primes there are infinitely many global false positives even if arbitrary powers are used: choose \(t\equiv1\bmod (8\prod_{p\text{ odd}}p)\). All such numbers are local squares at the specified primes, and only \(O(\sqrt X)\) of them through \(X\) can be integer squares.

Modulo 144 the graph is literally unchanged by replacing its edge test at the first \(Q_{24}\to Q_{25}\) change of boundary channel. The [exposed-cover calculation](../session321_selective_returns_and_fano_wiring_20261113/SQUARE_OVERLAP_PORTS.md) gives no Hamilton path at 24 and ten at 25: the ear \(11-25-24\) exits to 1 or 12, at covers counted by 4 and 6. It is not enough to record that both contacts are present. Likewise, to use the tournament residue decoder for an exact target one needs the actual two integer reads of the strong bridge, not just their CRT values. The false edge at \(48,49\) shows the loss of information sharply in the square-sum setting, without claiming that the two graph problems are equivalent.
