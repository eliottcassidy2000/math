## 2. The first prime failure and a certified return

Exactly the seven terms `k=2,...,8` are prime before the first failure. At `k=9`,

\[
a_9=333333331=17\cdot19607843.
\]

The following proper-divisor witnesses certify that all nine terms from `k=9` through `k=17` are composite:

| k | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 | 13 | 14 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| divisor | 17 | 673 | 307 | 19 | 523 | 607 | 181 | 199 | 31 |

The next term is prime:

\[
n=a_{18}=333333333333333331,\qquad
n-1=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot2071723\cdot5363222357.
\tag{2}
\]

This is an exact elementary certificate, not a probable-prime test. The script proves the five factors in (2) prime by trial division, verifies their product, verifies `2^(n-1)=1 mod n`, and verifies

\[
\gcd\bigl(2^{(n-1)/q}-1,n\bigr)=1
\quad\text{for each of those five primes }q.
\]

For any prime divisor `ell` of `n`, the order of 2 modulo `ell` divides `n-1` and fails to divide `(n-1)/q` for every factor `q`. As the factorization in (2) is squarefree, that order is `n-1`. Therefore `n-1 <= ell-1`, so `ell >= n`, proving primality. The modular residues and gcds are retained in the output certificate. Thus “the ninth-digit term fails” is true; “the family stops being prime there” is false.

For every prime `p` not dividing 30,

\[
p\mid a_k\iff 10^k\equiv7\pmod p.
\tag{3}
\]

If 7 lies in the subgroup generated by 10 modulo `p`, its occurrence is exactly one exponent class modulo `ord_p(10)`; otherwise it never occurs. None of 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 divides any term. The final digit excludes 2 and 5; `a_k=1 mod3`; modulo 7 the right side of (3) is zero; modulo 11 powers of 10 are `±1`; modulo 13 their set is `{1,10,9,12,3,4}`. Consequently 17 is the smallest possible prime divisor of a term, and it actually occurs at `k=9`.

For 17 the complete phase is particularly simple:

\[
\operatorname{ord}_{17}(10)=16,\quad 10^8=-1\pmod {17},\quad
a_{k+8}=1-a_k\pmod {17},\quad
17\mid a_k\iff k=9\pmod {16}.
\tag{4}
\]

The append map `a -> 10a+21` fixes residue 9 and cycles the other sixteen residues. Those residues for `k=1,...,16` are

`1,14,8,16,11,12,5,3,0,4,10,2,7,6,13,15`.

The related orders are `ord_17(2)=8`, `2^4=-1 mod17`, and `ord_17(4)=4`. These are exact finite phase relations, not interchangeable counts of Collatz steps. Each initial prime also seeds a later composite phase:

| initial k | prime a_k | ord_(a_k)(10) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2 | 31 | 15 |
| 3 | 331 | 110 |
| 4 | 3331 | 3330 |
| 5 | 33331 | 11110 |

For example, 31 divides exactly the terms `k=2 mod15`: at `k=2` it is the prime itself, while at `k=17` it is a proper factor. This distinction between equality and proper divisibility is essential. Ordinary primality is also different from circular primality: 331 rotates to `133=7*19`, as already recorded in [ternary_digits_20260925.md](ternary_digits_20260925.md).

## 3. A complete signed inverse-fibre decoder

Use **signed edge germs** `(n,sigma)` with `n` positive odd and `sigma in {+1,-1}`. Define

\[
U_\sigma(n)=\frac{3n+\sigma}{2^{v_2(3n+\sigma)}}=y,
\qquad k=v_2(3n+\sigma)\ge1.
\]

The map `(n,sigma) -> (y,k)` is a bijection onto

\[
\{y>0:y\text{ odd},\ 3\nmid y\}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge1}.
\tag{5}
\]

Indeed, given `(y,k)`, let `sigma_k` be the unique member of `{±1}` congruent to `2^k y modulo3`, and put

\[
n_k=\frac{2^k y-\sigma_k}{3}.
\tag{6}
\]

The numerator is divisible by three, positive, and odd; hence `n_k` is a positive odd integer. Equation `3n_k+sigma_k=2^k y` gives the exact valuation `k`, and uniqueness follows directly. Conversely, every source germ has odd target not divisible by three and is recovered by (6).

Now define the half-step

\[
F(n,\sigma)=(2n+\sigma,-\sigma).
\tag{7}
\]

The identity

\[
3(2n+\sigma)-\sigma=2(3n+\sigma)
\]

proves that `F` preserves the common odd **target value** `y` and increases `k` by one. Under (5), `F` is exactly `(y,k) -> (y,k+1)`. This gives all source germs of every joint signed inverse fibre, with no omitted residue classes. More generally,

\[
F^r(n,\sigma)=\left(2^r n+\frac{2^r-(-1)^r}{3}\sigma,\;(-1)^r\sigma\right),\qquad r\ge0.
\tag{8}
\]

In particular,

\[
F^2=(4n+\sigma,\sigma),\quad
F^3=(8n+3\sigma,-\sigma),\quad
F^6=(64n+21\sigma,\sigma).
\tag{9}
\]

Thus a genuine eightfold affine recursion exists after three half-steps, and it switches sheets. The same-sheet sibling map takes two half-steps; its threefold iterate takes six and returns `64n+21sigma`. The clock resolves the apparent mismatch.

For `k>=2` the inverse is

\[
F^{-1}(n,\sigma)=((n+\sigma)/2,-\sigma).
\]

Its odd-parent guard is `n+sigma=2 mod4`, equivalently `k>=2`. At `k=1` that guard fails. Backward iteration therefore terminates at the first source germ by strictly decreasing `k`. It keeps `y` fixed, however: it does not certify a future route for `y`.

For the root target `y=1`, (6) gives the inherited Jacobsthal trunk

\[
n_k=\frac{2^k-(-1)^k}{3},\qquad \sigma_k=(-1)^k.
\]

Its start is `(1,-),(1,+),(3,-),(5,+),(11,-),(21,+),(43,-),(85,+)`. The two occurrences of the numerical value 1 have different sheets and clocks. Dropping that sidecar destroys injectivity.

There is also an exact finite ternary clock. On odd residues modulo `2*3^d` with both signs, `F` is one cycle of length `2*3^d`, for every `d>=1`. For the same-sheet map `S_sigma=F^2`,

\[
S_\sigma^t(n)-n=\frac{4^t-1}{3}(3n+\sigma),\qquad
v_3(S_\sigma^t(n)-n)=v_3(t),\quad t\ge1.
\]

The second equality follows from `3n+sigma` being a 3-adic unit and `v_3(4^t-1)=1+v_3(t)`. Thus `S_sigma` has period exactly `3^d` and traverses every odd residue in its sheet; `F` alternates sheets, doubling that period. This is a finite inverse-fibre residue statement.

**Hostile to forward conjugacy.** `U_-(5)=7` and `F(5,-)=(9,+)` with `U_+(9)=7`. But the next fixed-sheet values are `U_-(7)=5` and `U_+(7)=11`. Therefore (7) does not conjugate forward plus dynamics to forward minus dynamics. The preserved predicate is equality of a single odd target; the lost information is which forward system should act on that target.

## 4. The exact Berggren height, and its half-step obstruction

For a source germ in (6), `gcd(n,y)=1`, because any common divisor divides `3n+sigma-2^k y=sigma`. In the positive odd-root chamber `n>y`, the pair `(n,y)` therefore parametrizes a primitive Pythagorean triple. Use the inherited parabolic operation

\[
B_1(s,t)=(s+2t,t)
\]

from [ternary_berggren_20260925.md](ternary_berggren_20260925.md). Equations (6) and (9) give

\[
F^2(n,\sigma)_n-n=3n+\sigma=2^k y,
\]

so the same-sheet sibling jump is exactly `2^(k-1)` successive `B_1` operations on `(n,y)`. After `j` same-sheet jumps, its accumulated height is

\[
H_j=2^{k-1}\frac{4^j-1}{3}.
\tag{10}
\]

One cross-sheet half-step would require the height

\[
\frac{F(n,\sigma)_n-n}{2y}=\frac{n+\sigma}{2y}.
\]

For every odd `y>1` this is nonintegral: integrality would imply `y | n+sigma`, and combining that with `3n=-sigma modulo y` implies `y | 2sigma`, impossible. For `y=1` it is integral. Thus the joint signed fibre interleaves two sparse same-sheet parabolic rays; an individual half-step is generally not an integer move on that particular `B_1` ray. This does not rule out other Berggren relations. The target `y`, sign, and height remain necessary sidecars, and (10) is not a forward Collatz-time conjugacy.

## 5. Decimal carry descent can be followed by forced growth

The inherited [ternary_digits_20260925.md](ternary_digits_20260925.md) proves `U_+^2(a_k)<a_k` for `k>=4`. At `k=4`, the two exponents are 1 and 4, with `3331 -> 4997 -> 937`. For `k>=5` they are 1 and 3, giving

\[
U_+^2(a_k)=\frac{3\cdot10^k}{16}-1,
\qquad
v_2\bigl(U_+^2(a_k)+1\bigr)=k-4.
\tag{11}
\]

Write this last value as `x=3*2^(k-4)*5^k-1`. For every `1<=j<=k-5`, repeated odd steps have exact halving exponent one and satisfy

\[
U_+^{j+2}(a_k)=3^{j+1}2^{k-4-j}5^k-1.
\tag{12}
\]

To prove the induction, an input `c*2^r-1` with `c` odd and `r>=2` has `3x+1=2(3c*2^(r-1)-1)`, with the parenthesis odd. Each such step increases the positive input. Hence the initial two-step descent is followed by `k-5` forced growing odd steps. In particular, for every `k>=7`,

\[
U_+^4(a_k)=\frac{27\cdot10^k}{64}-1>a_k,
\]

where the difference is `(17*10^k+256)/192`. The two-step decrease is useful local information, but it is not a monotone rank along every later step.

As a representation check, `a_k` tends to `-7/3` in the 2-adic metric. The local odd rules send `-7/3 -> -3 -> -1`, and `-1` is fixed for the plus rule. Equations (11)–(12) describe positive approximants shadowing that local pattern for longer initial stretches. They do not assert that a positive integer orbit enters negative states. No global convergence or divergence conclusion follows from this finite shadowing.
