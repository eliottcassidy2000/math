## A. Correct general lattice proof

**Checkpoint status:** this section is an independent proof review and continuation. It is outside checkpoint1's promoted producer headlines (the metric norm-seven bridge and torus-selected four-code orbit); the general-lattice producer package and its stronger periodic-coloring refinement are audited separately.

Let Q be positive-definite on Z² and D>0. Let S={v:Q(v)=D}; count directions modulo v~−v.

* With zero, one, or two directions, the connected components are respectively isolated vertices, paths, or square grids. In the two-direction case their representatives u,v are linearly independent, and each component is a coset of `H=Zu+Zv`; parity of the unique H coordinates gives a two-coloring. H need not equal the original lattice.
* With three distinct directions u,v,w, form rows `(a²,2ab,b²)` for each vector `(a,b)`. Their determinant is exactly

      2 det(u,v) det(u,w) det(v,w).

  Every factor is nonzero. The three equal-norm equations imply by Cramer's rule that the three entries of the normalized Gram matrix G/D are rational. This is an exact implication, not a genericity argument.
* Now use normalized Q with Q(p)=1 for some edge p. Write its rational Gram matrix G and δ=det G>0. The map

      z(v)=<v,p>_G + sqrt(−δ) det(p,v)

  is an isometric real-linear identification with a subset of the imaginary quadratic field `K=Q(sqrt(−δ))`: the two-dimensional Gram determinant identity gives `z(v) conjugate(z(v))=Q(v)Q(p)=Q(v)`. In particular every normalized edge has field norm one.
* Choose a rational prime ℓ ramified in K (every imaginary quadratic discriminant has a prime divisor) and its unique prime ideal P above ℓ. Conjugation preserves the P-adic valuation. For any normalized edge z,

      0=v_P(z conjugate(z))=2v_P(z).

  Thus every edge is a unit in the local ring O_(K,P). Let H be the additive group generated by the normalized edges. Then H lies in this local ring. Reduction modulo P has residue field F_ℓ, and conjugation acts trivially there. Hence `bar(z)^2=1`, so every edge reduces to `+1` or `−1`.
* Apply reduction **on each coset of H separately**. This yields a graph homomorphism to the additive cycle with steps ±1 on F_ℓ. For ℓ=2 it is K2; for odd ℓ it is C_ℓ, which has a three-coloring. One explicit coloring uses alternating 0/1 on residues 0 through ℓ−2 and color2 on ℓ−1.

**Necessary denominator sidecar:** arbitrary normalized lattice vertices need not be P-integral. Only the edge-generated H is guaranteed integral. Color components after choosing a coset origin; do not silently reduce every vertex of the original lattice.

The proof also gives at most three colors for the unit-distance graph on the entire additive field K by the same coset construction. It does not cover arbitrary finite-rank planar additive groups, multiple prescribed distances, or the full plane.

## B. The exact metric and symmetry invoice

Put ω²+ω+1=0 and identify `(a,b)` with a+bω, so `q=a²−ab+b²`. An index-seven sublattice is a kernel of a nonzero linear form on F7², up to scalar. There are eight candidates; exactly

    c2(a,b)=a+2b,       c4(a,b)=a+4b

are bijective on B. They are the ideals `(2−ω)` and `(3+ω)`, respectively. This enumeration is complete, not a search over a bounding box of sublattices.

For q the first nonzero ideal shell has q=7 and six vectors; the next possible shell has q≥21 because ideal norms are seven times an Eisenstein norm and the next norm after1 is3. The Voronoi hexagon H has diameter `2/sqrt3`. Its difference body2H has vertices

    ±(4/3,2/3), ±(2/3,4/3), ±(2/3,−2/3).

For the shortest ideal displacement `(2,−1)`, the nearest point of2H is `(2/3,−2/3)`, giving squared separation7/3. Rotation covers all six shortest vectors. For every later vector, the triangle inequality gives distance at least `sqrt21−2/sqrt3 > sqrt(7/3)`. Thus the minimum is global and exact, not merely a finite sampled minimum.

The cell diameter condition `<1` and intercell separation condition `>1` yield precisely

    sqrt(3/7) < h < sqrt3/2.

At h=3/4 the two values are `sqrt3/2<1` and `sqrt21/4>1`. Assign every boundary point to any one incident cell; strict inequalities make all choices safe. At either endpoint one of the strict inequalities becomes equality, so this proof cannot admit it without a separate ownership argument. The interval is sharp **for these strict two-metric inequalities**, not claimed to classify every possible boundary-colored tiling construction.

Rotation `R(a,b)=(a−b,a)` preserves each ideal and acts on colors by multipliers3 and5. Conjugation `C(a,b)=(a−b,−b)` interchanges the ideals. Reflection is not a symmetry of a single fixed chiral ideal quotient; it is an isomorphism to the conjugate quotient. Keep the chosen ideal and marked central point.

The triangular graph T is three-chromatic. Its square T² is seven-chromatic: B is a seven-clique, and either ideal quotient is a proper seven-coloring. T² has Euclidean edge lengths1,sqrt3,2, not only1. Hence this seven-clique is not a seven-chromatic planar unit-distance graph.
