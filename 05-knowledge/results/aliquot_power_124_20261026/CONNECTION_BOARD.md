More generally two cyclic orbits of periods d,e have diagonal orbit length
`lcm(d,e)` and `gcd(d,e)` relative-phase classes, since their product has de
points. Independently quotienting the two orbits would erase those phases.

## The simplex / spheroid / cuboid heuristic, tested here

The divisor carrier is literally a product of integer chains, a discrete
box, with its top corner removed. For a squarefree integer this becomes all
proper subsets: the boundary of a simplex, geometrically a sphere. This is
an exact occurrence of the user's simplex/spheroid/cuboid triple: the box
collapses to a Boolean cube, and deleting the maximal face leaves a simplex
boundary under the monomial-to-face interpretation. It does **not** identify
their face posets. With three squarefree prime coordinates, all pairs are
allowed but the triple is forbidden, which is exactly the nonflag obstruction.
The simplex boundary here comes from monomial supports as faces. It is not
the geometric boundary of the cube. Nor is it the order complex of the
proper-divisor poset including1: that order complex has a least vertex and
is a cone. Removing both1 and n before taking an order complex instead
gives the barycentric subdivision of the simplex boundary.

For 30 the three active primes give the triangle boundary:1+3X+3X² in
unweighted degree. Its geometric realization is a circle. At prime weights
2,3,5 it instead counts proper divisors and evaluates to42. The geometric
circle retains the incidence pattern; it does not retain that arithmetic
value. Repeated-prime boxes cease to be squarefree simplicial complexes.
This makes the triangle/circle interchange precise on one carrier, while
also identifying the weight and multiplicity data it forgets.

No claim that a circle is a maximal-complexity object in every dimension or
that every cuboid is combinatorially self-dual is required. Complexity and
duality need a chosen category. The useful exact object here is the divisor
downset with its prime weights, not a universal ordering of all shapes.
