## The order-eight theorem

Every cell in a directed equitable tournament partition has odd size: its internal outgoing degree is constant and equals (size−1)/2. Two specified marks are singleton cells. At order at most eight the remaining non-singleton possibilities are therefore a single directed C3, one regular five-vertex cell, or two directed C3 cells. All arcs between a non-singleton cell and a singleton are uniform, fixed by the quotient.

A regular tournament on five vertices is unique up to isomorphism. To see this without a table, let v have out-neighbours a,b with a→b and in-neighbours c,d with c→d. Degree two forces b→c,d; c already beats v,d so a→c; then d→a is forced. This specifies the whole tournament. A single C3 is likewise unique. Thus only two C3 cells X,Y can retain a choice.

Their adjacency block is regular with degree q. For q=0 or3 it is uniform. For q=1 it is the permutation matrix of a unique bijection π:X→Y. For q=2 its complement is that matrix. Retain the polarity q already present in the quotient. The bijection either preserves or reverses the cyclic orientations; denote its sign by ε=+1 or−1. Cyclic rotations on X and Y act transitively on the three bijections of each sign. Consequently **one bit ε determines this last block up to isomorphism preserving every cell and mark**. Conversely the sign cannot change under such an isomorphism. This proves the stated theorem for ALL marked tournaments of order ≤8, not just Hamiltonian-feasible pairs.
