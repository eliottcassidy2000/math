## 2. What this note adds (THM-4805)

Write `t(n) = tau(n) - 1`. A composite `n` has `s(n) >= 1 + sqrt n` (the proper divisor `n/p`), so sources of `v>1` lie below `v^2`; targets0 and1 require their separate fibres.

**Lemma 1 (shapes by number of proper divisors).** `t = 2`: `p^2`, `s = p + 1`. `t = 3`: `p^3` (`1 + p + p^2`) or `pq` (`1 + p + q`). `t = 4`: `p^4`, `s >= 15`. `t = 5`: `p^5` (`s >= 31`) or `p^2 q` (`1 + p + p^2 + q + pq >= 16`). `t >= 6`: `s >= 22` (six distinct proper divisors sum to at least 21, equality forcing `60 | n`). The least sources with `t = 4, 5` are 16 (`s = 15`) and 12 (`s = 16`).

**Theorem A' (the t = tau - 1 <= 3 regime and the Mersenne sources).** (i) Every value `2 <= v <= 14` has only sources of the three shapes `p^2`, `pq` (distinct primes), `p^3`; the first value with a four-proper-divisor source is `15 = s(16)` (the row of 1) and the first with five is `16 = s(12)` (the row of 3). Separately, `s^-1(0)={1}` and `s^-1(1)` is the set of all primes. After separating these two fibres, targets2..5 have only the square sources4 and9, from the columns of2 and3. The second handful brings the first row source `6=2*3`, the row-column coincidence8, and the first off-grid source `15=3*5`.

(ii) **CITED FINITE-EXACT:** for `1<=k<=26`, the Mersenne value `2^k-1=s(2^k)` has the single source `2^k` exactly for k2,3. This follows from S57's five single-source odd values below `10^8`; that cited range was not independently recomputed in this bounded repair. At k1 the target1 has infinitely many prime sources. The small fibres are `3 <- {4}`, `7 <- {8}`, `15 <- {16,33}`, `31 <- {32,125,161,209,221}`, `63 <- {64,177,817}`; 127 has eleven sources. **CONDITIONAL:** assuming every even integer at least8 is a sum of two distinct primes, the same iff holds for every `k>=1`. Unconditionally, the all-k classification is unproved here. Thus the claimed uniqueness of the 1-2-4 decomposition beyond `3=1+2` has precisely this finite or conditional scope.

*Proof.* (i) Lemma 1 with the two minimal sources, after separating the unit and prime fibres. (ii) The finite statement is cited from S57's list. Under distinct-prime Goldbach, for each `k>=4` a distinct-prime partition `2^k-2=p+q` supplies the odd semiprime source `pq`, different from `2^k`, with `s(pq)=2^k-1`. The exact fibres at k2,3 and the infinite prime fibre at k1 give the conditional iff. QED

**Theorem B' (row-source uniqueness and the perfect numbers).** (i) For a prime `P`, the sources of `P + 1` of the form `2^a q` (`a >= 1`, `q` an odd prime) are exactly the twin source `2(P - 2)` (when `P - 2` is prime): the row formula `s(2^a q) = (2^a - 1) q + 2^(a+1) - 1` (the guarded prime-extension formula with `C = 2^a`, [cited](aliquot_guarded_cycles_20261014e.md)) gives `(2^a - 1) q = P - 2(2^a - 1)`, so `2^a - 1 | P`; `2^a - 1 = P` would make `q < 0`. (ii) FINITE-EXACT, by the parity lemma (even sources `m <= 2N - 2`, odd sources are odd squares `q^2`, `q < N`): `s^-1(6) = {6, 25}`, `s^-1(28) = {28}`, `s^-1(496) = {496, 652}`, `s^-1(8128) = {8128, 10682, 10828}`, `s^-1(33550336) = {33550336, 44655764}` with companions `652 = 4 x 163`, `10828 = 4 x 2707`, `10682 = 2 x 7^2 x 109`, `44655764 = 4 x 1669 x 6689`. So **28 is its own only aliquot source**, the answer to the question left open by the audited uniqueness theorem ("another perfect target such as 28 is not excluded from having other source shapes"): it has none. The row source `4q` needs `q = (N - 7)/3` prime (an integer for every even perfect `N > 6`, since `N = 1 mod 3`): true for 28 (`q = 7`, giving 28 itself), 496 and 8128, false for the next four perfect numbers. (iii) Twin-value source counts: of the 205 twin values `P + 1 <= 10^4`, 115 have exactly the two twin sources (`74 <- {70, 142, 5329}` is the first with a third); in `(10^4, 10^5]`, 494 of 1019 (share 0.4848), mean 2.816 sources, maximum 11.

**Theorem C' (the two-ray carrier).** Take the rows `D(n) = {2^j n : j>=0}` and the columns `P(n) = {n^k : k>=1}` for positive bases n, including the degenerate `P(1)={1}`, as separate rays (the owner's picture; session69 keeps the carriers apart and this is the one with the two owner's "chains"). Then, as corollaries of session69's earliest-seed and row-intersection theorems and aliquot124's P1-P2: (1) a number is old (on a ray of a smaller number) iff it is even or a perfect power, and the two rays of an odd non-perfect-power meet no ray of a smaller number; (2) for `n = 2^a r`, `r` odd, `n^2` lies on `D(r^2)`, already occurs iff `r^2 < n` or `n` is a perfect power, and is a later doubling iff `a >= 1`, `r > 2^a` (session69's predicate); (3) the whole column of `2^a r` is the column of `r` shifted along the rows, `(2^a r)^k = 2^(ak) r^k`, the diagonal of the grid spanned by the row of 1 and the column of `r`: this is the microcosm/macrocosm, the single coincidence `36 = 6^2 = 4 x 9` repeated for every even number; (4) the row of an odd `n` meets an earlier column iff `n` is a perfect power (`9 -> 36 = 6^2`; `25 -> 100, 400`; `27 -> 216, 1728, 13824`): `2^j n = m^k` with `n` odd forces `n` to be a `k`-th power, and `n = b^k` gives `(2b)^k`; (5) `D(1)={1} union P(2)`, while `D(2)=P(2)` is the unique exact equality between an unrestricted doubling row and a positive-exponent power column. The segment `1,2,4` of `D(1)` below8 is the 1-2-4 decomposition of `7=s(8)`, and2 is the only prime on `D(1)`.

*Proof of (5).* If `D(n)=P(m)`, the power column must be infinite, hence `m>=2`. Its least and second elements give `n=m` and `2n=m^2`, forcing `m=n=2`. Conversely `D(2)=P(2)` directly, and adding the missing unit gives `D(1)`. This retains the positive-exponent convention used by the cited address theorems and the producer.

**Theorem D' (abc on the row of 1).** (i) For `n>=2`, `(1,s(n),n)` is a positive pairwise-coprime additive triple iff `sigma(n) = 2n - 1` (`n` almost perfect; the powers of 2 are the only known ones). The excluded n1 has `sigma(1)=1=2*1-1` but gives `(1,0,1)`, not a positive abc triple. (ii) For `k>=1`, `(1, 2^k - 1, 2^k)` is an abc hit (`rad(abc) < c`) iff `2^k - 1` is not squarefree, iff `ord_(p^2)(2) | k` for some odd prime `p` (`6, 20, 21, 110, 156, 253, ...` for `p = 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 23`): if squarefree, `rad = 2(2^k - 1) >= 2^k`; otherwise `rad(2^k - 1) <= (2^k - 1)/3 < 2^(k-1)`. The hits with `k <= 64` are `6, 12, 18, 20, 21, 24, 30, 36, 40, 42, 48, 54, 60, 63` (sympy factorisation; exactly the multiples of 6, 20, 21 below 64). The first is `1 + 63 = 64`; session69's square-later carrier has its first excess at the same triple `1 + 8 = 9` read from `n = 72`, and the row of 1 reaches that triple only through `s(8) = 7`, `7 + 1 = 8`, `8 + 1 = 9`. (iii) Census: 121 abc hits with `c <= 10^4` (94 containing an odd perfect power, 48 with a power of two as the even term, 30 with `a = 1`; exactly one term of every hit is even), 419 with `c <= 10^5` (323, 119, 63; best quality 1.5679 at `1 + 4374 = 4375`).

## 3. The crevasse analogy (ANALOGY) and the coincidences (NUMEROLOGY)

The dictionary *shape <-> strong component, floor `s(n) > sqrt n` <-> Busch floor, Goldbach partition of `v - 1` <-> factorisation of `H`* is an ANALOGY and nothing more: `s` is not multiplicative, `H` has no Goldbach shape, and session69's firewall (5 is an `H`-value; 7 and 21 are touchable; no descent mod 7) closes every literal identification tried so far.
