## The final two-count correction requires a larger operation

[Endpoint constraints, Theorem5](../session258_endpoint_seed_20261111/endpoints/PROOF.md)
proves that a one-vertex extension with H(child)−H(parent)=2 can only
have the count pair(1,3) or(3,5). Its proof uses the full nonnegative
even-path cut-capacity matrix, including capacities on nonarcs.

This strengthens the [5991 one-birth control](tournament/BIRTH_CLOSURE.md):
**no** tournament of count5991 has a one-vertex5993 extension, regardless
of the chosen source or root. Our complete cut calculation remains an
independent finite receipt for the named source, but changing only the
5991 representative cannot repair that one-birth strategy. The native
two-birth H→3H operation and changes of old arcs are outside this theorem's
scope and remain valid.

## A finite machine decides each exact ray multiplier

[The fixed-multiplier seed theorem](../session258_endpoint_seed_20261111/seed_decision/PROOF.md)
decides whether there is a selected-set unit-offset seed with

    H(B)−W_B(O)=1,     U_B(O)=q

for a **fixed** q. Positivity bounds H(B)≤q+1. A finite strong-component
alphabet and nine nonnegative endpoint-cover coordinates, capped atq+2,
then give a terminating complete search over every ordinal padding length.
It emits a literal source when successful. Its exact small classification
is limited to q1..13.

The remaining universal seed quantifier is different: for every odd c,
find some j with q=c2^j accepted, and handle all below-start targets.
Our periodic defect preparations retain an integer translation; they do
not establish those selected-set seed equations. Likewise the all-Z rays
after our root changes have constant term d', which is generally not1.
The nine-coordinate seed machine is a precise possible consumer only
after an actual selected-set interface is supplied.

Both incoming results support the same next selection requirement as this
packet: preserve the exact target and the physical endpoint constraints,
rather than only a residue, a common divisor, or the existence of a repeatable
arithmetic clock.
