# Atomic consequences of the hypothetical exact tournament spectrum

## Status and scope

This note **assumes for conditional consequences**, and does not prove, the conjecture that the directed Hamilton-path counts of finite tournaments form

    M = {positive odd integers} \ {7,21}.

The all-odd theorem for tournament path counts and the transitive ordering of strong components are standard inputs. The repository proves exclusion of 7 and 21, and strong witnesses for 49, 63 and 343, in [THM-4094](https://github.com/eliottcassidy2000/math/blob/7589463e54e20585aff6a2e68019ee4a4af582cd/01-canon/theorems/THM-4094-hamiltonian-matching-deficit-and-two-prime-lane-completeness.md). An ordinal sum X⊕Y has h(X⊕Y)=h(X)h(Y), because every Hamilton path visits all of X before Y. The corresponding decomposition of a tournament into strongly connected components is unique up to isomorphism; its component graph is a transitive tournament. A singleton is allowed as a strong component and has count 1. No claim of novelty against the literature is made. Session 159's explicit constructions establish only restricted families and modular coverage, not this axiom: https://github.com/eliottcassidy2000/math/blob/7589463e54e20585aff6a2e68019ee4a4af582cd/00-navigation/S159.md .

## 1. Complete list of multiplicative atoms

M is a multiplicative submonoid: products of positive odd numbers cannot equal 7 or 21 unless a factor is 7 or 21. Its irreducibles (atoms), meaning elements >1 not expressible as a product of two elements >1 of M, are precisely

  A = {p: p an odd prime, p≠7}
      ∪ {7p: p an odd prime, p≠3}
      ∪ {63,343}.

Proof. Each listed element is an atom. Primes are clear; a factorization of 7p would split the primes and require the forbidden factor 7 (this includes p=7). The only nontrivial divisors of 63 below it pair as 3·21 or 7·9, and those of 343 pair as 7·49, so those are atoms too.

For the converse, factor n=7^a 3^b r, with gcd(r,21)=1. If a=0, n factors into odd primes other than 7. If a is even and positive, factor n as 49^(a/2)·3^b·r. If a is odd and at least 3, factor as 343·49^((a−3)/2)·3^b·r. Finally let a=1. If b≥2, factor as 63·3^(b−2)·r. If b is 0 or 1, membership of M forces r>1; choose any prime p|r and factor as (7p)·3^b·(r/p). All factors on the right are in A after splitting r into prime factors. Thus every element of M has a factorization into the displayed atoms, and no other atoms exist. Empty products are allowed.

## 2. Equivalence to a strong-witness program

Write S for the set of path counts of strongly connected finite tournaments. Independently of the conjecture, the full spectrum is the multiplicative monoid generated by S, by the strong-component decomposition and ordinal sums. If the spectrum is M, every a∈A has a strong witness: any tournament with h=a has strong component counts in M multiplying to a, so atomicity forces one component to have count a and the rest count 1.

Conversely, suppose no tournament has count 7 or 21, and that every element of A has a strong tournament witness. Then every integer in M occurs by taking ordinal sums according to the factorizations above. The standard parity theorem excludes even counts, yielding exactly M. Thus, **relative to the standard parity theorem**, the exact spectrum conjecture is equivalent to these two statements:

* no tournament has path count 7 or 21;
* each odd prime p≠7, each 7p for odd primes p≠3, and 63 and 343 has a strong tournament witness.

In particular, the conjecture forces strong witnesses at 49, 63, 343 and at 7p for every prime p≥5. The first three are already established, as are finite prefixes of both infinite prime lanes. The still-unresolved infinite lanes are concrete, falsifiable research targets. [S150](https://github.com/eliottcassidy2000/math/blob/7589463e54e20585aff6a2e68019ee4a4af582cd/00-navigation/S150.md) reports exact witnesses for every allowed odd integer through 100001. Together with strong-atom extraction, this already yields the ordinary-prime lane for odd primes p≤100001 with p≠7 and the 7p lane for odd primes p≤14285 with p≠3. Disproving the existence of a strong witness for any listed atom would disprove the exact-spectrum conjecture. This section’s equivalence and case split are essentially the already established THM-4094 two-prime-lane theorem, with the atom language made explicit; they are not claimed as a new reduction. Since the finite carry atoms and exclusions are established there, only the two infinite lanes remain. Infinite template families are still necessary: no finite set of fixed tournament blocks, combined only by ordinal sum, can realize all M, since their counts contain only finitely many prime divisors. This does not rule out one parametrized family or other tournament operations.

## 3. A total search only under the axiom

Given n, decide arithmetically whether n is in M. If not, report that the axiom predicts no witness. If so, enumerate tournaments in increasing vertex count and, within a size, in a fixed order of edge orientations; compute h by exact subset/endpoint dynamic programming and return the first tournament whose h equals n. Under the exact-spectrum assumption this algorithm halts, and the returned witness has minimum order. This is a uniform constructive consequence, but it is exhaustive search, not a practical complexity bound, a finite template, or a proof that all targets occur. In particular, regularity of the membership language (canonical positive binary representations of odd numbers, except 111 and 10101) by itself provides no uniform tournament construction.

The stronger atomic search enumerates only strongly connected tournaments when n∈A; conditional halting follows from Section 2. Combined with integer factorization and the explicit case split in Section 1, it constructs a witness for every n∈M via ordinal sum. No bound on witness size or runtime follows from the axiom alone.

## 4. Factorization lengths and a sharp invariant

The atom factorizations are not unique. The abstract monoid M has elasticity exactly 5/2: this is the supremum of L_max(n)/L_min(n) over n∈M, where the lengths count nonunit atom factors.

For n>0 define w(n)=Ω(n/7^v7(n))+v7(n)/2, where Ω counts prime factors with multiplicity. This is additive under multiplication. Every atom has weight at least 1 and at most 5/2: prime 3 and primes other than 7 have weight 1; 7p has weight 3/2 when p≠7; 49 has weight 1; 63 has weight 5/2; and 343 has weight 3/2. Thus any factorization of n into L atoms satisfies L≤w(n)≤(5/2)L. Any two factorization lengths therefore have ratio at most 5/2. Equality occurs at

    63·63 = 49·3·3·3·3 = 3969,

with lengths 2 and 5.

Unconditionally, choose the known strong tournaments of counts 63 and 49 from THM-4094 and the directed 3-cycle of count 3. The two ordinal sums corresponding to the displayed identity have equal Hamilton-path count 3969 but different numbers of nontrivial strong components, 2 and 5, so they are non-isomorphic. The same conclusion holds for every positive power of 3969, with 2k versus 5k nontrivial strong components. This speaks about existence of different tournaments and arithmetic factorizations, not nonuniqueness of the strong decomposition of an individual tournament. Since 3, 49 and 63 are atoms of M and are actual counts, they are also atoms of the actual spectrum. Thus the actual spectrum has arithmetic elasticity at least 5/2 unconditionally; equality is conditional on the conjecture, and no upper bound for the actual spectrum follows merely from its inclusion in M.

## 5. Finite verification

`python3 check.py` independently checks the atom criterion by divisor enumeration and the constructive factorization case split for every odd n≤100001, and checks the sharp weight inequality and extremal equality on those atoms. The proof above is universal; this computation is a finite control, not evidence that the spectrum axiom is established.
