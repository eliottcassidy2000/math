# Exact all-order spectra of Moon interval chains

Status: PROVED using the KNOWN Moon product law and CITED Bang–Zsigmondy
theorem; independent mathematical and computational audit PASS. This classifies a particular physical
family for every order. It does not classify the full tournament spectrum.

## 1. Objects and inherited mechanisms

For a composition (p_1,...,p_k) with p_i>=2, the interval chain IC(p) has
vertices 0,...,N, N=sum p_i. All arcs point from smaller to larger index,
except the back arc from the right endpoint to the left endpoint of each
consecutive block of length p_i. Consecutive blocks share their endpoint.
This is a strong tournament when k>=1. We also allow the empty composition,
giving a singleton of order 1 and H=1, as an explicit algebraic boundary.

Moon's known product law is

    H(IC(p)) = product_i (2^(p_i-1)+1).               (1)

It is credited to Moon (1972), page 102, in THM-4744,
01-canon/theorems/THM-4744-interval-chains-attain-busch-and-are-the-extremal-strong-tournaments-through-order-twelve.md.
The repository contains a full restriction/merge proof. We inherit that
physical construction and proof; this is not a new product-law claim.
The earlier note

sections 3 and 7 used multiplicative independence at distinct odd prime indices.
Here we use the full set of indices and classify every scalar collision.

Write q_m=2^m+1 for m>=1; part p contributes q_(p-1) with weight p=m+1.
The exceptional identity is q_3=9=q_1^2, whose weights agree:4=2+2.

## 2. Full multiplicative independence after the one exception

The needed Bang–Zsigmondy statement is: for every integer r>=2 other than 6,
2^r-1 has a prime divisor which divides no 2^s-1 for 1<=s<r. At r=6 this
fails. For a general two-base formulation there is also an exponent-two
exception when the base sum is a power of two; it does not apply to bases 2,1.

For each m>=1 with m!=3, apply the theorem at r=2m. A primitive prime l_m
has multiplicative order 2m for the residue 2. It divides q_m: it divides
(2^m-1)(2^m+1) and cannot divide the first factor. For every j<m it does not
divide q_j, since q_j divides 2^(2j)-1 and 2j<2m.

Therefore the integers q_m with m!=3 are multiplicatively independent.
If two finite products were equal, cancel the common powers first and take
the largest remaining index m. The prime l_m divides the side with q_m and
no factor on the other side, a contradiction. No assertion that q_m are
prime, or pairwise coprime, is being made.

Reference checked directly: S. P. Glasby, F. Lubeck, A. C. Niemeyer and
C. E. Praeger, *Primitive prime divisors and the nth cyclotomic polynomial*,
J. Aust. Math. Soc. 102 (2017), 122–135, page 123, introduction,
https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788715000269 . The paper recalls the needed
Bang–Zsigmondy theorem. Our base-two specialization and the deduction of
multiplicative independence are as above. The primitive-divisor theorem
is cited, not proved or inferred from the finite checks.

## 3. Complete scalar collision classification and exact chain spectrum

Replace every part 4 by two parts 2, then sort the parts. Call the resulting
partition the canonical partition. Its allowed parts are

    2,3,5,6,7,...                                    (2)

and its total weight is unchanged. By section2, two interval chains have
the same H if and only if their canonical partitions agree. Equivalently,
all scalar collisions are generated by permuting parts and replacing a 4
with two 2s or the reverse. In particular H determines the total order of
an interval chain, even though different compositions at that order can
have the same H.

For n>=1 the exact interval-chain count support is consequently

    C_n = { product_(w>=2,w!=4) (2^(w-1)+1)^a_w :
            a_w>=0 and sum w*a_w=n-1 }.              (3)

At n=1 this includes the singleton count 1; at n=2 it is empty. For n>=3
all witnesses in this definition are strong. If p(t) denotes the ordinary
integer partition number, with p(0)=1 and p(t)=0 for t<0, then

    |C_n| = p(n-1)-p(n-2)-p(n-5)+p(n-6).            (4)

Indeed the generating function for partitions with parts excluding 1 and 4
is (1-z)(1-z^4) times the ordinary partition generating function P(z).

## 4. Source padding, exact constructed-family size, and growth

Let D_n be the union of these chain count supports at orders 1 through n.
Each is physically realized at order n by adding universal-source singleton
vertices. The sets C_j are pairwise disjoint because a chain count determines
its order. The padding length for a chosen chain count is uniquely fixed;
padding is not an extra multiplicity variable at fixed n. Thus

    D_n is a subset of S_n,
    |D_n| = p(n-1)-p(n-5).                           (5)

This is an exact cardinality for the constructed family, and a lower bound
for the full tournament spectrum, not an equality with S_n. The generating
function calculation is (1-z)^(-1)(1-z)(1-z^4)P(z)=(1-z^4)P(z).

An elementary all-order growth bound needs no partition asymptotic. For M>=3,
choose any subset of the M-1 indices 1<=m<=M other than 3. Their total maximum
weight is

    W_M=M(M+3)/2-4.

Whenever n>=1+W_M, all 2^(M-1) subset products belong to D_n and are distinct.
Consequently log|S_n| >= (log2)*sqrt(2n)-O(1) as n grows. This is a lower bound
only, not an asymptotic equality. No cancellation of approximate partition
asymptotics is used.

## 5. Polynomial-time recognition and unique chain size

There is an exact factorization-free recognition algorithm for the infinite
monoid generated by q_m, m!=3. Given h>=1, scan m downward from
floor(log_2(h-1)) to 1, skipping 3, and divide the current remainder by q_m as
many times as possible, recording those exponents. Handle h=1 separately
as the empty product. Use integer bit lengths, not floating logs.

If h is a monoid element, a q_m above its largest actual generator cannot
divide it: the primitive prime l_m occurs in none of the lower generators.
After larger generators are peeled off, the same argument identifies exactly
the exponent at the current m. Induction proves greedy recognition. For an
arbitrary input, a remainder of 1 supplies the recorded product certificate;
a nonunit remainder therefore certifies nonmembership. No primality or
integer factorization oracle is used by the algorithm.

The unique chain order is 1+sum(m+1)*a_m. Thus h belongs to C_n precisely when
recognition succeeds and this order equals n; it belongs to D_n precisely
when recognition succeeds and this order is at most n. With binary input
length L, there are O(L) failed divisions and O(L) successful divisions,
each involving O(L)-bit integers. The computed weight is at most 2 log_2 h, since m+1<=2m<=2 log_2 q_m,
so its output length is controlled. Schoolbook arithmetic gives O(L^3) bit
time, plus the cost of comparing the computed size with n. This is a
polynomial-time solution for this family, not for arbitrary tournaments.

## 6. Hostiles, controls, and scope

- Keeping m=3 as an independent generator would count9 twice and break the
  cardinality formulas. Its cost-preserving elimination is essential.
- The q_m are not pairwise coprime: q_1=3 divides q_5=33. The proof uses a
  largest-index primitive prime, not disjoint prime supports.
- Permuting parts changes the tournament in general but not its scalar H.
  This result classifies count collisions, not graph isomorphism.
- Source padding is not strong padding. The strong exact family is C_n;
  D_n contains padded tournaments which may be nonstrong.
- The full spectrum still has additional counts, already 11 at order 5.
- Unique chain order is not the unrestricted minimum: count 15 has chain
  order 6 (parts 2,3), but a general 5-vertex tournament already realizes it.

Finite checks support the implementation; the all-order results use the
