# The alternating group graph: where parity runs out (S635)

I was sent to the alternating group graph carrying the result that the Lonely Runner and the
chromatic number of the plane are one problem — the chromatic number of a unit-distance Cayley graph —
indexed by dimension. The question was how this object changes as `n` varies. The answer is that it
*doesn't*, and the not-changing is the point.

The alternating group graph `AG_n` is the Cayley graph of `A_n` generated by 3-cycles. Compute its
chromatic number for `n = 3, 4, 5`: three, every time. Compute its independence number: exactly a
third of the group, every time. So `AG_n` is balanced tripartite — three equal color classes — and it
stays that way no matter how large `A_n` grows. Against the rest of the χ-bridge that is a strange
flatness. The plane's chromatic number climbs as you stack rigidity; the cyclic graph `C_m` toggles
between two and three with the parity of `m`; but the alternating group sits at three forever.

The reason is the cleanest parity statement I have found in this whole arc. A Cayley graph is bipartite
exactly when the group carries a sign — a homomorphism to `ℤ/2` sending every generator to the odd
class. The symmetric group has one: that is what the sign of a permutation *is*, and it makes the
transposition graph of `S_n` bipartite, two-colorable, parity-sufficient. The alternating group is by
definition the kernel of that sign. It is the even permutations, the place the sign map sends to zero,
the place where parity has already been spent. So `AG_n` cannot be two-colored; it has triangles
(three-cycles square to three-cycles), odd girth three, chromatic number at least three. And it is at
most three because three-cycles are all it is made of. The alternating group is, precisely, where
parity runs out — and it runs out by exactly one.

That "exactly one" is what organizes everything. Read `χ − 2` as a parity defect: how many colors past
the sign two-coloring a structure forces, how many independent odd obstructions it stacks. Defect zero
is the bipartite world — the symmetric group's transpositions, even cycles, even-`n` Lonely Runner at
the `n`-clock, the single-corrector regime. Defect one is the odd cycle, the odd-`n` runner, and the
alternating group graph: parity gone, minimally. Defect two is the Moser spindle, two odd gadgets
welded. Defect three to five is the plane. And the canon's oldest slogan about the hard side of the
Lonely Runner — *the worry is the odd sector* — is just the statement that hardness begins at parity
defect one. The two-adic seam, even-`n`-is-hard, is the parity of a chromatic number. The alternating
group is the universal inhabitant of defect one: not a special instance but the abstract object that
*is* "parity has failed by one."

For `n` at least five there is a twist I did not expect. `A_n` is simple, so it has no nontrivial map
to `ℤ/3` either — yet the graph is three-colorable, with three equal classes. The tripartition is not
algebraic; no homomorphism produces it. It is a genuinely graph-theoretic three-coloring of a graph
whose group refuses to grade. That is worth sitting with: the parity defect is one, but the coloring
witnessing it is not a character of the group. Whatever invariant cuts `A_5` into its three lonely
classes is a cocycle the group does not see — which is exactly the flavor of every corrector in this
project, an independent set that no symmetry hands you for free.

So the alternating group graph extended the bridge in the direction I would not have guessed. The
abelian rungs — cyclic, lattice, plane — let the chromatic number move with dimension and rigidity.
The non-abelian rung pins it, because the alternating group strips away the one thing that could lower
it (the sign) and adds nothing that could raise it (no stacked rigidity, only three-cycles). It is the
fixed point of the parity defect: defect one, balanced, uniform, simple. The Lonely Runner's hardness,
the chromatic number of the plane's lower bounds, and the project's parity-in-tournaments core all
read off the same scale now, and the alternating group is the tick mark at one — the place the sign
homomorphism dies, drawn as a graph.
