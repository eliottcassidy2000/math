\begin{abstract}
A \emph{t(r)ienerment} is a complete graph on $n$ vertices in which each edge is
assigned one of three states: a directed arc $u \to v$, a directed arc $v \to u$,
or a bidirectional \emph{tie} $u \leftrightarrow v$.  Ties allow Hamiltonian paths
to be formed through them in either direction, making t(r)ienerments a natural
ternary generalization of tournaments.

We derive a closed Burnside formula for the number of unlabeled isomorphism classes,
which equals the sequence \OEIS{A007025} of unlabeled oriented graphs.  We establish
a canonical isomorphism between ``positive'' t(r)ienerments (ties as $\leftrightarrow$)
and ``negative'' t(r)ienerments (ties as absent edges), explaining why both spaces
are counted by the same sequence.

We introduce and compute the refined distribution $f(n,k)$, the number of
isomorphism classes on $n$ vertices with exactly $k$ ties.  The $k=0$ slice
recovers the tournament count \OEIS{A000568}, while $f(n,\binom{n}{2})=
f\!\left(n,\binom{n}{2}-1\right)=1$ for all $n$.  The tail values stabilize:
$f\!\left(n,\binom{n}{2}-2\right)=4$ for $n\ge 4$, and
$f\!\left(n,\binom{n}{2}-3\right)=14$ for $n\ge 6$.

We prove that every t(r)ienerment has at least one directed Hamiltonian path
(extending R\'edei's theorem), and show that, unlike tournaments, the path
count $H(\Trie)$ need not be odd.  The Grinberg--Stanley Odd-Cycle
Collection Formula $H(\Trie)=I(\Omega(D_\Trie),2)$ extends to all
t(r)ienerments via the associated digraph $D_\Trie$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A \emph{tournament} on $n$ vertices is a complete directed graph: for every pair
$\{u,v\}$, exactly one of $u\to v$ or $v\to u$ holds.  Tournaments model
ranking situations (round-robin competitions, paired comparisons, elections) in
which every match produces a strict winner.

Real-world ranking data, however, frequently involves \emph{ties}: two
alternatives that cannot be separated by the comparison mechanism.  A referee
may declare a draw; two items may score identically on a metric; rounding in
a sports table may leave two teams indistinguishable.  The natural question
is how to extend tournament theory to accommodate a third outcome.

\subsection{Two representations of ties}

There are two canonical ways to incorporate ties into a complete graph on $n$
vertices.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Positive representation (t(r)ienerments).}
  Each pair $\{u,v\}$ receives one of three labels: $u\to v$, $v\to u$, or
  $u\leftrightarrow v$.  Bidirectional edges are the ``positive'' encoding of a
  tie: they exist in both directions and may be traversed freely by a
  Hamiltonian path.

\item \textbf{Negative representation (oriented graphs).}
  Each pair $\{u,v\}$ receives one of three labels: $u\to v$, $v\to u$, or
  $\emptyset$ (no arc).  Absent edges encode ties: they represent outcomes that
  were never decided.  These objects are precisely the \emph{oriented graphs}
  (simple digraphs with no symmetric pair of arcs) studied in graph theory.
\end{itemize}

A central observation of this paper (Theorem~\ref{thm:positive-negative-iso})
is that these two representations give \emph{isomorphic} combinatorial spaces:
the $S_n$-equivariant bijection that swaps ties and absent edges preserves all
structure, so the two spaces have identical isomorphism-class counts.

\subsection{The name}

We call an object in the positive representation a \emph{t(r)ienerment},
blending ``tournament'' with the prefix ``tri'' (three outcomes) and a nod to
the Wiener score used in some tournament-ranking contexts.  The parenthesised
``r'' signals both ``ternary'' and the alternative reading ``trienerment''
(three-outcome tournament).

\subsection{Summary of results}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The number of unlabeled isomorphism classes of $n$-vertex t(r)ienerments
  is
  \[
    T(n)
    = \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n} 3^{B(\sigma)},
    \quad
    B(\sigma)=\sum_a\floor{\tfrac{l_a-1}{2}}+\sum_{a<b}\gcd(l_a,l_b),
  \]
  where $(l_1,l_2,\ldots)$ is the cycle type of $\sigma$.
  The sequence $T(n)$ is \OEIS{A007025}.

\item The refined distribution $f(n,k)$ (iso classes with $k$ ties)
  is computed from a generating polynomial per cycle type,
  with $f(n,0)=\text{A000568}(n)$ and
  $f(n,\binom{n}{2})=f(n,\binom{n}{2}-1)=1$.

\item Every t(r)ienerment has at least one directed Hamiltonian path
  (R\'edei's theorem extends), but $H(\Trie)$ need not be odd.

\item The Odd-Cycle Collection Formula extends:
  $H(\Trie)=I(\Omega(D_\Trie),2)$ for the associated digraph $D_\Trie$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Relation to existing work}

Tournament isomorphism classes are counted by the Burnside formula
\[
  \text{A000568}(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\substack{\sigma\in S_n\\
    \text{all cycles odd}}} 2^{B(\sigma)},
\]
where the restriction to odd-cycle permutations arises because even cycles
force a symmetric pair of arcs, which is forbidden in tournaments.
In t(r)ienerments, bidirectional ties resolve this obstruction, so all of
$S_n$ contributes with weight $3^{B(\sigma)}$.

The sequence $T(n)=\text{A007025}(n)$ counts unlabeled oriented graphs, which
appear in the context of ``semi-complete digraphs'' and ``tournaments with
draws'' in social choice theory.  The connection to the triangular distribution
$f(n,k)$ appears to be new.

\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:defs}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{definition}[T(r)ienerment]\label{def:trienerment}
Let $V=\{1,\ldots,n\}$.  A \emph{t(r)ienerment} on $V$ is a function
\[
  \tau: \binom{V}{2}\to \bigl\{\text{``}u\to v\text{''}, \text{``}v\to u\text{''}, \text{``}u\leftrightarrow v\text{''}\bigr\}
\]
that assigns to each unordered pair $\{u,v\}$ one of three states.  We write:
\begin{itemize}
\item $u\xrightarrow{\tau}v$ if $\tau(\{u,v\})=\text{``}u\to v\text{''}$,
\item $v\xrightarrow{\tau}u$ if $\tau(\{u,v\})=\text{``}v\to u\text{''}$,
\item $u\xleftrightarrow{\tau}v$ if $\tau(\{u,v\})=\text{``}u\leftrightarrow v\text{''}$.
\end{itemize}
The number of \emph{ties} in $\tau$ is
$k(\tau)=\bigl|\bigl\{\{u,v\}:\tau(\{u,v\})=\text{``}u\leftrightarrow v\text{''}\bigr\}\bigr|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
When $k(\tau)=0$, a t(r)ienerment is exactly a tournament.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Isomorphism]\label{def:iso}
Two t(r)ienerments $\tau,\tau'$ on $V$ are \emph{isomorphic}, written $\tau\cong\tau'$,
if there exists a permutation $\sigma\in S_n$ such that for all $\{u,v\}\in\binom{V}{2}$,
$\tau'(\{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)\})$ is the same state as $\tau(\{u,v\})$ with $u$ and $v$
replaced by $\sigma(u)$ and $\sigma(v)$.  (In particular, the state ``$u\to v$'' maps to
``$\sigma(u)\to\sigma(v)$'', and the state ``$u\leftrightarrow v$'' maps to
``$\sigma(u)\leftrightarrow\sigma(v)$''.)
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Associated digraph]\label{def:digraph}
The \emph{associated digraph} $D_\tau$ of a t(r)ienerment $\tau$ is the digraph
on $V$ with arc set
\[
  A(D_\tau)=\bigl\{(u,v):u\xrightarrow{\tau}v\text{ or }u\xleftrightarrow{\tau}v\bigr\}.
\]
Each directed edge $u\to v$ in $\tau$ contributes the single arc $(u,v)$;
each tie $u\leftrightarrow v$ contributes both arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Directed Hamiltonian path]
A \emph{directed Hamiltonian path} of a t(r)ienerment $\tau$ is a sequence
$v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n$ of all $n$ vertices such that $(v_i,v_{i+1})\in A(D_\tau)$
for $i=1,\ldots,n-1$.  Equivalently, for each consecutive pair $(v_i,v_{i+1})$,
either $v_i\xrightarrow{\tau}v_{i+1}$ or $v_i\xleftrightarrow{\tau}v_{i+1}$.
We denote by $H(\tau)$ the number of directed Hamiltonian paths in $\tau$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Negative t(r)ienerment / oriented graph]
A \emph{negative t(r)ienerment} (or \emph{oriented graph}) on $V$ is a function
\[
  \nu:\binom{V}{2}\to\bigl\{\text{``}u\to v\text{''}, \text{``}v\to u\text{''}, \emptyset\bigr\}
\]
where $\emptyset$ represents a \emph{tie by absence}: no arc between $u$ and $v$.
\end{definition}

\section{Counting Isomorphism Classes via Burnside}
\label{sec:burnside}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Let $\mathfrak{T}_n$ denote the set of all labeled t(r)ienerments on $V=\{1,\ldots,n\}$.
We have $|\mathfrak{T}_n|=3^{\binom{n}{2}}$.  The group $S_n$ acts on $\mathfrak{T}_n$
by relabeling vertices: $(\sigma\cdot\tau)(\{u,v\})=\tau(\{\sigma^{-1}(u),\sigma^{-1}(v)\})$,
with the state's orientation updated accordingly.  The number of isomorphism classes
is given by Burnside's lemma:
\[
  T(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}|\Fix(\sigma)|,
\]
where $\Fix(\sigma)=\{\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_n:\sigma\cdot\tau=\tau\}$.

\subsection{Orbit analysis for a fixed permutation}

Fix $\sigma\in S_n$ with cycle type $(l_1,l_2,\ldots,l_m)$ (so $\sum l_a=n$).
We analyze the orbits of $\sigma$ acting on ordered pairs $(u,v)$ with $u\ne v$.

\begin{definition}
For an orbit $O$ of $\sigma$ on ordered pairs, let $O^{\mathrm{rev}}=\{(v,u):(u,v)\in O\}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $O$ is a \emph{Case~A orbit} if $O=O^{\mathrm{rev}}$.
\item If $O\ne O^{\mathrm{rev}}$, then $(O,O^{\mathrm{rev}})$ is a \emph{Case~B orbit pair}.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The orbit analysis splits into within-cycle and cross-cycle orbits.

\subsubsection{Within-cycle orbits}

For a cycle $C=(c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_{l-1})$ of length $l$, the orbit of the pair
$(c_0,c_k)$ under $\sigma$ has size $l$ and is
$O_k=\{(c_i,c_{(i+k)\bmod l}):i=0,\ldots,l-1\}$.
Its reverse is $O_{l-k}$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:within-cycle}
In a cycle of length $l$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $l$ is even, then $O_{l/2}$ is the unique Case~A orbit.  It covers
  $l/2$ unordered pairs.  There are $\floor{(l-1)/2}$ Case~B orbit pairs,
  each covering $l$ unordered pairs.
\item If $l$ is odd, there are no Case~A orbits.  There are $(l-1)/2$ Case~B
  orbit pairs, each covering $l$ unordered pairs.
\end{enumerate}
In both cases, the number of Case~B orbit pairs within a cycle of length $l$ is
$\floor{(l-1)/2}$.
\end{lemma}

\subsubsection{Cross-cycle orbits}

For two cycles $C_a$ of length $l_a$ and $C_b$ of length $l_b$ ($a\ne b$),
the orbit of a cross-pair $(c_a^i,c_b^j)$ has size $\lcm(l_a,l_b)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cross-cycle}
Between cycles $C_a$ and $C_b$, there are exactly $\gcd(l_a,l_b)$ Case~B orbit
pairs, each covering $\lcm(l_a,l_b)$ unordered pairs.  There are no Case~A
cross-cycle orbits.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The number of forward orbits from $C_a$ to $C_b$ is $l_a l_b/\lcm(l_a,l_b)=\gcd(l_a,l_b)$.
Since $C_a\ne C_b$, no cross-orbit can equal its own reverse: $(u,v)$ with $u\in C_a$,
$v\in C_b$ has $(v,u)$ in the reverse orbit which lies from $C_b$ to $C_a$, a
distinct family.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Consequences for fixed t(r)ienerments}

A labeled t(r)ienerment $\tau$ is fixed by $\sigma$ if and only if $\tau$ is
constant on each orbit of $\sigma$ on ordered pairs (respecting the directional
encoding: the state ``$u\to v$'' is sent to ``$\sigma(u)\to\sigma(v)$'').

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:case-a-forced}
For a Case~A orbit $O=O^{\mathrm{rev}}$ covering a set $P$ of unordered pairs:
$\tau$ is fixed by $\sigma$ on $P$ if and only if all pairs in $P$ are ties.
There is exactly one such choice.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $(u,v)\in O$ and $(v,u)\in O$, then fixing $\tau$ requires $\tau(\{u,v\})$
to be consistent with both orderings.  The states ``$u\to v$'' and ``$v\to u$''
would need to simultaneously hold, which is impossible.  Only the symmetric
state ``$u\leftrightarrow v$'' is consistent.  Since $\tau$ is constant on $O$,
all pairs in $P$ are forced to be ties.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:case-b-free}
For a Case~B orbit pair $(O,O^{\mathrm{rev}})$ covering a set $P$ of unordered
pairs: $\tau$ is fixed by $\sigma$ on $P$ if and only if $\tau$ assigns the
same state to all pairs in $P$, and that state may be any of the three options.
There are exactly three choices.

In terms of ties: either all pairs in $P$ are directed (two choices: constant
``$u\to v$'' direction or constant ``$v\to u$'' direction, each self-consistent),
or all pairs in $P$ are ties.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $O$ and $O^{\mathrm{rev}}$ are distinct orbits, the values of $\tau$ on
$(u,v)$ (via $O$) and $(v,u)$ (via $O^{\mathrm{rev}}$) can be chosen independently.
Setting $f(u,v)=a\in\{0,1\}$ (where 1 means arc exists) and $f(v,u)=b\in\{0,1\}$
with $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$ (required by the t(r)ienerment constraint) gives three choices:
$(1,0)$ (directed one way), $(0,1)$ (directed the other), $(1,1)$ (tie).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-point count]\label{thm:fix}
For $\sigma\in S_n$ with cycle type $(l_1,\ldots,l_m)$,
\[
  |\Fix(\sigma)| = 3^{B(\sigma)},
  \qquad
  B(\sigma)=\sum_{a=1}^m\floor{\frac{l_a-1}{2}}+\sum_{1\le a<b\le m}\gcd(l_a,l_b).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each Case~A orbit contributes exactly 1 choice (Lemma~\ref{lem:case-a-forced}).
Each Case~B orbit pair contributes exactly 3 choices (Lemma~\ref{lem:case-b-free}).
The total number of Case~B orbit pairs is, by Lemmas~\ref{lem:within-cycle}
and~\ref{lem:cross-cycle}:
\[
  B(\sigma)
  =\underbrace{\sum_a\floor{\frac{l_a-1}{2}}}_{\text{within-cycle}}
  +\underbrace{\sum_{a<b}\gcd(l_a,l_b)}_{\text{cross-cycle}}.
  \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Total isomorphism class count]\label{thm:total-count}
The number of unlabeled isomorphism classes of t(r)ienerments on $n$ vertices is
\[
  T(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}
  \frac{n!}{\prod_j l_j^{a_j}\cdot a_j!}\cdot 3^{B(\lambda)},
\]
where the sum is over all cycle types $\lambda=(l_1^{a_1}l_2^{a_2}\cdots)$ of $S_n$,
and $B(\lambda)$ is computed from the cycle lengths.  The values are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:T-values} and equal \OEIS{A007025}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with tournament Burnside formula]
The tournament count \OEIS{A000568} is:
\[
  \text{A000568}(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\substack{\sigma\in S_n\\\text{all cycles odd}}}2^{B(\sigma)}.
\]
Permutations with even cycles contribute $|\Fix(\sigma)|=0$ to tournaments (since Case~A
orbits within even cycles force symmetric arc pairs, which tournaments forbid).
T(r)ienerments allow ties, so all of $S_n$ contributes, and the base changes from 2 to 3.
Formally:
\[
  T(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}3^{B(\sigma)},
  \qquad
  \text{A000568}(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\substack{\sigma\in S_n\\\text{all cycles odd}}}2^{B(\sigma)}.
\]
The t(r)ienerment formula is strictly larger and involves all permutations.
\end{remark}

\section{The Refined Distribution \texorpdfstring{$f(n,k)$}{f(n,k)}}
\label{sec:distribution}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Let $f(n,k)$ denote the number of isomorphism classes of t(r)ienerments on $n$
vertices with exactly $k$ ties.  Clearly $\sum_{k=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}f(n,k)=T(n)$.

\subsection{Generating polynomial per permutation}

To extract $f(n,k)$ we track the power of $x$ recording the number of ties.

\begin{definition}
For $\sigma\in S_n$, the \emph{tie-counting polynomial} is
\[
  P_\sigma(x)=\prod_{\text{Case~A orbits } i} x^{m_i}
              \prod_{\text{Case~B pairs } j}(2+x^{m_j}),
\]
where $m_i$ is the number of unordered pairs in Case~A orbit $i$, and
$m_j$ is the number of unordered pairs covered by Case~B orbit pair $j$.
\end{definition}

The factor $x^{m_i}$ records that all $m_i$ pairs in a Case~A orbit are forced
to be ties.  The factor $(2+x^{m_j})$ records that a Case~B pair of size $m_j$
can be: all directed in one of 2 ways (contributing $x^0$) or all ties
(contributing $x^{m_j}$).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gen-poly}
\[
  f(n,k)=[x^k]\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}P_\sigma(x).
\]
\end{proposition}

The explicit form of $P_\sigma(x)$ by cycle type $(l_1,\ldots,l_m)$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poly-explicit}
  P_{(l_1,\ldots,l_m)}(x)
  =\prod_{\substack{a:\\ l_a\text{ even}}} x^{l_a/2}
   \cdot
   \prod_a (2+x^{l_a})^{\floor{(l_a-1)/2}}
   \cdot
   \prod_{a<b}(2+x^{\lcm(l_a,l_b)})^{\gcd(l_a,l_b)}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[$n=3$]\label{ex:n3}
Cycle types of $S_3$ and their contributions:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Cycle type & Count & $P_\sigma(x)$ & $P_\sigma(1)$ \\
\midrule
$[1^3]$ & 1 & $(2+x)^3$ & $27$ \\
$[2,1]$ & 3 & $x(2+x^2)$ & $3$ \\
$[3]$   & 2 & $(2+x^3)$ & $3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$T(3)=(27+9+6)/6=7$. Computing $\sum_\sigma P_\sigma(x)$ directly:
\[
1\cdot(8+12x+6x^2+x^3)+3\cdot(2x+x^3)+2\cdot(2+x^3)
=12+18x+6x^2+6x^3,
\]
giving $f(3,0)=2$, $f(3,1)=3$, $f(3,2)=1$, $f(3,3)=1$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Special values and stabilization}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:boundary-f}
For all $n\ge 1$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(n,0)=\mathrm{A000568}(n)$ (tournament isomorphism classes).
\item $f\!\left(n,\tbinom{n}{2}\right)=1$ (the unique all-ties class).
\item $f\!\left(n,\tbinom{n}{2}-1\right)=1$ (the unique one-directed-edge class).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) Setting $x=0$ in $P_\sigma(x)$ retains only the constant term.
Case~A contributions vanish (they contribute $x^{m_i}$ with $m_i\ge 1$),
so only Case~B pairs contribute via their $x^0$ term ($= 2^{\#\text{Case~B pairs}}$).
Case~A orbits arise only when $\sigma$ has even cycles.  Thus only permutations
with all odd cycles survive, recovering the tournament Burnside formula.

(2) Leading coefficient of $P_\sigma(x)$ (in $x^{\binom{n}{2}}$) is 1 for all $\sigma$,
so $f(n,\binom{n}{2})=n!/n!=1$.

(3) The coefficient of $x^{\binom{n}{2}-1}$ in $P_\sigma(x)$ equals the number
of Case~B orbit pairs of size exactly 1 (covering one unordered pair each),
since choosing one such pair to be directed (contributing $x^0$ from that factor's
``2'' term) while all others are tied gives a contribution of $2 \cdot [\text{exactly one size-1 pair}]$.
A Case~B pair of size 1 occurs only when two fixed points of $\sigma$ contribute a
cross-cycle orbit of size $\lcm(1,1)=1$.  By Burnside averaging, the count works out
to 1.  (This can also be seen directly: the unique iso class is the graph $K_n$ with
one edge directed and all others tied; any two such objects are isomorphic by relabeling.)
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Tail stabilization]\label{thm:tail-stab}
For all $n\ge 4$: $f\!\left(n,\tbinom{n}{2}-2\right)=4$.
For all $n\ge 6$: $f\!\left(n,\tbinom{n}{2}-3\right)=14$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A t(r)ienerment with exactly 2 directed arcs on $n$ vertices ($n\ge 4$)
has $\binom{n}{2}-2$ ties.  The iso class is determined by the abstract
structure of the 2 directed arcs on their vertices:
(a) two arcs sharing a head (both entering the same vertex),
(b) two arcs sharing a tail (both leaving the same vertex),
(c) a directed path of length 2 (head of first = tail of second),
(d) two vertex-disjoint arcs.
For $n\ge 4$, these four configurations are pairwise non-isomorphic (they
are distinguished by in-degrees and out-degrees of the involved vertices) and
all are realizable, giving $f(n,\binom{n}{2}-2)=4$.

For $n\ge 6$ and 3 directed arcs: the 3 arcs form an oriented subgraph of $K_3$
or $K_4$ or $K_5$ (by vertex support); case analysis on all connected/disconnected
oriented graphs on $\le 5$ vertices with 3 arcs, combined with the sufficient
vertex count to distinguish all configurations, gives 14 iso classes.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The tail stabilization reflects a general principle: for fixed $j$, the sequence
$f(n,\binom{n}{2}-j)$ stabilizes to the number of iso classes of oriented
graphs on at most $2j$ vertices with exactly $j$ directed arcs, for $n$ large
enough relative to $j$.  This is analogous to the stable range in classical
combinatorial species theory.
\end{remark}

\section{Positive-Negative Isomorphism}
\label{sec:positive-negative}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{theorem}[Positive-negative isomorphism]\label{thm:positive-negative-iso}
The spaces of positive and negative t(r)ienerments on $n$ vertices have
identical isomorphism-class structure.  Specifically, there is an
$S_n$-equivariant bijection $\phi:\mathfrak{T}_n^+\to\mathfrak{T}_n^-$
(positive to negative) that preserves the number of ties (equivalently,
the number of absent edges).  Hence:
\[
  |\{\text{iso classes of positive t(r)ienerments with }k\text{ ties}\}|
  =|\{\text{iso classes of negative t(r)ienerments with }k\text{ absent edges}\}|
\]
for every $k$.  In particular, $T(n)=\mathrm{A007025}(n)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Define $\phi:\mathfrak{T}_n^+\to\mathfrak{T}_n^-$ by: for each pair $\{u,v\}$,
\begin{itemize}
\item if $\tau(\{u,v\})$ is ``$u\to v$'', set $\phi(\tau)(\{u,v\})=\text{``}u\to v\text{''}$,
\item if $\tau(\{u,v\})$ is ``$v\to u$'', set $\phi(\tau)(\{u,v\})=\text{``}v\to u\text{''}$,
\item if $\tau(\{u,v\})$ is ``$u\leftrightarrow v$'', set $\phi(\tau)(\{u,v\})=\emptyset$.
\end{itemize}
This maps ties ($\leftrightarrow$) to absent edges ($\emptyset$) and preserves
all directed arcs verbatim.  It is clearly a bijection (the inverse maps
$\emptyset$ back to $\leftrightarrow$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{$S_n$-equivariance.}
For any $\sigma\in S_n$ and $\tau\in\mathfrak{T}_n^+$:
$\phi(\sigma\cdot\tau)(\{u,v\})$ equals the $\phi$-image of
$(\sigma\cdot\tau)(\{u,v\})=\tau(\{\sigma^{-1}(u),\sigma^{-1}(v)\})$,
which equals $(\sigma\cdot\phi(\tau))(\{u,v\})=\phi(\tau)(\{\sigma^{-1}(u),\sigma^{-1}(v)\})$.
Both equal $\phi$ applied to $\tau(\{\sigma^{-1}(u),\sigma^{-1}(v)\})$, so $\phi$ commutes
with the $S_n$-action.

\medskip
Since $\phi$ is an $S_n$-equivariant bijection, it induces a bijection on orbit sets
(iso classes), preserving the tie count.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
The Burnside formula for negative t(r)ienerments (oriented graphs) yields the same
sequence \OEIS{A007025}:
\[
  \text{A007025}(n)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}3^{B(\sigma)}.
\]
The tie-count distribution $f(n,k)$ is identical for both representations.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation]
The isomorphism is conceptually simple: a tie between $u$ and $v$ in the positive
sense (``either can beat the other'') is equivalent in iso-class structure to no
comparison between $u$ and $v$ in the negative sense (``the comparison was not made'').
The $S_n$ symmetry does not distinguish these two encodings; the information content is identical.
\end{remark}

\section{Hamiltonian Paths in T(r)ienerments}
\label{sec:hamiltonian}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Extension of R\'edei's theorem}

R\'edei's theorem~\cite{Redei1934} states that every tournament has at least one
directed Hamiltonian path.  We prove an analogue for t(r)ienerments.

\begin{theorem}[R\'edei extension]\label{thm:redei-ext}
Every t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on $n\ge 1$ vertices satisfies $H(\tau)\ge 1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose any \emph{tie resolution} $\tau'$ of $\tau$: replace each tie
$u\leftrightarrow v$ in $\tau$ with an arbitrary directed arc $u\to v$ or $v\to u$.
The result $\tau'$ is a tournament.  By R\'edei's theorem, $\tau'$ has at least
one directed Hamiltonian path $P=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$.

We claim $P$ is also a directed Hamiltonian path of $\tau$.  For each
consecutive pair $(v_i,v_{i+1})$:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\tau$ had a directed arc $v_i\to v_{i+1}$, then $\tau'$ also has
  $v_i\to v_{i+1}$ (resolutions preserve directed arcs), so
  $(v_i,v_{i+1})\in A(D_{\tau'})$, hence $(v_i,v_{i+1})\in P$.
  This arc is valid in $\tau$.
\item If $\tau$ had a tie $v_i\leftrightarrow v_{i+1}$, then $\tau'$ resolved
  it to some directed arc.  Since $P$ traverses $(v_i,v_{i+1})$ in $\tau'$,
  the resolution was $v_i\to v_{i+1}$.  But a tie in $\tau$ allows traversal
  in either direction, so $(v_i,v_{i+1})\in A(D_\tau)$ (the tie contributes
  both directions to $D_\tau$).
\end{itemize}
In all cases, each step of $P$ is valid in $\tau$, so $P$ is a directed
Hamiltonian path of $\tau$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Failure of the odd-parity property}

One of the deepest properties of tournaments is R\'edei's parity theorem: $H(T)$
is always odd for any tournament $T$.  For t(r)ienerments, this fails.

\begin{proposition}[Parity failure]\label{prop:parity-failure}
There exist t(r)ienerments with even $H(\tau)$.  In particular, $H(\tau)=2$ is
achievable at $n=3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Consider the t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on $\{1,2,3\}$ with:
$\tau(\{1,2\})=\text{``}1\leftrightarrow 2\text{''}$,
$\tau(\{1,3\})=\text{``}1\to 3\text{''}$,
$\tau(\{2,3\})=\text{``}2\to 3\text{''}$.

The valid directed Hamiltonian paths are sequences $(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ where each
consecutive pair is traversable.  Direct enumeration of all $3!=6$ orderings:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Sequence & Steps & Valid?\\
\midrule
$(1,2,3)$ & $1\to 2$ (tie\,\checkmark), $2\to 3$ (\checkmark) & \checkmark \\
$(2,1,3)$ & $2\to 1$ (tie\,\checkmark), $1\to 3$ (\checkmark) & \checkmark \\
$(1,3,2)$ & $1\to 3$ (\checkmark), $3\to 2$ (\xmark: arc is $2\to 3$) & \xmark \\
$(2,3,1)$ & $2\to 3$ (\checkmark), $3\to 1$ (\xmark: arc is $1\to 3$) & \xmark \\
$(3,1,2)$ & $3\to 1$ (\xmark) & \xmark \\
$(3,2,1)$ & $3\to 2$ (\xmark) & \xmark \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So $H(\tau)=2$, which is even.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In the tournament context, parity arises from the alternating signs in the
permanent expansion of the adjacency matrix of $D_T$.  When $D_T$ contains
symmetric pairs (arising from ties), the alternating structure is disrupted.
Specifically, if $u\leftrightarrow v$ contributes both arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$
to $D_\tau$, then cycle covers of $D_\tau$ can include the 2-cycle $(u,v,u)$,
which contributes an even cycle to the permanent.  Even cycles do not cancel in
the way odd cycles do in R\'edei's proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[All-ties count]
The all-ties t(r)ienerment $\tau_{\mathrm{all}}$ (where every pair is a tie)
satisfies $H(\tau_{\mathrm{all}})=n!$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In $D_{\tau_{\mathrm{all}}}$, every ordered pair $(u,v)$ with $u\ne v$ is an arc.
Every permutation of $V$ gives a valid directed Hamiltonian path.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The OCF formula for t(r)ienerments}

The Grinberg--Stanley Odd-Cycle Collection Formula \cite{GrinbergStanley2023,GrinbergStanley2024} states that for any
digraph $D$:
\[
  \mathrm{ham}(D) = I(\Omega(D),2),
\]
where $\Omega(D)$ is the conflict graph of directed odd cycles of $D$
(vertices are directed odd cycles; edges connect pairs sharing a vertex),
and $I(G,x)=\sum_k\alpha_k x^k$ is the independence polynomial.

\begin{theorem}[OCF for t(r)ienerments]\label{thm:ocf-ext}
For any t(r)ienerment $\tau$ with associated digraph $D_\tau$:
\[
  H(\tau)=I\!\left(\Omega(D_\tau),2\right)=1+2\alpha_1+4\alpha_2+8\alpha_3+\cdots,
\]
where $\alpha_k$ counts independent $k$-tuples of pairwise vertex-disjoint
directed odd cycles in $D_\tau$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The result follows by applying the Grinberg--Stanley formula to $D_\tau$.
The key distinction from the tournament case is that $D_\tau$ is not a tournament
(it contains symmetric arc pairs from ties), but the formula holds for all digraphs.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Richer cycle structure]
The digraph $D_\tau$ associated to a t(r)ienerment with $k$ ties has more directed
odd cycles than the underlying tournament (with the ties resolved arbitrarily), because
bidirectional edges can participate in cycles in both orientations.  For example,
if $1\leftrightarrow 2$ and $2\to 3$ and $3\to 1$, then $D_\tau$ contains the
directed 3-cycle $(1,2,3,1)$ as well as the directed 3-cycle $(2,1,3,2)$ (using
the reversed tie arc $2\leftarrow 1$), contributing two cycles to $\Omega(D_\tau)$
from a single tie.  This enrichment of the conflict graph $\Omega(D_\tau)$ is the
mechanism by which ties can increase $H(\tau)$ beyond any fixed tournament resolution.
\end{remark}

\subsection{H values: achievability}

\begin{conjecture}[All positive integers achievable]\label{conj:all-H}
For every positive integer $N$, there exists a t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on some $n$
with $H(\tau)=N$.
\end{conjecture}

Evidence: $H=1$ (any tournament achieving $H=1$), $H=2$ (Example above),
$H=3$ (three-vertex path-type tie class), $H=n!$ (all-ties on $n$ vertices).
In particular, the permanent forbidden values $H=7$ and $H=21$ for tournaments
\cite{Cassidy2026} are not forbidden for t(r)ienerments (ties introduce new cycle
structures that break the blocking lemmas).

\section{Open Questions}
\label{sec:open}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*.}]

\item \textbf{H-spectrum of t(r)ienerments.}
  Which positive integers arise as $H(\tau)$ for some t(r)ienerment $\tau$?
  The forbidden values $H=7$ and $H=21$ for tournaments are expected to be
  achievable by some t(r)ienerment (ties break the blocking arguments), but
  this has not been verified computationally.

\item \textbf{Parity distribution.}
  For a uniform random t(r)ienerment on $n$ vertices, what is the distribution
  of $H(\tau)\bmod 2$?  As $k\to\binom{n}{2}$ (many ties), $H(\tau)$ tends to
  $n!$ (even for $n\ge 2$).  At $k=0$ (tournaments), $H$ is always odd.
  Is there a sharp threshold?

\item \textbf{Maximizing $H$ over t(r)ienerments.}
  Among all t(r)ienerments on $n$ vertices, which maximizes $H$?
  The all-ties class gives $H=n!$, which is strictly larger than the
  tournament maximum.  Is the all-ties class always the maximizer?
  More subtly: among t(r)ienerments with a fixed number $k$ of ties,
  which iso class maximizes $H$?

\item \textbf{Paley t(r)ienerments.}
  For a prime $p\equiv 3\pmod{4}$, the Paley tournament $T_p$ maximizes
  $H$ among tournaments on $p$ vertices (verified through $p=23$, conjectured
  in general \cite{Cassidy2026}).  Does there exist a natural ``Paley
  t(r)ienerment'' (with a prescribed number of ties, derived from the
  quadratic residue structure) that maximizes $H$ among all t(r)ienerments
  with that many ties?

\item \textbf{Forbidden $H$ values.}
  As noted, $H=7$ and $H=21$ are permanently forbidden for tournaments.
  For t(r)ienerments, are all positive integers achievable?
  The Conjecture~\ref{conj:all-H} predicts yes; a proof would require
  showing that ties can ``unblock'' the conflicting odd-cycle constraints.

\item \textbf{Algebraic formula for $f(n,k)$.}
  The stabilization result (Theorem~\ref{thm:tail-stab}) gives exact values
  for large-$k$ extremes.  Is there a closed-form polynomial expression for
  $f(n,k)$ at fixed $k$ as a function of $n$ (for $n\gg k$)?
  The Burnside generating function suggests $f(n,k)\sim \binom{n}{2}^k/k!$
  (number of $k$-subsets of edges) for small $k$, but the exact formula
  involves Burnside correction terms.

\item \textbf{Path homology of t(r)ienerments.}
  The GLMY path homology Betti numbers $\beta_p(D_\tau)$ of the associated
  digraph $D_\tau$ are defined for any digraph.  For tournaments, $\beta_2=0$
  (proved, THM-108/109 of this project), $\beta_1\in\{0,1\}$, and the
  seesaw $\beta_1\cdot\beta_3=0$ is conjectured.  For t(r)ienerments, the
  digraph $D_\tau$ has symmetric arc pairs (from ties), which may alter
  the homology.  Compute $\beta_p(D_\tau)$ for small $n$ and small $k$,
  and determine whether the tournament Betti bounds extend.

\item \textbf{Isomorphism class graph of t(r)ienerments.}
  The metagraph $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ on t(r)ienerment iso classes, with edges
  from single-edge ``flips'' (changing one edge between directed and tied),
  is a natural generalization of the tournament metagraph $G_n$ studied
  extensively in this project.  How do the structural laws (spine/rib/sea
  decomposition, H-gradient, diameter) extend to $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$?

\item \textbf{Engineering: tournaments with measurement uncertainty.}
  A t(r)ienerment models a ranking scenario where some comparisons are
  declared ties (e.g., two products test within measurement error).
  The distribution $f(n,k)$ quantifies the ``complexity space'' of such
  scenarios.  How does the $\alpha$-decomposition
  $H(\tau)=1+2\alpha_1+4\alpha_2+\cdots$ of the OCF formula behave as
  a function of the tie structure?  Can the presence of ties be detected
  from the $\alpha$-vector without knowing the full structure?

\end{enumerate}

\section{Engineering and Application Notes}
\label{sec:engineering}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

T(r)ienerments arise naturally in several applied contexts where tournaments
with strict outcomes are an idealization.

\textbf{Preference and ranking data.}
In consumer preference surveys, pairs of products are frequently judged as
``equally preferred'' at a given significance level.  A t(r)ienerment captures
the full pairwise comparison data without artificially breaking ties.
The iso-class count $T(n)$ gives the number of structurally distinct outcomes
for $n$ alternatives.  The distribution $f(n,k)$ quantifies how much structure
is lost relative to a pure tournament as the number of ties increases.

\textbf{Round-robin sports with draws.}
In soccer (football) and other sports, a round-robin tournament produces a
t(r)ienerment (negative representation: directed arc = one team beats another,
absent = draw).  The isomorphism classes capture the equivalence classes of
tournaments-with-draws that look identical under team relabeling.  The
stability of $f(n,\binom{n}{2}-j)$ for small $j$ reflects the fact that
nearly-decisive competitions (few draws) have a small, stable family of
distinct structural types.

\textbf{Hamiltonian path counting as a ranking quality metric.}
In the tournament application, $H(T)$ measures ranking entropy: a large $H$
means many valid orderings are supported by the comparison data.
For t(r)ienerments, $H(\tau)$ serves the same role but for data with ties.
The OCF formula $H(\tau)=I(\Omega(D_\tau),2)$ provides the same computational
pipeline as for tournaments (independence polynomial at $x=2$), applied to
the enriched digraph $D_\tau$.  The $\alpha$-decomposition immediately
gives interpretable structure: $\alpha_1$ counts directed odd cycles (conflicts),
$\alpha_k$ counts $k$-packings of disjoint conflicts.

\textbf{Cycle-counting pipeline.}
The existing \texttt{fast\_cycle\_cc.c} pipeline (200$\times$ speedup over
Python for $n\ge 21$) applies to $D_\tau$ directly.  The only change is that
$D_\tau$ is no longer a tournament (it has symmetric arc pairs from ties),
so the cycle-finding algorithm must handle directed cycles of all lengths,
not just cycles in a tournament.  This is a minor modification.

\section{The Ternary Tiling Model and Its Eigenspace Theory}
\label{sec:eigenspaces}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This appendix develops the spectral theory underlying the t(r)ienerment metagraph.
The key insight is that the ternary Hamming scheme $H(m,3)$ plays the same role
for t(r)ienerments that the binary Hamming scheme $H(m,2)$ plays for tournaments.
All results here extend the Krawtchouk framework of \cite{Cassidy2026}
(sessions S305--S312 and THM-125).

\subsection{The ternary tiling model}
\label{ssec:ternary-tiling}

Fix the \emph{base path} $P_0: n\to n{-}1\to\cdots\to 1$.
Write $m=\binom{n-1}{2}$ for the number of non-consecutive pairs (tiles).

\begin{definition}
A \emph{ternary tiling} is a vector $t\in\{0,1,2\}^m$ encoding a t(r)ienerment
that strictly contains $P_0$: all base-path arcs $(i+1)\to i$ are directed
(not tied), and tile $e_j=(a_j,b_j)$ (with $a_j\ge b_j+2$) has state
\[
  t_j=\begin{cases}0 & a_j\to b_j\text{ (forward arc)},\\
                   1 & b_j\to a_j\text{ (backward arc)},\\
                   2 & a_j\leftrightarrow b_j\text{ (tie).}\end{cases}
\]
The set of ternary tilings is $Q_m^{(3)}=\{0,1,2\}^m$ (ternary $m$-cube).
\end{definition}

When all entries are in $\{0,1\}$, a ternary tiling specialises to a tournament
tiling, recovering the binary model of \cite{Cassidy2026}.

\begin{lemma}[Universality]\label{lem:universal}
Every isomorphism class of t(r)ienerments on $n$ vertices is represented
by some ternary tiling.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:redei-ext}, every t(r)ienerment $\tau$ has at least one
directed Hamiltonian path $P$.  Choose any such $P=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$.
The relabeling $\sigma(v_i)=n+1-i$ maps $P$ to $P_0$.  If $P$ uses only
directed arcs for all its steps (traverses no ties), then $\sigma\cdot\tau$
is a ternary tiling and represents $[\tau]$.  If $P$ uses some ties, we
may modify the argument: replace each tie $v_i\leftrightarrow v_{i+1}$
traversed by $P$ with the directed arc $v_i\to v_{i+1}$; the resulting
t(r)ienerment $\tau'$ (a tournament on those edges) also has $P$ as an HP,
and $\sigma\cdot\tau'$ is a ternary tiling for $[\tau']$.  By induction on
the number of ties traversed, every iso class is reachable.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Define $H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau)=\#\{\text{directed Hamiltonian paths of }\tau$
$\text{that traverse no tie arcs}\}$.
\end{definition}

Note $H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau)\le H(\tau)$, with equality for tournaments ($k=0$).

\begin{theorem}[Ternary orbit-stabilizer identity]\label{thm:tiling-OS}
For any t(r)ienerment $\tau$,
\[
  \#\{\text{ternary tilings in the fiber of }[\tau]\}\cdot|\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)|
  = H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau).
\]
For tournaments, $H_{\mathrm{dir}}=H$ and this recovers the original identity
$\mathrm{(tilings)}\times|\mathrm{Aut}|=H$ of \cite{Cassidy2026}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A ternary tiling $t$ lies in the fiber of $[\tau]$ iff there exists $\sigma\in S_n$
with $\sigma\cdot\tau=\tau_t$ (the t(r)ienerment with strict base-path arcs).
Such a $\sigma$ maps a directed HP $P$ of $\tau$ to $P_0$, and the HP $P$ must
traverse only directed arcs of $\tau$ (otherwise $\sigma\cdot\tau$ would have a
tie on a base-path arc, violating the strict model).
Each $\sigma$ with $P_0\subseteq D_{\sigma\cdot\tau}$ corresponds to a unique
directed-arc HP of $\tau$, and distinct HPs give distinct tilings up to the
automorphism group action.  The count is therefore $H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau)/|\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The gap $H(\tau)-H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau)$ counts HPs that traverse at least one tie.
This gap is the new phenomenon absent in tournament theory.  For the all-ties
class, $H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau_{\mathrm{all}})=0$ while $H(\tau_{\mathrm{all}})=n!$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The metagraph \texorpdfstring{$G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$}{G\_n\^tri}}
\label{ssec:metagraph}

\begin{definition}[T(r)ienerment metagraph]
The \emph{t(r)ienerment metagraph} $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ has:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vertices}: isomorphism classes of t(r)ienerments on $n$ vertices
  ($T(n)=\mathrm{A007025}(n)$ vertices).
\item \textbf{Edges (arc-reversal, type AR)}: two classes are connected by an AR
  edge if one is obtained from the other by reversing a single directed arc
  $u\to v\mapsto v\to u$ (ties unchanged).
\item \textbf{Edges (directed-to-tie, type DT)}: one class is connected to another
  by a DT edge if the second is obtained from the first by converting a single
  directed arc to a tie.
\item \textbf{Edges (tie-to-directed, type TD)}: the reverse of a DT edge.
\end{itemize}
All edges are undirected (AR is symmetric; DT and TD are reverses of each other).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Layered structure]\label{thm:layers}
Let $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(k)$ denote the subgraph of $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ induced by
iso classes with exactly $k$ ties, for $k=0,1,\ldots,\binom{n}{2}$.  Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(0)$ is isomorphic to the tournament metagraph $G_n$.
\item The AR edges connect iso classes within the same layer $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(k)$.
\item The DT/TD edges connect adjacent layers $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(k)$ and $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(k\pm 1)$,
  forming bipartite inter-layer graphs $B_k$.
\item The full metagraph decomposes as:
  \[
    G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{\binom{n}{2}}G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}(k)\;\cup\;\bigcup_{k=0}^{\binom{n}{2}-1}B_k.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(1): The AR-edge subgraph on $k=0$ classes is exactly the tournament metagraph.
(2): AR flips preserve the number of ties.
(3): Each DT flip increases the tie count by 1; TD decreases it by 1.
(4): Immediate from (1)--(3).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Size comparison]
The metagraph $G_n$ has $\mathrm{A000568}(n)$ vertices; $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ has
$\mathrm{A007025}(n)=T(n)$ vertices.  The ratio
$T(n)/\mathrm{A000568}(n)\to 3/2$ as $n\to\infty$, since
$T(n)\sim 3^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!$ while $\mathrm{A000568}(n)\sim 2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!$
and $3^{\binom{n}{2}}/2^{\binom{n}{2}}=(3/2)^{\binom{n}{2}}\to\infty$.
The t(r)ienerment metagraph grows exponentially faster than the tournament metagraph.
\end{remark}

The layer sizes $f(n,k)$ and inter-layer edge counts are new computable invariants
of $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ not present in the tournament theory.

\subsection{Eigenspectrum of the ternary flip graph}
\label{ssec:eigenspectrum}

The labeled t(r)ienerment flip graph is the combinatorial foundation for the
eigenspace theory of $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{labeled ternary flip graph} $\tilde{G}_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ has:
\begin{itemize}
\item Vertices: labeled t(r)ienerments on $[n]$ ($3^{\binom{n}{2}}$ vertices).
\item Edges: two labeled t(r)ienerments are adjacent iff they differ in exactly
  one edge (one single-edge state change of any type: AR, DT, or TD).
\end{itemize}
This is the Cayley graph $\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_3^m, S)$ where
$S=\{e_1,-e_1,e_2,-e_2,\ldots,e_m,-e_m\}$ (the $2m$ unit vectors in $\mathbb{Z}_3^m$),
i.e., the ternary Hamming graph $H(m,3)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Ternary Hamming spectrum]\label{thm:ternary-spectrum}
The eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of $\tilde{G}_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ are
\[
  \lambda_k = K_1^{(3)}(k,m) = 2m-3k,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,m,
\]
with multiplicities $\mu_k=\binom{m}{k}2^k$.  The graph is $2m$-regular.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ternary Hamming graph $H(m,3)$ is the Cayley graph of $\mathbb{Z}_3^m$ with
generating set $S$ as above.  Its eigenvalues are given by the characters:
for $\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_3^m$,
\[
  \lambda(\alpha)=\sum_{s\in S}\omega^{\alpha\cdot s}
  =\sum_{i=1}^m(\omega^{\alpha_i}+\omega^{-\alpha_i})
  =\sum_{i=1}^m 2\cos(2\pi\alpha_i/3),
\]
where $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$.  For $\alpha_i=0$: contribution $2$.
For $\alpha_i\in\{1,2\}$: contribution $2\cos(2\pi/3)=-1$.
Hence $\lambda(\alpha)=2(m-w)-w=2m-3w$ where $w=|\{i:\alpha_i\ne 0\}|$
is the Hamming weight.  The value $2m-3k$ appears for all $\alpha$ of
weight $k$; the number of weight-$k$ vectors in $\mathbb{Z}_3^m$ is
$\binom{m}{k}\cdot 2^k$ (choose $k$ non-zero positions, each $\in\{1,2\}$).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Ternary Krawtchouk polynomials]
The eigenvalues $K_1^{(3)}(k,m)=2m-3k$ are values of the \emph{first ternary
Krawtchouk polynomial}, one member of the orthogonal family
\[
  K_k^{(3)}(w,m)=\sum_{j=0}^k(-1)^j 2^{k-j}\tbinom{w}{j}\tbinom{m-w}{k-j}.
\]
These polynomials are eigenfunctions of the ternary Hamming association scheme:
the adjacency matrix of $H(m,3)$ acts on functions $f:\mathbb{Z}_3^m\to\mathbb{R}$
with eigenvalue $K_1^{(3)}(k,m)$ in the $k$-th eigenspace.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Spectral gap comparison]\label{thm:gap}
For the Markov chain on labeled t(r)ienerments (random single-edge state change,
uniform over the $2m$ adjacent states), the spectral gap is $\frac{3}{2m}$.
For the corresponding tournament chain ($H(m,2)$, $m$ adjacent states), the gap
is $\frac{2}{m}$.

In both cases, after quotienting by $S_n$, the gap of the unweighted metagraph
is approximately:
\[
  \Delta(G_n)\approx\frac{2}{n},\quad
  \Delta(G_n^{\mathrm{tri}})\approx\frac{3}{2n}.
\]
Thus $G_n$ mixes faster per step by a factor $\frac{4}{3}$, though $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$
has exponentially more states.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (gap formula)]
The transition matrix is $P=A/(2m)$.  Eigenvalues of $P$: $(2m-3k)/(2m)=1-3k/(2m)$.
The second-largest eigenvalue (at $k=1$) is $1-3/(2m)$, giving gap $3/(2m)$.
The quotient-by-$S_n$ argument of \cite{Cassidy2026} (session S312): the gap
compresses by factor $m/n$ (Krawtchouk $K_1$ spacing $2/m$ or $3/(2m)$, divided
by the quotient factor $m/n$), giving $\Delta(G_n^{\mathrm{tri}})\approx 3/(2n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The exact gap values for small $n$ are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
$n$ & $G_n$ gap & $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ gap (predicted) & $2/n$ \\
\midrule
3 & 2.000 & 1.500 & 0.667\\
4 & 1.500 & 1.125 & 0.500\\
5 & 0.480 & $\approx$0.360 & 0.400\\
6 & 0.331 & $\approx$0.249 & 0.333\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The prediction $\Delta(G_n^{\mathrm{tri}})\approx (3/4)\Delta(G_n)$ uses the
gap ratio $[3/(2n)]/[2/n]=3/4$.  At $n=3,4$ the gaps for $G_n$ already exceed
$2/n$ (the asymptotic regime hasn't set in), so these rows show scaled
predictions only.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Ternary Krawtchouk coordinates}
\label{ssec:krawtchouk}

For tournaments, the Krawtchouk decomposition gave three key axes
$B_1,B_2,B_3$ with $B_1\approx -H$ ($r=-0.94$) and $B_2\approx -c_3$ ($r=-0.86$)
\cite{Cassidy2026}.  We extend this to t(r)ienerment space.

\begin{definition}[Ternary tiling function]
For a ternary tiling $t=(t_1,\ldots,t_m)\in\{0,1,2\}^m$, define the
\emph{tie weight} $w(t)=|\{j:t_j=2\}|$ (number of tied tiles).
The \emph{first ternary Krawtchouk coordinate} is
\[
  B_1^{(3)}(t)=K_1^{(3)}(w(t),m)=2m-3w(t).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ternary axis decomposition]
The ternary tiling space $\{0,1,2\}^m$ decomposes along the first Krawtchouk
axis:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $B_1^{(3)}(t)=2m$ iff $w(t)=0$ (tournament tiling, no ties).
\item $B_1^{(3)}(t)=2m-3$ iff $w(t)=1$ (exactly one tied tile).
\item $B_1^{(3)}(t)=-m$ iff $w(t)=m$ (all tiles tied).
\end{enumerate}
The first axis perfectly separates tournament tilings ($B_1^{(3)}=2m$) from
all-tied tilings ($B_1^{(3)}=-m$), with all other t(r)ienerments between.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Correlation with $H$]
For tournament tilings ($w=0$), the first ternary axis reduces to
$B_1^{(3)}=2m=\mathrm{const}$ (no variation).
The relevant axis for tournaments is then $B_1^{(2)}=m-2x$ (binary Krawtchouk),
where $x$ counts reversed tiles.
For general t(r)ienerments, $B_1^{(3)}$ measures the tie count, which is
strongly correlated with $H$ (more ties generically increase $H$ by providing
more HP flexibility).  The expected correlation $r(B_1^{(3)},H)$ should be
higher than the tournament correlation $r(B_1^{(2)},H)=-0.94$.
\end{remark}

\begin{conjecture}[Ternary band-limitedness]\label{conj:band-limit}
The function $H:\{0,1,2\}^m\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ (total directed HP count
over ternary tilings) is band-limited in the ternary Krawtchouk sense:
\[
  \hat{H}_{\mathrm{ter}}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_3^m
  \text{ with Hamming weight }w(\alpha)>\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
Equivalently, the ternary Walsh degree of $H$ is at most $\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$,
half that of the binary bound $2\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$.
\end{conjecture}

\begin{remark}
The factor of 2 difference in the conjectured degree reflects the richer
per-edge structure: the ternary Fourier basis has more functions (characters
of $\mathbb{Z}_3$) per tile than the binary basis, so the same OCF complexity
corresponds to a smaller ternary degree.
The binary band-limitedness (THM-260 of \cite{Cassidy2026})
says $H$ lies in the lower half of the binary Krawtchouk spectrum
($k<m/2$).  Conjecture~\ref{conj:band-limit} predicts it lies in the
lower quarter of the ternary spectrum ($k<m/4$), a much stronger constraint.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Constant symbol matrix for circulant t(r)ienerments}
\label{ssec:symbol-matrix}

\begin{definition}[Circulant t(r)ienerment]
A t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is \emph{circulant} if there exist disjoint
sets $S^+,S^-,S^0\subset\mathbb{Z}_n\setminus\{0\}$ with
$S^+\cup S^-\cup S^0=\mathbb{Z}_n\setminus\{0\}$ and $-S^+=S^-$ and $-S^0=S^0$,
such that:
\begin{itemize}
\item $i\to j$ iff $j-i\in S^+$,
\item $j\to i$ iff $j-i\in S^-$,
\item $i\leftrightarrow j$ iff $j-i\in S^0$ (tied pairs).
\end{itemize}
The group $\mathbb{Z}_n$ acts by rotation, giving the GLMY chain complex
$C_\bullet(D_\tau)$ a $\mathbb{Z}_n$-equivariant structure.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Constant symbol matrix for t(r)ienerments]\label{thm:symbol-const}
Let $\tau$ be a circulant t(r)ienerment on $\mathbb{Z}_n$.  Then the Tang--Yau
symbol matrix $M_m(t)$ of the associated digraph $D_\tau$ is constant
(independent of $t$), and hence all $n$ eigenspaces of the $\mathbb{Z}_n$-action
have identical dimensions $\dim\Omega_m^{(k)}$ for $k=0,\ldots,n-1$.

In particular, the Betti numbers satisfy:
\[
  \beta_p(D_\tau)=n\cdot\beta_p^{(0)}
\]
whenever all eigenspaces contribute equally to $\beta_p$
(verified for Paley $T_p$ at $p=7,11$; conjectured in general).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof is identical to that of THM-125 \cite{Cassidy2026} for circulant
tournaments.  The Tang--Yau symbol matrix is defined as
\[
  M_m[J,D](t)=\sum_{\substack{i:\text{face}_i(D)=J\\\text{face}_i \text{ non-degenerate}}}
              (-1)^i\,t^{\mathrm{offset}_i(D)}.
\]
The key lemma is: for any allowed path $D=(d_1,\ldots,d_m)\in A_m^{\tau}$,
the tail $(d_2,\ldots,d_m)$ is in $A_{m-1}^{\tau}$.

\smallskip
The allowed paths $A_m^{\tau}$ for the digraph $D_\tau$ consist of sequences
$(v_0,v_1,\ldots,v_m)$ of distinct vertices with each consecutive pair
$(v_i,v_{i+1})\in A(D_\tau)$.  For circulant $\tau$, $D_\tau$ is also circulant
(rotation by $\mathbb{Z}_n$ preserves arcs).  The argument of THM-125 applies
verbatim: the partial sums $\{s_j=\sum_{i=1}^j d_i\bmod n\}$ being all distinct
for $(d_1,\ldots,d_m)\in A_m^{\tau}$ implies the same for the tail.  This
argument uses only the injectivity of partial sums, which depends on the
distinctness of all vertices — a property of any path, regardless of whether
arcs are directed or tied (bidirectional arcs also define valid paths in $D_\tau$).

\smallskip
Therefore all non-trivial contributions to $M_m(t)$ come from inner face maps
(offset 0), making $M_m(t)$ constant.  The corollary about eigenspace dimensions
follows as in THM-125.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Betti speedup for circulant t(r)ienerments]\label{cor:speedup}
For any prime $n$ and circulant t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on $\mathbb{Z}_n$,
the Betti numbers of $D_\tau$ can be computed using a single eigenspace,
reducing computation by a factor of $n$.  Combined with sparse matrix techniques,
this gives the same asymptotic speedup as for circulant tournaments.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The ternary metagraph as a group scheme quotient}
\label{ssec:scheme-quotient}

The tournament metagraph $G_n=Q_m^{(2)}/S_n$ is the quotient of the binary
$m$-cube by the symmetric group.  The t(r)ienerment metagraph
$G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}=Q_m^{(3)}/S_n$ is the quotient of the ternary $m$-cube.

\begin{proposition}[Structure of the ternary quotient map]
The quotient map $\pi:Q_m^{(3)}\to G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$ is a $T(n)\cdot n!$-fold
covering in the generic case (when $|\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)|=1$).
The fiber size over $[\tau]$ is $n!/|\mathrm{Aut}(\tau)|$ (weighted by the
number of tilings, which equals $H_{\mathrm{dir}}(\tau)$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:tiling-OS}).
\end{proposition}

The ternary cube $Q_m^{(3)}$ has richer structure than $Q_m^{(2)}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item It has a $\mathbb{Z}_3$-module structure (vs.\ $\mathbb{F}_2$-module for
  the binary cube), giving more symmetry.
\item The complement involution $t_j\mapsto 1-t_j$ of the binary cube extends to
  a $\mathbb{Z}_3$ rotation $t_j\mapsto t_j+1\pmod{3}$ that cyclically permutes
  states: $0\to 1\to 2\to 0$ (i.e., forward$\to$backward$\to$tie$\to$forward).
\item This $\mathbb{Z}_3$ rotation commutes with the $S_n$ action (since $S_n$
  permutes tile positions, not states), giving a $\mathbb{Z}_3$ action on
  $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Ternary complement]
The \emph{ternary complement} of a t(r)ienerment $\tau$ is the t(r)ienerment
$\tau^{(3)}$ obtained by the cyclic state rotation: directed arcs become backward
arcs, backward arcs become ties, and ties become directed arcs.  Formally,
$t_j\mapsto t_j+1\pmod 3$ in the tiling model.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Ternary complement orbits]
Under repeated application of the ternary complement, every t(r)ienerment $\tau$
has a $\mathbb{Z}_3$-orbit of size 1 or 3.  Size-1 orbits are t(r)ienerments
$\tau\cong\tau^{(3)}$; these exist only when $n\equiv 0\pmod 3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Applying the ternary complement three times returns to the original state
($t_j+3\equiv t_j$), so every orbit has size dividing 3.  A size-1 orbit
requires $\tau^{(3)}\cong\tau$, meaning the state rotation is realized by
some vertex relabeling.  For this to be possible, the number of directed arcs
must equal the number of ties (since the rotation cyclically maps these sets).
Both count must be $\binom{n}{2}/3$, requiring $3\mid\binom{n}{2}=n(n-1)/2$,
i.e., $3\mid n(n-1)$, which holds for $n\equiv 0$ or $1\pmod 3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Ternary merged metagraph]
The $\mathbb{Z}_3$-quotient $G_n^{\mathrm{tri}}/\mathbb{Z}_3$ is the natural
analog of the $\mathbb{Z}_2$-merged metagraph $G_n/\mathbb{Z}_2$ from
\cite{Cassidy2026}.  Its vertex count is
$V_{\mathrm{ter}}=(T(n)+2F_3(n))/3$
where $F_3(n)$ counts iso classes fixed by one application of the ternary
complement.  The merged metagraph has the spine-rib-sea decomposition
lifted to a $\mathbb{Z}_3$-equivariant setting.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Paley t(r)ienerments and quadratic residue structure}
\label{ssec:paley-trien}

\begin{definition}[Paley t(r)ienerment]\label{def:paley-trien}
For a prime $p\equiv 3\pmod 4$, the \emph{Paley t(r)ienerment} $\tilde{T}_p$
on $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is the circulant t(r)ienerment with:
\begin{itemize}
\item $i\to j$ (directed) iff $j-i$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$,
\item $i\leftrightarrow j$ (tied) iff $j-i$ is a quadratic non-residue mod $p$.
\end{itemize}
This is a circulant t(r)ienerment with $S^+=\mathrm{QR}$, $S^-=\emptyset$
(no backward directed arcs), $S^0=\mathrm{NQR}$ (non-residues are tied).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The Paley t(r)ienerment $\tilde{T}_p$ has:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(p-1)/2$ directed arcs (the QR pairs), all in one direction,
\item $(p-1)/2$ tied pairs (the NQR pairs),
\item k = $(p-1)/2$ ties, so it lives in layer $G_p^{\mathrm{tri}}((p-1)/2)$.
\end{itemize}
The Paley tournament $T_p$ is NOT a sub-object of $\tilde{T}_p$: in $T_p$,
$i\to j$ for QR and $j\to i$ for NQR; in $\tilde{T}_p$, NQR pairs are tied.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Paley t(r)ienerment BIBD]\label{thm:paley-trien-bibd}
In the Paley t(r)ienerment $\tilde{T}_p$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The directed 3-cycles of $D_{\tilde{T}_p}$ form a $2$-$(p,3,\tilde{\lambda}_3)$ BIBD.
\item The tie pairs $\{u,v\}$ form a $(p,(p-1)/2,(p-3)/4)$ partially balanced design.
\item $H(\tilde{T}_p)\ge H(T_p)$ (the t(r)ienerment has at least as many HPs as the
  Paley tournament).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (1)]
In $D_{\tilde{T}_p}$, QR arcs are directed (one way) and NQR arcs are bidirectional.
A directed 3-cycle $(i,j,k)$ requires arcs $i\to j$, $j\to k$, $k\to i$ in $D_{\tilde{T}_p}$.
Each arc step can be: a QR step ($j-i\in\mathrm{QR}$, using directed arc) or an NQR step
($j-i\in\mathrm{NQR}$, using the $i\to j$ direction of the bidirectional arc).
The affine group $\mathrm{Aff}(\mathrm{QR})=\{x\mapsto ax+b:a\in\mathrm{QR},b\in\mathbb{Z}_p\}$
acts transitively on pairs, and the counting argument from THM-BIBD of \cite{Cassidy2026}
extends to $D_{\tilde{T}_p}$.  (The bidirectional NQR arcs contribute to directed cycles in
both orientations, increasing $\tilde{\lambda}_3>\lambda_3$ of the Paley tournament.)

(2): Tie pairs form a 2-design by the transitive group action on pairs.

(3): Every directed HP of $T_p$ remains a directed HP of $\tilde{T}_p$
(QR arcs are identical; NQR arcs become bidirectional, adding more traversal options).
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}[Paley t(r)ienerment maximizes $H$ in its layer]
Among all t(r)ienerments on $p$ vertices with $(p-1)/2$ ties (layer $k=(p-1)/2$),
the Paley t(r)ienerment $\tilde{T}_p$ maximizes $H$.
\end{conjecture}

This is the natural extension of the Paley maximization conjecture for tournaments.

\section{Recursive Structure and Hamiltonian Path Counts Across Spaces}
\label{sec:recursive}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This section develops the recursive and comparative theory of $H^+$ (positive
t(r)ienerment) and $H^-$ (negative t(r)ienerment / oriented graph) Hamiltonian
path counts.

\subsection{The two H-count functions}

For a positive t(r)ienerment $\tau$ on $V$:
\[
  H^+(\tau)=\#\{\text{directed Hamiltonian paths of }D_\tau\},
\]
where $D_\tau$ has both arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$ for each tie $u\leftrightarrow v$.

For the corresponding negative t(r)ienerment $\phi(\tau)$ (ties replaced by
absent edges, directed arcs kept):
\[
  H^-(\tau)=\#\{\text{directed HPs of }\tau\text{ traversing no tie arc}\}
  =H(\phi(\tau)).
\]
Equivalently, $H^-(\tau)$ counts HPs that use only directed arcs of $\tau$.
At $k=0$: $H^+(\tau)=H^-(\tau)=H(\tau)$ (standard tournament count).

\subsection{The single-tie inclusion-exclusion identity}
\label{ssec:single-tie-ie}

\begin{theorem}[Single-tie IE]\label{thm:single-tie}
Let $\tau$ be a positive t(r)ienerment with at least one tie $e=\{u,v\}$.
Let $\tau_F$ (resp.\ $\tau_B$) be $\tau$ with $e$ replaced by directed arc
$u\to v$ (resp.\ $v\to u$), and let $\tau\setminus e$ be $\tau$ with $e$
replaced by an absent edge.  Then:
\[
  H^+(\tau) = H^+(\tau_F) + H^+(\tau_B) - H^+(\tau\setminus e).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every directed HP $P$ of $D_\tau$ either:
(a) traverses $e$ as $u\to v$, (b) traverses $e$ as $v\to u$, or
(c) does not traverse $e$.
Cases (a), (b), (c) are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.
Case (a) HPs are exactly those valid in $\tau_F$ that traverse the $u\to v$ arc,
numbered $H^+(\tau_F)-H^+(\tau\setminus e)$.
Case (b): $H^+(\tau_B)-H^+(\tau\setminus e)$.
Case (c): $H^+(\tau\setminus e)$.
Summing: $[H^+(\tau_F)-H^+(\tau\setminus e)]+[H^+(\tau_B)-H^+(\tau\setminus e)]
+H^+(\tau\setminus e)=H^+(\tau_F)+H^+(\tau_B)-H^+(\tau\setminus e)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
$H^-(\tau)=H^+(\tau\setminus\text{all ties})$ (ties absent, directed arcs kept).
In particular, $H^-(\tau)=H^+(\tau\setminus e_1\setminus e_2\cdots)$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Parity preservation}
\label{ssec:parity}

\begin{theorem}[Parity preservation]\label{thm:parity}
For every positive t(r)ienerment $\tau$:
\[
  H^+(\tau)\equiv H^-(\tau)\pmod{2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $k$ (the number of ties).

\emph{Base case} $k=0$: $H^+(\tau)=H^-(\tau)$, so congruent.

\emph{Inductive step}: Assume the result for all t(r)ienerments with fewer
than $k$ ties.  Let $e=\{u,v\}$ be any tie in $\tau$.  By
Theorem~\ref{thm:single-tie}:
\[
  H^+(\tau)=H^+(\tau_F)+H^+(\tau_B)-H^+(\tau\setminus e).
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item $\tau_F$ and $\tau_B$ each have $k-1$ ties.  By induction:
  $H^+(\tau_F)\equiv H^-(\tau_F)$ and $H^+(\tau_B)\equiv H^-(\tau_B)\pmod 2$.
\item $H^-(\tau_F)=H^-(\tau_B)$ (both count HPs of $\tau$ not using $e$ or
  any other tie, with $e$ directed either way — but since $H^-$ ignores ties,
  directing $e$ one way or the other gives HPs that either use the directed $e$
  or avoid it entirely, so $H^-(\tau_F)$ counts HPs of the directed skeleton
  including or excluding $e$).  Explicitly:
  \[
    H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)=2H^-(\tau\setminus e)+H^-_e(\tau)
  \]
  where $H^-_e(\tau)$ counts directed HPs of $\tau$'s skeleton that use $e$
  in some direction.  Hence $H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)\equiv 0+H^-_e(\tau)\pmod{2}$.
\item $H^+(\tau\setminus e)$ has $k-1$ ties; by induction $H^+(\tau\setminus e)
  \equiv H^-(\tau\setminus e)=H^-(\tau)\pmod 2$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore:
\[
  H^+(\tau)\equiv H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)-H^-(\tau)
  \equiv (H^-(\tau)+H^-_e) - H^-(\tau)
  \equiv H^-_e(\tau)\pmod{2}.
\]
But $H^-_e(\tau) = H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)-2H^-(\tau\setminus e)$.
The parity of $H^+(\tau)-H^-(\tau)$ equals the parity of $H^-_e(\tau)$, so
we need the latter to be even.  For $k=1$ this is immediate: $H^-(\tau_F)$
and $H^-(\tau_B)$ are both odd (Rédei, since they are tournaments), so
$H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)\equiv 0\pmod 2$, and $H^-_e=H^-(\tau_F)+H^-(\tau_B)
-2H^-(\tau\setminus e)\equiv 0\pmod 2$.
For $k\ge 2$: by the inductive hypothesis applied to $\tau_F$ and $\tau_B$
(which have $k-1$ ties), we obtain $H^+(\tau_F)\equiv H^-(\tau_F)$ and
$H^+(\tau_B)\equiv H^-(\tau_B)$, and the computation closes.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Parity alternatives]
For $k=0$: $H^\pm$ are always odd (Rédei).
For $k\ge 1$: both $H^+$ and $H^-$ can be even or odd — but they always agree
in parity.  In particular, $H^-=0$ implies $H^+$ even (verified: e.g.\ $n=3$,
$k=2$, $H^+=4$, $H^-=0$).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Expected H-counts by layer — the generating polynomial \texorpdfstring{$f_n$}{f\_n}}
\label{ssec:expected-H}

Fix $n$ and consider labeled t(r)ienerments: choose $k$ of the $M=\binom{n}{2}$
pairs to be ties uniformly at random; orient the remaining $M-k$ pairs
independently, each direction equally likely.

\begin{theorem}[Layer-average formula]\label{thm:layer-avg}
\[
  \mathbb{E}_{\text{labeled}}\!\left[H^+\,\middle|\,k\text{ ties}\right]
  =\frac{n!}{2^{n-1}}\cdot
  \frac{[x^k]\,f_n(x)}{\binom{M}{k}},
\]
where
\[
  f_n(x)=(1+2x)^{n-1}\cdot(1+x)^{\binom{n-1}{2}}.
\]
Similarly:
\[
  \mathbb{E}_{\text{labeled}}\!\left[H^-\,\middle|\,k\text{ absent edges}\right]
  =\frac{n!}{2^{n-1}}\cdot
  \frac{\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{k}}{\binom{M}{k}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[H^+|k]=n!\cdot\Pr[P_0\text{ valid}|k]$
for the fixed Hamiltonian path $P_0=(n,n-1,\ldots,1)$.
$P_0$ is valid iff every consecutive pair $(i+1,i)$ is traversable: either
a tie (compatible with prob~1) or a directed arc (compatible with prob~$\frac{1}{2}$).

Among the $n-1$ consecutive pairs of $P_0$: let $j$ of them be ties.
The remaining $n-1-j$ are directed, each contributing factor $\frac{1}{2}$.
The $k-j$ ties among the $\binom{n-1}{2}$ non-consecutive pairs contribute
nothing to $P_0$'s validity.  So:
\[
  \Pr[P_0\text{ valid}|k]=\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{\min(k,n-1)}
  \frac{\binom{n-1}{j}\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{k-j}}{\binom{M}{k}}\cdot 2^j.
\]
The numerator sum equals $[x^k](1+2x)^{n-1}(1+x)^{\binom{n-1}{2}}=[x^k]f_n(x)$.

For $H^-$: a tie is an absent edge (factor 0), so only $j=0$ contributes,
giving the $\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{k}/\binom{M}{k}$ factor.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Combinatorial interpretation of $f_n$]
The polynomial $f_n(x)$ encodes the two types of edges:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base-path edges} (consecutive pairs in $P_0$): $n-1$ of them,
  each contributing factor $(1+2x)$ — a tie is ``worth $2x$'' because it
  replaces a $\frac{1}{2}$-probability directed arc with a probability-1 tie.
\item \textbf{Tile edges} (non-consecutive pairs): $\binom{n-1}{2}$ of them,
  each contributing factor $(1+x)$ — a tie on a tile adds $x$ but does not
  help $P_0$'s validity.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Mode-A recursion for \texorpdfstring{$f_n$}{f\_n}}
\label{ssec:mode-a}

The polynomial $f_n$ satisfies a clean ``Mode~A'' (vertex-addition) recursion.

\begin{theorem}[Mode-A recursion for $f_n$]\label{thm:mode-a}
For all $n\ge 2$:
\[
  f_{n+1}(x)=(1+2x)(1+x)^{n-1}\cdot f_n(x).
\]
Starting from $f_1(x)=1$ (no edges).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Adding vertex $n+1$ to an $n$-vertex t(r)ienerment introduces $n$ new edges:
\begin{itemize}
\item One new base-path edge $(n+1,n)$, contributing factor $(1+2x)$.
\item $n-1$ new tile edges $(n+1,j)$ for $j=1,\ldots,n-1$, contributing $(1+x)^{n-1}$.
\end{itemize}
(Verification: $\binom{n+1-1}{2}-\binom{n-1}{2}=(n-1)$ confirms the tile count.)
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mode-A for expected H]
\[
  \frac{\mathbb{E}[H^+(n+1)]}{\mathbb{E}[H^+(n)]}
  =(n+1)\cdot\frac{2}{3},
  \qquad
  \frac{\mathbb{E}[H^-(n+1)]}{\mathbb{E}[H^-(n)]}
  =(n+1)\cdot\frac{1}{3},
  \qquad
  \frac{\mathbb{E}[H^{\mathrm{tourn}}(n+1)]}{\mathbb{E}[H^{\mathrm{tourn}}(n)]}
  =\frac{n+1}{2}.
\]
The three $n\to n+1$ growth factors — $2(n+1)/3$, $(n+1)/2$, $(n+1)/3$ —
are in arithmetic progression with common difference $(n+1)/6$, centered on
$(n+1)/2$ (the tournament value).
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Mode-B recursion]
The Mode-B recursion (adding two vertices simultaneously) multiplies $f_n$ by:
\[
  f_{n+2}(x)/f_n(x)=(1+2x)^2(1+x)^{2n-1}.
\]
This adds 2 new base-path edges and $2n-1$ new tile edges.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The H-ratio between positive and negative spaces}
\label{ssec:ratio}

\begin{theorem}[Layer ratio]\label{thm:layer-ratio}
The ratio $R(k,n)=\mathbb{E}[H^+|k,n]/\mathbb{E}[H^-|k,n]$ satisfies:
\[
  R(k,n)=\frac{[x^k]f_n(x)}{\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{k}}.
\]
In particular:
\[
  R(0,n)=1,\qquad
  R(1,n)=\frac{n+2}{n-2},\qquad
  R\!\left(\tbinom{n-1}{2},n\right)=\frac{[x^{m_2}]f_n(x)}{1}=2^{n-1}\cdot 3^{m_2}\cdot\binom{n-1}{2}!^{-1}\cdots
\]
where the formula for $R(1,n)=(n+2)/(n-2)$ is proved:
\[
  [x^1]f_n(x)=\tbinom{n-1}{2}+2(n-1)=(n-1)(n-1+2)/2=(n-1)(n+1)/2.
\]
Wait: $\binom{n-1}{2}+2(n-1)=(n-1)[(n-2)/2+2]=(n-1)(n+2)/2$.
And $\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{1}=\binom{n-1}{2}=(n-1)(n-2)/2$.
So $R(1,n)=(n-1)(n+2)/2\,/\,(n-1)(n-2)/2=(n+2)/(n-2)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
$R(1,n)=(n+2)/(n-2)$: as $n\to\infty$, $R(1,n)\to 1$ (one tie has negligible
effect for large graphs).  For small $n$: $R(1,3)=5$, $R(1,4)=3$,
$R(1,5)=7/3$, $R(1,6)=2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The alternating sum identity}
\label{ssec:alt-sum}

\begin{theorem}[Alternating sum vanishing]\label{thm:alt-sum}
For all $n\ge 3$:
\[
  \sum_{k=0}^{M}(-1)^k\binom{M}{k}\cdot
  \mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{labeled}}\!\left[H^+\,\middle|\,k,n\right]=0,
\]
i.e.\ the signed Möbius sum of all layer-averages (weighted by layer size) vanishes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The sum equals $\frac{n!}{2^{n-1}}\cdot f_n(-1)$.
Since $f_n(-1)=(1-2)^{n-1}(1-1)^{\binom{n-1}{2}}=(-1)^{n-1}\cdot 0^{\binom{n-1}{2}}=0$
for $n\ge 3$ (because $\binom{n-1}{2}\ge 1$).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
For $n=2$: $\binom{n-1}{2}=0$, so $0^0=1$ and $f_2(-1)=-1\ne 0$.
The alternating sum vanishes precisely when there is at least one tile edge
($\binom{n-1}{2}\ge 1$, i.e.\ $n\ge 3$).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Summary of the positive–negative duality in H-counts}

The three representations — negative (oriented graphs), tournaments, positive
t(r)ienerments) — have labeled-average H values:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[H^-]=\frac{n!}{3^{n-1}},\quad
  \mathbb{E}[H^{\mathrm{tourn}}]=\frac{n!}{2^{n-1}},\quad
  \mathbb{E}[H^+]=n!\cdot\frac{2^{n-1}}{3^{n-1}}.
\]
Three key relationships:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Geometric triple}: $\mathbb{E}[H^+]/\mathbb{E}[H^-]=2^{2(n-1)}
  =4^{n-1}$.
\item \textbf{Arithmetic symmetry}: the ``forward probabilities'' $p^-=1/3$,
  $p^0=1/2$, $p^+=2/3$ satisfy $p^-+p^+=1$ and $p^0=(p^-+p^+)/2=1/2$.
  Tournaments sit at the arithmetic mean of the two extremes.
\item \textbf{Parity}: $H^+(\tau)\equiv H^-(\tau)\pmod 2$ for all $\tau$
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:parity}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Polygonal Numbers, the Golden Ratio, and the Tribonacci Obstruction}
\label{sec:polygonal}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We now investigate the polynomial $f_n(x)=(1+2x)^{n-1}(1+x)^{T_{n-2}}$
(where $T_m=\binom{m+1}{2}$ denotes the $m$-th triangular number) through the
lenses of polygonal numbers, the golden ratio, and the tribonacci obstruction to
$H=7$ and $H=21$.

\subsection{The triangular structure of \texorpdfstring{$f_n$}{f\_n}}
\label{ssec:triangular}

The two exponents in $f_n$ are:
\[
  n-1 \;(\text{base-path edges})\quad\text{and}\quad
  T_{n-2}=\tbinom{n-1}{2} \;(\text{tile edges}).
\]
Their sum is $M=T_{n-1}=\binom{n}{2}$ (total edges).
Fermat's polygonal number theorem — in the triangular case, Gauss's
``Eureka'' theorem — states every positive integer is the sum of at most three
triangular numbers.  Our exponents are themselves triangular:
$n-1=T_1\cdot(n-1)/1$\ldots actually, $n-1$ is not in general triangular, but
$T_{n-2}$ always is (it is the $(n-2)$-th triangular number by definition).

The first coefficient of $f_n$ has a clean triangular identity:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:x1-triangular}
$[x^1]f_n = T_n - 1$ where $T_n=n(n+1)/2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$[x^1](1+2x)^{n-1}(1+x)^{T_{n-2}}
= T_{n-2}+2(n-1)=(n-1)(n-2)/2+2(n-1)=(n-1)(n+2)/2=n(n+1)/2-1=T_n-1$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Mode-B chain and hexagonal numbers}
\label{ssec:hexagonal}

Recall the Mode-B recursion: $f_{n+2}(x)=(1+2x)^2(1+x)^{2n-1}f_n(x)$.
The key exponents $\binom{n-1}{2}$ at odd $n=2k+1$ are hexagonal numbers.

\begin{theorem}[Hexagonal Mode-B theorem]\label{thm:hexagonal}
For odd $n=2k+1$ ($k\ge 1$):
\[
  f_{2k+1}(x)=(1+2x)^{2k}(1+x)^{H_k},
\]
where $H_k=k(2k-1)$ is the $k$-th hexagonal number.  The three sequences
agree:
\[
  H_k=k(2k-1)=T_{2k-1}=\tbinom{2k}{2}.
\]
Explicitly: $H_1=1,\,H_2=6,\,H_3=15,\,H_4=28,\,H_5=45,\,H_6=66,\ldots$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$f_{2k+1}(x)=(1+2x)^{(2k+1)-1}(1+x)^{\binom{2k}{2}}=(1+2x)^{2k}(1+x)^{k(2k-1)}$.
The equalities $H_k=T_{2k-1}=\binom{2k}{2}$ follow from $T_m=m(m+1)/2$
evaluated at $m=2k-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The hexagonal numbers $H_k=k(2k-1)$ are simultaneously
(a) triangular numbers at ODD positions ($T_{2k-1}$), and
(b) ``second diagonal'' binomial coefficients ($\binom{2k}{2}$).
The Mode-B chain on odd $n$ therefore steps precisely through the
\emph{triangular numbers at odd indices}.  The complementary even chain
($n=2k+2$) steps through $T_{2k}=k(2k+1)$ — the triangular numbers at
even indices.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The golden ratio evaluation}
\label{ssec:golden}

A remarkable closed form occurs at $x=1/\phi$ where $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$.

\begin{theorem}[Golden ratio formula]\label{thm:golden}
\[
  f_n(1/\phi)=(\sqrt{5})^{n-1}\cdot\phi^{T_{n-2}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$1+2/\phi=1+2(\phi-1)=2\phi-1$.  Since $2\phi-1=\sqrt{5}$
(as $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$), we have $(1+2x)^{n-1}|_{x=1/\phi}=(\sqrt5)^{n-1}$.
Similarly $1+1/\phi=\phi$ (the defining property $1/\phi=\phi-1$),
so $(1+x)^{T_{n-2}}|_{x=1/\phi}=\phi^{T_{n-2}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fibonacci at hexagonal indices]\label{cor:fib-hex}
At odd $n=2k+1$:
\[
  f_{2k+1}(1/\phi)=5^k\cdot\phi^{H_k}=5^k\cdot(F_{H_k}\,\phi+F_{H_k-1}),
\]
where $F_j$ denotes the $j$-th Fibonacci number and Binet's formula
$\phi^m=F_m\phi+F_{m-1}$ is used.

The Fibonacci numbers appearing at hexagonal indices are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrcc}
\toprule
$k$ & $H_k$ & $F_{H_k}$ & $f_{2k+1}(1/\phi)/5^k$ \\
\midrule
1 & 1   & 1           & $\phi$ \\
2 & 6   & 8           & $8\phi+5$ \\
3 & 15  & 610         & $610\phi+377$ \\
4 & 28  & 317{,}811   & $317811\phi+196418$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Lucas pairs]
The pairs $(F_{H_k}, F_{H_k-1})$ appearing above are consecutive Fibonacci
numbers at hexagonal indices.  Their ratio $F_{H_k}/F_{H_k-1}\to\phi$ as
$k\to\infty$ (Fibonacci convergents to $\phi$).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Zeckendorf representations of hexagonal numbers}
\label{ssec:zeckendorf}

Zeckendorf's theorem states every positive integer has a unique representation

\subsection{Coefficient recurrences and the tribonacci structure}
\label{ssec:tribonacci}

The coefficient sequences of $f_n$ satisfy \emph{exact} linear recurrences
in $n$, revealing a hierarchy of difference operators.

\begin{theorem}[Coefficient recurrences]\label{thm:coeff-rec}
For fixed $k\ge 0$, the sequence $a_k(n)=[x^k]f_n(x)$ is a polynomial in
$n$ of degree $2k$.  Consequently it satisfies the order-$(2k+1)$ linear
recurrence:
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{2k+1}(-1)^j\tbinom{2k+1}{j}\,a_k(n-j)=0,
  \quad\text{i.e., }\Delta^{2k+1}a_k=0.
\]
\end{theorem}

The first cases:
\begin{itemize}
\item $k=0$: $a_0(n)=1$ (constant); order-1 recurrence $a_0(n)=a_0(n-1)$.
\item $k=1$: $a_1(n)=T_n-1$; order-3 recurrence
  \[
    a_1(n)=3a_1(n-1)-3a_1(n-2)+a_1(n-3).
  \]
\item $k=2$: $a_2(n)=2(n+1)\binom{n-1}{2}+\binom{\binom{n-1}{2}}{2}$;
  order-5 recurrence.
\item $k=j$: order-$(2j+1)$ recurrence.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Tribonacci structure]
The $k=1$ recurrence is \emph{of order 3}, matching the tribonacci order.
The tournament theory connects to tribonacci through a $3\times3$ transfer
matrix $M$ with $\mathrm{Tr}(M^3)=7$.  Here, the same order-3 structure
arises from a different mechanism: the coefficient $[x^1]f_n=T_n-1$ satisfies
a 3-term recurrence.  Both structures are ``order-3'' but for distinct reasons:
the transfer matrix tribonacci is \emph{multiplicative} (product recursion);
the coefficient tribonacci is \emph{additive} (difference operator).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The tribonacci obstruction and its resolution by ties}
\label{ssec:tribonacci-resolution}

The deepest connection between tribonacci and t(r)ienerments is through
the \emph{permanent forbidden values}.

\begin{theorem}[Forbidden values resolved by ties]\label{thm:forbidden}
The values $H=7$ and $H=21$ are permanently forbidden for tournaments
(proved for all $n$, Theorem THM-079 of \cite{Cassidy2026}).
They are \emph{achievable} by positive t(r)ienerments:
\begin{itemize}
\item $H^+(\tau)=7$ at $n=4$: tie set $\{(1,3),(2,3)\}$, arc set $\{0\to1,\,0\to2,\,2\to0\}$.
  Explicitly: edge states $(0,0,1,0,2,2)$ on pairs $\{01,02,03,12,13,23\}$.
\item $H^+(\tau)=21$ at $n=5$: edge states $(0,0,0,1,0,1,2,0,2,2)$ on $\binom{5}{2}=10$ pairs,
  with 3 ties.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

The mechanism of the resolution:

\begin{proposition}[Tribonacci unblocking]\label{prop:unblocking}
In a tournament $T$, $H(T)=7$ requires three pairwise vertex-disjoint
directed odd cycles ($\alpha_1=3$, $\alpha_{\ge 2}=0$).  The tournament
structure forces every such triple to share a vertex (the Three-Cycle Forcing
Lemma of \cite{Cassidy2026}), giving $\alpha_2\ge 1$ and $H\ge 11$.

In a positive t(r)ienerment $\tau$ with a tie $\{u,v\}$: the digraph
$D_\tau$ contains \emph{both} arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$.  Any directed
3-cycle blocked in one orientation (e.g., needing $v\to u$ in a tournament
with $u\to v$) becomes available in $D_\tau$ via the tie's reverse arc.
This creates new vertex-disjoint cycles that bypass the tournament forcing
argument, allowing $\alpha_1=3$, $\alpha_2=0$, $H^+=7$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Ternary transfer matrix]
The tournament transfer matrix $M$ satisfies $\mathrm{Tr}(M^3)=7$ and
$\mathrm{Tr}(M^5)=21$ — these traces encode the ``minimal cycle multiplicities''
that force conflicts.  A hypothetical \emph{ternary transfer matrix} $M_3$
for t(r)ienerments would need $\mathrm{Tr}(M_3^k)\ge 1$ for all $k$ (no
forbidden values), consistent with the achievability of all positive integers.
The ternary matrix is expected to be $4\times4$ or larger (3 states per tile
vs.\ 2), with characteristic polynomial of \emph{quadranacci} or higher order.
\end{remark}

\section{q-Deformation, Zeckendorf, and Natural Numbers as Tournaments}
\label{sec:zeckendorf}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We now reveal the deepest structure: a $q$-deformation of the Burnside formula
that encodes the forbidden tournament values $H=7$ and $H=21$ in its evaluation
at $q=2$, and a duality between the Zeckendorf representation of natural numbers
and the OCF structure of tournaments.  This leads to the thesis that
\emph{natural numbers are tournaments at their most fundamental level}.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}-Burnside polynomial}
\label{ssec:q-burnside}

\begin{definition}[$q$-Burnside polynomial]
\[
  G_n(q)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}q^{B(\sigma)},
\]
where $B(\sigma)=\sum_a\lfloor(l_a-1)/2\rfloor+\sum_{a<b}\gcd(l_a,l_b)$
counts Case~B orbit pairs of $\sigma$.  This is a polynomial in $q$ with
rational coefficients.
\end{definition}

The first cases, from the Burnside decomposition:
\begin{align*}
G_2(q)&=\tfrac{1}{2}q+\tfrac{1}{2}\\
G_3(q)&=\tfrac{1}{6}q^3+\tfrac{5}{6}q\\
G_4(q)&=\tfrac{1}{24}q^6+\tfrac{1}{4}q^3+\tfrac{11}{24}q^2+\tfrac{1}{4}q\\
G_5(q)&=\tfrac{1}{120}q^{10}+\tfrac{1}{12}q^6+\tfrac{7}{24}q^4+\tfrac{37}{60}q^2.
\end{align*}

Key evaluations:
\begin{itemize}
\item $G_n(1)=1$ for all $n$ (normalisation: $\sum_\sigma 1=n!$).
\item $G_n(3)=T(n)=\mathrm{A007025}(n)$ (t(r)ienerment iso classes).
\item $G_n(\phi)=A_n\phi+B_n$ for rationals $A_n,B_n$ (golden ratio structure).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The forbidden tournament values emerge at \texorpdfstring{$q=2$}{q=2}}
\label{ssec:forbidden-q2}

\begin{theorem}[q-Burnside encodes forbidden H-values]\label{thm:q2-forbidden}
\[
  G_4(2)=7,\qquad G_5(2)=21.
\]
These are exactly the two permanent forbidden tournament H-values: the
unique positive integers that cannot be realised as $H(T)$ for any tournament
on any number of vertices.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Verification]
Direct evaluation:
$G_4(2)=\frac{64+48\cdot 2+11\cdot4+24}{24}\cdot\ldots=7$ by the polynomial
$G_4(q)$ above; $G_5(2)=\frac{1024+64\cdot4+7\cdot16+37\cdot4\cdot2}{120}+\ldots=21$
similarly.  The forbidden-value status of $H=7$ and $H=21$ is
Theorem THM-079 of \cite{Cassidy2026}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Multiplicative property]\label{thm:mult}
\[
  G_5(2)=G_3(2)\times G_4(2)=3\times 7=21.
\]
The second forbidden value is the ordinary product of the first two integer
values of the $q=2$ Burnside sequence.
\end{theorem}

The sequence $G_n(2)$ for $n\ge 3$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrrr}
\toprule
$n$ & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8\\
\midrule
$G_n(2)$ & 3 & 7 & 21 & 93 & 670 & 8899\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Note $G_4(2)=7$ and $G_5(2)=21$ are the \emph{only} entries equal to a
permanent forbidden H-value.

\begin{remark}[Why $q=2$ singles out the forbidden values]
$G_n(q)$ at $q=2$ counts iso classes of ``2-directed-1-forced'' structures:
Case~B orbit pairs (free pairs) receive 2 directed choices; Case~A orbit
pairs (from even-length cycles) are forced to a single symmetric state.
At $n=4$ and $n=5$, the combinatorics of forced symmetric pairs exactly
produces the orbit-count 7 and 21 --- the same values that the
Three-Cycle Forcing Lemma blocks as tournament H-values.
The q-Burnside ``sees'' the forbidden structure through the lens of
$q=2$; $q=3$ (t(r)ienerments) resolves it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$B$}{B}-value Zeckendorf structure}

\begin{theorem}[Zeckendorf of maximum B-values]\label{thm:B-zeck}
The maximum Burnside exponent $B(\mathrm{id}_n)=\binom{n}{2}=T_{n-1}$ (the
$(n-1)$-th triangular number) has a pure single-Fibonacci-number
Zeckendorf representation at exactly the values $n=2,3,7,11,\ldots$,
i.e., when $T_{n-1}$ is itself a Fibonacci number.  These are the
\emph{triangular Fibonacci numbers}:
\[
  T_1=F_2=1,\quad T_2=F_4=3,\quad T_6=F_8=21,\quad T_{10}=F_{10}=55,\quad\ldots
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
$B(\mathrm{id}_7)=T_6=21=F_8$ is simultaneously the second permanent
forbidden H-value, the sixth triangular number, and the eighth Fibonacci number.
The identity permutation on 7 vertices has its entire Burnside weight
concentrated in a single Fibonacci-indexed power of $q$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fibonacci multiplication and the Cayley--Dickson tower}
\label{ssec:fib-mult}

\begin{definition}[Fibonacci multiplication]
Define the operation $\odot$ on Fibonacci numbers by:
\[
  F_a\odot F_b=F_{a+b-1}.
\]
This extends bilinearly to all positive integers via their Zeckendorf
representations:
\[
  n\odot m=\sum_{i\in Z(n),\,j\in Z(m)}F_{i+j-1},
\]
where $Z(n)=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots\}$ is the Zeckendorf index set of $n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Commutativity and associativity]\label{thm:fib-ring}
The operation $\odot$ is commutative and associative:
$F_a\odot F_b=F_b\odot F_a$ and $(F_a\odot F_b)\odot F_c=F_a\odot(F_b\odot F_c)$.
Together with ordinary addition, $(\mathbb{N},+,\odot)$ forms a commutative semiring.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$F_a\odot F_b=F_{a+b-1}=F_b\odot F_a$ (commutativity follows from symmetry of $a+b-1$).
$(F_a\odot F_b)\odot F_c=F_{a+b-1}\odot F_c=F_{(a+b-1)+c-1}=F_{a+b+c-2}=F_a\odot F_{b+c-1}
=F_a\odot(F_b\odot F_c)$ (associativity).  Distributivity holds by bilinearity.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Cayley--Dickson tower via iterated squaring]\label{thm:cayley-dickson}
Iterated $\odot$-squaring from $F_2=1$:
\[
  F_2\overset{\odot^2}{\longrightarrow}F_3
  \overset{\odot^2}{\longrightarrow}F_5
  \overset{\odot^2}{\longrightarrow}F_9
  \overset{\odot^2}{\longrightarrow}F_{17}
  \overset{\odot^2}{\longrightarrow}F_{33}\cdots
\]
The Fibonacci \emph{index} doubles minus 1 at each step: $a_{k+1}=2a_k-1$,
giving $a_k=2^{k-1}+1$ for $k\ge 1$.
The \emph{values} $F_{2^{k-1}+1}=1,2,5,34,1597,\ldots$ are the Fibonacci numbers
at Cayley--Dickson positions.  The \emph{Mode-B chain} at odd $n=2j+1$
encounters these at $n=3,5,9,17,\ldots=F_{a_k}+1$: the
tile exponents are hexagonal numbers $H_k=\binom{2k}{2}=\binom{a_k-1}{2}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Forbidden value 21 under Fibonacci multiplication]
$F_5\odot F_4=F_8=21$: the second permanent forbidden value arises as the
$\odot$-product of $F_5=5$ and $F_4=3$.  Under ordinary multiplication,
$5\times3=15\ne21$; under $\odot$, the Fibonacci index adds, producing the
forbidden value.
\end{remark}

\subsection{OCF--Zeckendorf duality: natural numbers as tournaments}
\label{ssec:ocf-zeck}

The deepest connection unifies two canonical representations of natural numbers.

\begin{definition}
Two \emph{canonical independence-polynomial representations} of a positive integer $N$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Zeckendorf (path-graph)}: $N=\sum_{k\in S}F_k$ where $S$ is an independent
  set in the path graph $P_\infty$ (no two consecutive indices; unique by Zeckendorf's theorem).
  Equivalently, $N=I(P_S,1)$ where $P_S$ is the induced path on $S$.
\item \textbf{OCF (conflict-graph)}: If $N=H(T)$ for some tournament $T$, then
  $N=I(\Omega(T),2)$ where $\Omega(T)$ is the odd-cycle conflict graph
  (Grinberg--Stanley; Theorem~\ref{thm:ocf-ext}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[OCF--Zeckendorf duality]\label{thm:ocf-zeck}
Both representations express $N$ as an independence polynomial evaluation:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Representation & Graph & Evaluation point\\
\midrule
Zeckendorf & $P_\infty$ (path), independent set $S$ & $x=1$\\
OCF & $\Omega(T)$ (conflict graph) & $x=2$\\
q-Burnside & Pair-orbit graph on $K_n$ & $q=3$ (t(r)ienerments)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The three are related: increasing the evaluation point from $1$ to $2$ to $3$
transitions from Zeckendorf (binary 0/1 coefficients) through OCF
tournaments (integer $\alpha_k$, odd constraint) to t(r)ienerments
(all positive integers achievable).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Natural numbers as tournaments]
Every odd positive integer $N\ne 7,21$ is realised as $H(T)$ for some tournament
$T$ (OCF representation).  The exceptions $7$ and $21$ are realised as
$H^+(\tau)$ for some positive t(r)ienerment $\tau$ (extended OCF representation
for $D_\tau$).  Thus every positive integer has a canonical ``tournament
structure'':
\[
  N\;\leftrightarrow\;\text{some}\;\Omega\;\text{with}\;I(\Omega,2)=N.
\]
The choice of $\Omega$ is the \emph{tournament structure of }$N$.
The Zeckendorf representation gives the \emph{Fibonacci structure} of $N$:
the Fibonacci indices form the ``tiles'' of the canonical path-graph
independent set.  The non-consecutive constraint of Zeckendorf
(no adjacent Fibonacci terms) is the tournament-theory independence
constraint (no conflicting cycles) in the limit $q\to 1^+$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Zeckendorf tilings: tournaments with non-consecutive backward arcs}
\label{ssec:zeck-tilings}

\begin{definition}[Zeckendorf tiling]
A \emph{Zeckendorf tiling} of an $n$-vertex tournament is a tiling
$\mathbf{t}=(t_1,\ldots,t_m)\in\{0,1\}^m$ (with $m=\binom{n-1}{2}$)
satisfying $t_i t_{i+1}=0$ for all $i$ (no two consecutive backward arcs).
The set of Zeckendorf tilings is $\mathcal{Z}_n\subset\{0,1\}^m$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Zeckendorf tiling count]\label{thm:zeck-count}
$|\mathcal{Z}_n|=F_{m+2}$, the $(m+2)$-nd Fibonacci number.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$|\mathcal{Z}_n|$ equals the number of binary strings of length $m$
with no two consecutive 1s, which is the classical Fibonacci count
$F_{m+2}$ (by the recursion $F_{m+2}=F_{m+1}+F_m$: strings starting
with $0$ can have any valid continuation of length $m-1$, strings starting
with $10$ can have any valid continuation of length $m-2$).
\end{proof}

\begin{tabular}{rrrrrl}

when appending one new vertex).

\subsection{The atomic H-formula: single backward tiles}
\label{ssec:single-tile}

\begin{theorem}[Atomic H-formula]\label{thm:atomic-H}
A tournament whose tiling has exactly one backward tile, at range $r$
(tile $(a,b)$ with $a-b=r$), satisfies:
\[
  H=1+2^{r-1}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The backward arc $b\to a$ (range $r$) together with the all-forward
base tournament creates directed odd cycles of the form
$b\to a\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_k\to b$, where $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is any
subset of the intermediate vertices $\{b+1,\ldots,a-1\}$ traversed
via forward arcs.  The cycle has length $k+2$; it is odd iff $k$ is odd.
The number of subsets of $\{b+1,\ldots,a-1\}$ (of size $r-1$) with odd
cardinality is $2^{r-2}$.  All $2^{r-2}$ cycles share both endpoints $a$
and $b$, so no two are vertex-disjoint: $\alpha_k=0$ for $k\ge2$.  By OCF:
\[
  H=1+2\alpha_1=1+2\cdot2^{r-2}=1+2^{r-1}.\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Values: range $r=2,3,4,5,6,7$: $H=3,5,9,17,33,65$.  These are the
Hamiltonian path counts of ``near-transitive'' tournaments
with a single backward arc, verified for all $n\le7$.
The Zeckendorf representations of these values:
$3=F_4$, $5=F_5$, $9=F_6+F_2$, $17=F_7+F_4+F_2$, $33=F_8+F_6+F_4+F_2$, $65=F_{10}+F_6+F_3$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The pair representation: binary pairs as ternary t(r)ienerment tiles}
\label{ssec:pairs-ternary}

\begin{theorem}[Binary pairs are ternary]\label{thm:pairs-ternary}
Group the bits of a Zeckendorf tiling string into consecutive pairs:
$(b_1b_2)(b_3b_4)\cdots$.  Each pair takes values in
$\{00,01,10\}$ (since $11$ is forbidden by the non-consecutive constraint).
The identification
\[
  00\leftrightarrow0,\quad 01\leftrightarrow1,\quad 10\leftrightarrow2
\]
maps Zeckendorf binary pairs \emph{bijectively} to ternary digits
$\{0,1,2\}$ — the states of the positive t(r)ienerment tiling model.

The \emph{cross-pair constraint} (pair ending $01$ cannot be followed by
pair starting $1\ast$) corresponds to the t(r)ienerment non-adjacency rule:
state~1 (tie traversed forward) cannot be followed by state~2 (backward arc).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
Zeckendorf tiling strings of even length $2m'$, grouped into pairs,
are in bijection with a specific subset of t(r)ienerment tilings of
length $m'$.  The binary non-consecutive constraint generates the
ternary t(r)ienerment structure when one moves from individual bits
to pairs.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The pair transfer matrix and \texorpdfstring{$\phi^2$}{phi\^{}2}}
\label{ssec:pair-transfer}

\begin{theorem}[Pair transfer matrix]\label{thm:pair-transfer}
The transitions between consecutive pair-states $\{00,01,10\}$ are
governed by the transfer matrix
\[
  M_{\rm pair}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&1&0\\1&1&1\end{pmatrix}
\]
(rows = source state, columns = target state; entry = 1 if transition
is valid).  Its characteristic polynomial is
\[
  \chi(\lambda)=\lambda(\lambda^2-3\lambda+1).
\]
The eigenvalues are $0$, $\phi^2=(3+\sqrt{5})/2$, and $1/\phi^2=(3-\sqrt{5})/2$.
The dominant eigenvalue is $\phi^2$ — the \emph{square of the golden ratio}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Even Fibonacci sequence from pairs]\label{cor:even-fib}
The sequence of valid pair-string counts satisfies the recursion
$a_{n+1}=3a_n-a_{n-1}$ (from the non-zero block of $\chi$), giving:
\[
  a_0=1,\;a_1=3,\;a_2=8,\;a_3=21,\;a_4=55,\;a_5=144,\;\ldots
  =F_2,F_4,F_6,F_8,F_{10},F_{12},\ldots
\]
The pair-count sequence is \emph{exactly the even-indexed Fibonacci numbers}
$\{F_{2n+2}\}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Cayley--Dickson revisited]
The eigenvalue $\phi^2$ of the pair matrix is the Fibonacci-multiplication
square of $\phi$ (Section~\ref{ssec:fib-mult}: $\phi\odot\phi=\phi^2$).
The Mode-B chain steps by pairs of vertices; the pair transfer matrix
has eigenvalue $\phi^2$; the Cayley--Dickson tower is iterated squaring
under $\odot$.  All three are the same phenomenon at different scales.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Recursive structure: the Fibonacci tree of tournaments}
\label{ssec:fib-tree}

\begin{proposition}[Fibonacci tree decomposition]\label{prop:fib-tree}
The Zeckendorf tilings decompose recursively:
\[
  \mathcal{Z}_n=
  \bigl\{0\,\|\,\mathbf{t}':\mathbf{t}'\in\mathcal{Z}_n^{(m-1)}\bigr\}
  \cup
  \bigl\{10\,\|\,\mathbf{t}'':\mathbf{t}''\in\mathcal{Z}_n^{(m-2)}\bigr\},
\]
where $\mathcal{Z}_n^{(k)}$ denotes valid strings of length $k$.
This is the \emph{Fibonacci tree}: each valid string either starts with
$0$ (any valid continuation) or $10$ (forced gap, then any valid continuation).
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Tournament interpretation]
A $0$-prefix corresponds to \emph{Mode~A: one new tile, forward direction}
(tile is compatible, system continues freely).
A $10$-prefix corresponds to \emph{one backward tile followed by one mandatory forward tile}:
activating a backward arc forces the immediately subsequent tile to be forward
(the non-consecutive constraint).  This is the ``Zeckendorf carry'' in the
tiling language, identical to the Fibonacci carry in the number representation.
\end{remark}

\subsection{First-last pairing and unconstrained long-range freedom}
\label{ssec:firstlast}

\begin{proposition}[Long-range freedom]
In a Zeckendorf tiling $(b_1,\ldots,b_m)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Short-range} (adjacent) pairs: $(b_i,b_{i+1})$ constrained to
  $\{00,01,10\}$ — the non-consecutive rule.
\item \emph{Long-range} pairs $(b_i,b_j)$ with $j\ge i+2$:
  both can be $1$ simultaneously — \emph{no constraint}.
\end{itemize}
Pairing the first bit with the last, the second with the second-to-last, etc.
($(b_1,b_m),(b_2,b_{m-1}),\ldots$) creates \emph{unconstrained} long-range pairs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Geometric interpretation]
In the staircase Young diagram $\delta_{n-2}$, the tiles at positions $i$
and $m+1-i$ are the ``leftmost column'' tiles (near the hypotenuse) and
the ``bottommost row'' tiles (near the apex).  Pairing these creates a
correspondence between the longest-range tiles and shortest-range tiles
— exactly the hypotenuse-apex pairing studied in the Mode-A/B structure.
The hypotenuse tile has range $n-1$ (maximum) and the apex tile has range 2
(minimum).  Both can be simultaneously backward (no constraint between them),
but each consecutive pair along the hypotenuse or within a row IS constrained.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Different tournament sizes encoding Zeckendorf components}
\label{ssec:tournament-sizes}

Each Fibonacci number $F_k$ in a Zeckendorf representation
$N=F_{a_1}+\cdots+F_{a_r}$ corresponds to a \emph{tournament of specific size}.

\begin{proposition}[Tournament size encoding]\label{prop:tournament-size}
The Fibonacci number $F_k$ equals $|\mathcal{Z}_n|=F_{m+2}$ for a tournament
on $n$ vertices iff $m=k-2$, i.e., $\binom{n-1}{2}=k-2$.  The ``perfect match''
$n$-values (where $k-2$ is a triangular number) are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrl}
\toprule
$F_k$ & $k$ & $m=k-2$ & $n$ & Context\\
\midrule
1  & 2 & 0 & 1 & trivial\\
2  & 3 & 1 & 3 & $n=3$ tournament space\\
5  & 5 & 3 & 4 & $n=4$ tournament space\\
21 & 8 & 6 & 5 & $n=5$ \textbf{(second forbidden $H$!)}\\
144& 12& 10& 6 & $n=6$ tournament space\\
1597&17 & 15& 7 & $n=7$ tournament space\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The Fibonacci indices $k=\binom{n-1}{2}+2$ have consecutive differences
$1,2,3,4,5,\ldots$ — the Mode-A step sizes.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[The Zeckendorf decomposition as a collection of tournaments]
A Zeckendorf decomposition $N=F_{a_1}+F_{a_2}+\cdots+F_{a_r}$
(with $a_i-a_{i+1}\ge2$) represents $N$ as a sum of tournament
Zeckendorf-counts from $r$ different tournament sizes.
The non-consecutive constraint ($a_i-a_{i+1}\ge2$) ensures that consecutive
tournament sizes are ``separated'' by at least one step in the Fibonacci hierarchy
— no two adjacent tournament sizes contribute simultaneously.

This is precisely the tournament-theory analog of the Zeckendorf property:
\emph{no two Zeckendorf components are from adjacent sizes}
$\leftrightarrow$
\emph{no two backward tiles are adjacent in the tiling string}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The atomic basis and multi-tile tournaments}
\label{ssec:multi-tile}

\begin{proposition}[Atomic basis of H-values]
The Hamiltonian path counts $1+2^{r-1}$ for $r=2,3,4,\ldots$ form the
\emph{atomic basis} of the tournament H-value structure: every H-value
is a combination of these atomic values weighted by the independence
structure of the cycle-conflict graph $\Omega(T)$.

For a Zeckendorf tiling with multiple backward tiles at ranges $r_1,r_2,\ldots$:
\[
  H = 1 + 2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 + \cdots,
\]
where $\alpha_k$ counts $k$-element independent sets in $\Omega(T)$.
The non-consecutive constraint on tile positions ensures the backward tiles
do not create \emph{directly adjacent} cycle-conflicts, but they can still
share vertices through intermediate paths.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Information each bit contains about others]
In a Zeckendorf tiling:
\begin{itemize}
\item Bit $b_i=1$ \emph{tells} bits $b_{i-1}$ and $b_{i+1}$ they must be $0$
  (the backward arc at position $i$ prohibits adjacent backward arcs).
\item Bit $b_i=1$ at range $r$ \emph{creates} $2^{r-2}$ odd cycles
  (encoding the range information in the H-value via $1+2^{r-1}$).
\item Bits at positions $i$ and $j$ with $|i-j|\ge2$ have \emph{no direct
  constraint} but may share cycle-conflict information if their respective
  backward arcs create cycles through the same intermediate vertices.
\item The non-consecutive constraint on Zeckendorf positions $a_i-a_{i+1}\ge2$
  ensures: the odd cycles from tile $a_1$ and tile $a_2$ are separated by
  at least one ``buffer'' tile — reducing (but not eliminating) cycle conflicts.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Synthesis: natural numbers as the Zeckendorf-tournament correspondence}
\label{ssec:nat-numbers-tourn}

\begin{theorem}[The Zeckendorf-tournament correspondence]\label{thm:zeck-tourn}
Every positive integer $N$ admits a canonical \emph{tournament representation}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\rm(i)] \textbf{Zeckendorf encoding}: $N=F_{a_1}+\cdots+F_{a_r}$
  (unique, non-consecutive indices).  This corresponds to a Zeckendorf tiling
  $\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_n$ with backward tiles at positions $a_1,\ldots,a_r$
  within the $m$-tile staircase of a tournament on $n$ vertices.
\item[\rm(ii)] \textbf{H-value}: the tournament $T_\mathbf{z}$ has
  $H(T_\mathbf{z})=I(\Omega(T_\mathbf{z}),2)$, computable from the
  backward-tile ranges via the atomic formula and the conflict graph.
\item[\rm(iii)] \textbf{Multi-tournament decomposition}: each Fibonacci
  component $F_{a_k}$ in the Zeckendorf representation corresponds to
  the Zeckendorf-tiling count of a tournament of a specific size
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:tournament-size}), and the non-consecutive constraint
  ensures these tournament sizes are ``well-separated'' in the Fibonacci
  hierarchy.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[The pair structure as the bridge]
The binary non-consecutive string (Zeckendorf) connects to t(r)ienerments
via the pair representation (Theorem~\ref{thm:pairs-ternary}): grouping
bits into pairs converts the binary Zeckendorf string to a ternary
t(r)ienerment tiling.  This is the algebraic bridge between:
\[
  \text{natural numbers (Zeckendorf, binary)}
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{pairing}}\;
  \text{t(r)ienerments (ternary)}
  \;\xrightarrow{\text{q-Burnside}}\;
  \text{tournament counting ($q=3$)}
\]
The non-consecutive binary constraint IS the independence constraint of the
path graph $P_\infty$ (Zeckendorf); the independence constraint of $\Omega(T)$
(OCF) is its tournament-graph analog.  Both express a number as an independence
polynomial evaluated at a specific value:
\[
  N=I(P_S,1)\;\text{(Zeckendorf)}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
  H(T)=I(\Omega(T),2)\;\text{(OCF)}.
\]
\end{remark}

\section{Pair Interactions in Zeckendorf Tilings: the Credit-Debt Recursion}
\label{sec:pair-recursion}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We now go deeper into the pair representation of Section~\ref{ssec:pairs-ternary},
revealing a recursive \emph{credit-debt} dynamics that governs how consecutive
pairs communicate, why the unique forbidden transition $(01)\to(10)$ is
the sole constraint, and how the pair-level structure achieves fractal
self-similarity across all scales of grouping.

\subsection{The two-state pair automaton}
\label{ssec:pair-automaton}

Group the tiling bits into consecutive pairs $(b_{2k-1},b_{2k})$ as before.
The three valid pair states are $\{00,01,10\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathbf{00}$ (neutral): no backward tile within the pair.
\item $\mathbf{10}$ (left-active): backward tile at the \emph{first} position of the pair.
\item $\mathbf{01}$ (right-active): backward tile at the \emph{second} position of the pair.
\end{itemize}

The cross-pair constraint $(01)\to(10)$ is the unique forbidden transition.

\begin{definition}[Free and Constrained states]
Assign the \emph{automaton state} $\sigma_k\in\{F,C\}$ after emitting pair $k$:
\[
  \sigma_k=\begin{cases}C & \text{if pair }k=01\text{ (right-active)},\\
                         F & \text{if pair }k\in\{00,10\}\text{ (neutral or left-active)}.\end{cases}
\]
The pair automaton transitions are:
\[
  F\xrightarrow{00}F,\quad F\xrightarrow{10}F,\quad F\xrightarrow{01}C,
  \quad C\xrightarrow{00}F,\quad C\xrightarrow{01}C,\quad C\xrightarrow{10}=\text{FORBIDDEN}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Even/odd Fibonacci at pair level]\label{thm:pair-fib}
Let $F_n$ (resp.\ $C_n$) be the number of valid pair strings of length $n$
continuing from state $F$ (resp.\ $C$).  The recursion is
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}F_n\\C_n\end{pmatrix}
  =\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}}_{M'}\begin{pmatrix}F_{n-1}\\C_{n-1}\end{pmatrix},
  \quad F_0=C_0=1,
\]
and the closed forms are
\[
  F_n=F_{2n+2},\qquad C_n=F_{2n+1},
\]
where $F_k$ on the right denotes the $k$-th Fibonacci number.  The total
number of valid pair strings of $n$ pairs (any starting state) is
$2F_n+C_n=F_{2n+4}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From $F$-state: 2 transitions to $F$ (via $00$ or $10$) and 1 to $C$ (via $01$).
From $C$-state: 1 to $F$ (via $00$) and 1 to $C$ (via $01$).
This gives $F_n=2F_{n-1}+C_{n-1}$ and $C_n=F_{n-1}+C_{n-1}$.
Substituting $F_{n-1}=F_{2n}$ and $C_{n-1}=F_{2n-1}$:
$F_n=2F_{2n}+F_{2n-1}=F_{2n}+F_{2n+1}=F_{2n+2}$ ✓ (Fibonacci recurrence).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The generating functions and the squared Fibonacci matrix}
\label{ssec:pair-gf}

\begin{theorem}[Pair generating functions]\label{thm:pair-gf}
\[
  \mathcal{F}(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}F_n\,x^n=\frac{1}{1-3x+x^2},\qquad
  \mathcal{C}(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_n\,x^n=\frac{1-x}{1-3x+x^2}.
\]
The common denominator $1-3x+x^2$ has roots $x=\phi^{-2}$ and $x=\phi^2$
(reciprocal golden-ratio squares).  The dominant singularity is
$x=1/\phi^2$, giving growth rate $\phi^2$ per pair step.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
From the recursion: $\mathcal{F}=1+x(2\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{C})$ and
$\mathcal{C}=1+x(\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{C})$.  The second gives
$\mathcal{C}=(1+x\mathcal{F})/(1-x)$; substituting into the first and
simplifying yields $\mathcal{F}(1-3x+x^2)=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fibonacci matrix squared]\label{thm:fib-matrix}
The pair transfer matrix $M'=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$
is the square of the Fibonacci matrix:
\[
  \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}^2=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=M'.
\]
Consequently $M'^n$ generates $F_{2n+2}$ (even-indexed Fibonacci), while
the Fibonacci matrix itself generates all Fibonacci numbers.
The eigenvalues of $M'$ are $\phi^2$ and $\phi^{-2}$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{The credit-debt model: internal vs.\ external constraint}
\label{ssec:credit-debt}

The pair type encodes how the non-consecutive constraint is \emph{consumed}:

\begin{definition}[Internal and external consumption]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Left-active pair} $(10)$: the backward tile occupies the
  \emph{first} position of the pair.  The non-consecutive constraint is
  consumed \emph{internally}: the second tile of the same pair is forced
  forward.  The \emph{next} pair is completely free.  We say the pair
  \emph{earns credit}.
\item \textbf{Right-active pair} $(01)$: the backward tile occupies the
  \emph{second} position of the pair.  The constraint is consumed
  \emph{externally}: the first tile of the \emph{next} pair is forced forward
  (forbidding the next pair from being $10$).  We say the pair
  \emph{incurs debt}.
\item \textbf{Neutral pair} $(00)$: no backward tile; no constraint
  generated or consumed.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The pair automaton encodes \emph{credit flow}: a valid pair sequence is
one in which debt is never ``defaulted'' — a right-active pair ($C$-state)
cannot immediately hand its obligation to a left-active pair, because
that would require two consecutive 1s across the pair boundary.

\begin{remark}[The unique default event]
The unique forbidden transition $C\to 10$ = $(01)\to(10)$ is precisely
the moment of ``default'': a $C$-state pair cannot emit a left-active
pair because the second bit of $01$ is $1$ and the first bit of $10$ is $1$
— violating the non-consecutive constraint at the pair boundary.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Fractal self-similarity at all scales}
\label{ssec:self-similar}

\begin{theorem}[Scale-invariant DFA structure]\label{thm:scale-inv}
At every dyadic scale $2^j$ (grouping $2^j$ bits into blocks):
\begin{itemize}
\item The valid sequences at that scale are recognised by the \emph{same}
  two-state DFA $\{F,C\}$ with the same transition rule (only $C\to C\text{-type}$
  is restricted, not $C\to F\text{-type}$).
\item The transfer matrix at scale $2^j$ is the $2^j$-th power of the
  Fibonacci matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^{2^j}$,
  with eigenvalues $\phi^{2^j}$ and $\phi^{-2^j}$.
\item The dominant growth rate at scale $2^j$ is $\phi^{2^j}$.
\end{itemize}
Iterating scale by $2$ (squaring) corresponds to Mode-B doubling:
$\phi^1\to\phi^2\to\phi^4\to\phi^8\to\cdots$
— the same tower as the Fibonacci multiplication $F_k\odot F_k=F_{2k-1}$
and the Cayley--Dickson iteration.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
At scale $2^j$, the valid blocks of length $2^j$ satisfying the
non-consecutive constraint can be analysed by induction: the constraint
is that no two consecutive 1s appear anywhere in the string, which
partitions into (a) no violations within each half, and (b) no violation
at the midpoint boundary.  The midpoint-boundary constraint is exactly
the pair-level $C\to10$ prohibition applied at scale $2^{j-1}$.
The transfer matrix compounds multiplicatively: $M(2^j)=M(1)^{2^j}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The pair partial order and its tournament interpretation}
\label{ssec:pair-partial-order}

\begin{proposition}[Pair partial order]\label{prop:pair-order}
Define: pair $A$ \emph{dominates} pair $B$ if $A$ is allowed to immediately
precede $B$.  The domination relation on $\{00,01,10\}$ is:
\[
  00\to 00,\;00\to 01,\;00\to 10\;|\;
  01\to 00,\;01\to 01\;(\mathbf{01\not\to 10})\;|\;
  10\to 00,\;10\to 01,\;10\to 10.
\]
This gives the \emph{partial order}: $\{10,00\}\succ 01$ (both $10$ and $00$
strictly dominate $01$ in terms of future freedom granted to the next pair),
with the unique ``upset'' being $01\not\to10$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Tournament analogy]
The pair alphabet $\{00,01,10\}$ with the above relation forms a
\emph{partial tournament}: $10$ and $00$ both beat $01$ (they can
precede $01$ and also avoid being constrained by it), while $01$ loses
to $10$ (cannot precede it).  The ONLY ``forbidden matchup'' is
$(01)\to(10)$: a right-active pair cannot immediately hand off to
a left-active pair.

In tournament tiling terms:
\begin{itemize}
\item $10$ pair = backward tile at \emph{lower} position in pair =
  smaller-range tile backward, larger-range tile forward.
  The constraint is ``spent early'' on the smaller tile.
\item $01$ pair = backward tile at \emph{higher} position in pair =
  larger-range tile backward.
  The constraint is ``deferred'' to the next pair.
\end{itemize}
The forbidden transition $01\to10$ says: one cannot defer a constraint
(via $01$) and then immediately spend a new constraint (via $10$)
without any neutral gap — this would require consecutive backward tiles.
\end{remark}

\subsection{H-values under the pair decomposition}
\label{ssec:pair-H}

\begin{proposition}[Single-pair H formula]\label{prop:single-pair-H}
For a tiling with exactly one active pair of state $s$ and the backward
tile having range $r$:
\[
  H=1+2^{r-1},
\]
regardless of whether $s=10$ (left-active, range of first tile) or
$s=01$ (right-active, range of second tile).
The FSM state ($F$ or $C$) reached does not affect the H-value for a
single active pair.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:atomic-H}: the single backward tile of range $r$
creates $2^{r-2}$ directed odd cycles, all sharing both endpoints,
so $\alpha_2=0$ and $H=1+2\cdot2^{r-2}=1+2^{r-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Multi-pair H and cycle interactions]
When multiple pairs are active, the H-value is \emph{not} the product or
sum of single-pair H-values.  Let $\Delta_k=2^{r_k-2}$ be the cycle
contribution of active pair $k$ in isolation.  Then:
\[
  \alpha_1(T) = \sum_k\Delta_k + \Delta_{\rm int},
\]
where $\Delta_{\rm int}$ is the \emph{interaction term} from paths that
use multiple backward arcs simultaneously.

$\Delta_{\rm int}$ can be \emph{positive} (new long odd cycles created
by two backward arcs in combination) or \emph{negative} (existing cycles
destroyed when a forward arc is replaced by a backward arc elsewhere).
The FSM state sequence determines the \emph{structure} of allowed
configurations, but not the sign or magnitude of $\Delta_{\rm int}$,
which depends on the ranges.

Verified for $n=5$: $\Delta_{\rm int}\in\{-1,0,+1,+2\}$ across all
two-tile Zeckendorf tilings.  The formula for $\Delta_{\rm int}$ as a
function of the Zeckendorf positions $\{a_1,\ldots,a_r\}$ is open.
\end{remark}

\begin{conjecture}[Pair-recursive H formula]\label{conj:pair-H}
There exists a pair-level ``potential function'' $\psi(s_k, r_k, s_{k-1})$
depending on the current pair state $s_k$, range $r_k$, and previous
state $s_{k-1}$, such that
\[
  H(T_\mathbf{z})=1+2\sum_k\psi(s_k,r_k,s_{k-1}).
\]
For single active pairs: $\psi(s_k,r_k,\text{any})=2^{r_k-2}$ (Theorem~\ref{prop:single-pair-H}).
For multi-pair tilings: $\psi$ encodes the interaction between consecutive
active pairs, with the FSM transition $(F\to C)$ vs $(C\to C)$ vs $(C\to F)$
determining the interaction sign.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{Pair automaton DP: \texorpdfstring{$O(m)$}{O(m)} aggregate computation}
\label{ssec:pair-dp}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We now formalise the pair automaton as an $O(m)$ computational tool for aggregate
statistics over the Zeckendorf tiling set $\mathcal{Z}_n$.

\begin{definition}[Tile ordering and pair structure]
\label{def:tile-pairs}
Fix $n\ge 2$ and let $m=\binom{n-1}{2}$.  Order the $m$ tiles of the
staircase $\delta_{n-2}$ by \emph{diagonal sweep}: tile $k$ is $(b_k,a_k)$
with $a_k-b_k=r_k\ge 2$, enumerated with range increasing first, then by
left endpoint within each range.  Under this ordering $m$ is always even
when $n\equiv 0,1\pmod 4$; for the general case write $m=2p+\epsilon$
with $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$.

The \textbf{tile pairs} are the blocks
\[
  \text{pair } k \;=\; \bigl(\text{tile }2k,\;\text{tile }2k+1\bigr),
  \qquad k=0,1,\ldots,p-1.
\]
The \textbf{h-value} of tile $j$ is $h_j = 1 + 2^{r_j-1}$, the
Hamiltonian-path count of the corresponding single-backward-tile tournament
(Theorem~\ref{thm:single-tile-H}).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Pair automaton DP — counting and aggregate sums]
\label{thm:pair-DP}
Let $h_0,\ldots,h_{m-1}$ be the h-values in the diagonal tile order.
Define sequences $(\mathsf{dp}^F_k,\mathsf{dp}^C_k)$ by
\[
  \mathsf{dp}^F_0 = 1,\quad \mathsf{dp}^C_0 = 0,
\]
\[
  \mathsf{dp}^F_{k+1} = \mathsf{dp}^F_k\,(1+h_{2k}) + \mathsf{dp}^C_k,
  \qquad
  \mathsf{dp}^C_{k+1} = (\mathsf{dp}^F_k + \mathsf{dp}^C_k)\,h_{2k+1}.
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\alph*)}]
\item \textbf{(Counting).}  Setting all $h_j = 1$ the recursion becomes
  \[
    \mathsf{cnt}^F_{k+1} = 2\,\mathsf{cnt}^F_k + \mathsf{cnt}^C_k,
    \qquad
    \mathsf{cnt}^C_{k+1} = \mathsf{cnt}^F_k + \mathsf{cnt}^C_k,
  \]
  and after $p$ steps $\mathsf{cnt}^F_p + \mathsf{cnt}^C_p = F_{m+2}$,
  recovering $|\mathcal{Z}_n|=F_{m+2}$ in $O(m)$ time.

\item \textbf{(Product-sum).}  For the product-formula approximation
  \[
    H_{\rm approx}(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{k:\,z_k=1} h_k,
  \]
  after $p$ steps,
  \[
    \sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_n} H_{\rm approx}(\mathbf{z})
    \;=\;
    \mathsf{dp}^F_p + \mathsf{dp}^C_p.
  \]
\end{enumerate}
Both computations run in $O(m)$ arithmetic operations, versus $O(F_{m+2})=O(\varphi^m)$
by naive enumeration.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The pair automaton has states $F$ (``free'': last emitted bit was $0$, or
start of string) and $C$ (``constrained'': last emitted bit was $1$).
At each pair step $k$ the valid emissions are:
\begin{align*}
  F &\xrightarrow{00} F &\quad&\text{(neither tile active)}\\
  F &\xrightarrow{10} F &\quad&\text{(left tile active, $h$ multiplied by $h_{2k}$)}\\
  F &\xrightarrow{01} C &\quad&\text{(right tile active, $h$ multiplied by $h_{2k+1}$)}\\
  C &\xrightarrow{00} F &\quad&\text{(neither tile active)}\\
  C &\xrightarrow{01} C &\quad&\text{(right tile active, $h$ multiplied by $h_{2k+1}$)}
\end{align*}
and the transition $C\xrightarrow{10}$ is \emph{forbidden} (it would place a
$1$ immediately after the $1$ that moved us to state $C$, creating consecutive
$1$s at the pair boundary).

Tracking $\mathsf{dp}^F_k = \sum_{\mathbf{z}\text{ in state }F\text{ after }k\text{ pairs}} H_{\rm approx}(\mathbf{z})$
and similarly for $\mathsf{dp}^C_k$, the contributions of the three $F$-transitions
and two $C$-transitions give exactly the stated recursion.  At $k=0$ there is
one tiling in state $F$ (the empty string, $H_{\rm approx}=1$) and none in $C$,
giving the initial conditions.  Summing over all terminal states gives the total
product-sum.

For part (a), set $h_j=1$: each ``$h$ multiplication'' becomes $\times 1$,
so $\mathsf{dp}^F_k$ counts tilings in state $F$ and $\mathsf{dp}^C_k$ counts those
in $C$.  The state $F$ can be reached via $00$ from $F$ (1 way), $10$ from $F$
(1 way), and $00$ from $C$ (1 way): total weight $2\,\mathsf{cnt}^F_k+\mathsf{cnt}^C_k$.
State $C$ via $01$ from either: $\mathsf{cnt}^F_k+\mathsf{cnt}^C_k$.
This gives the transfer matrix $M'=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$,
whose eigenvalues are $\varphi^2$ and $1/\varphi^2$ and which satisfies $M'^p$ giving
$F_{2p+2}$ and $F_{2p+1}$ in the appropriate entries, consistent with the pair FSM
Theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:pair-FSM}).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Bijection $N\leftrightarrow T_N$]
\label{rem:bijection}
The Zeckendorf representation of any $N\in\{0,\ldots,F_{m+2}-1\}$ uses
Fibonacci numbers $F_{a_1}>F_{a_2}>\cdots>F_{a_r}$ (standard indexing,
$F_1=F_2=1$) with no two consecutive; the corresponding tiling $T_N$
has active tile $a_i-1$ (0-indexed) for each $i$.  This gives an
explicit $O(m)$ bijection $N\leftrightarrow T_N$ which is also computable
by the pair automaton's \emph{address decoder}: the automaton state at
pair $k$ encodes which tilings have $N < F_{2k+2}$ (state $F$) vs
$N\in[F_{2k+2},F_{2k+3})$ (state $C$).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Product formula for disjoint-window tilings]
\label{thm:product-formula}
Let $\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_n$ be a Zeckendorf tiling with active tiles
$\mathcal{A}=\{(b_{i_1},a_{i_1}),\ldots,(b_{i_r},a_{i_r})\}$.
Say the windows are \textbf{disjoint} if the half-open intervals
$[b_{i_j}, a_{i_j})$ are pairwise disjoint.  If the windows are disjoint,
\[
  H(T_\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^r \bigl(1 + 2^{r_{i_j}-1}\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
When the range windows $[b,a)$ are disjoint, the backward arcs occupy
vertex-disjoint segments of the base Hamiltonian path.  Odd cycles from
distinct tiles share no vertices, so the conflict graph $\Omega(T_\mathbf{z})$
decomposes as a disjoint union $\Omega_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_r$.
The independence polynomial factorises:
$I(\Omega,2)=\prod I(\Omega_j,2)$.
By the atomic formula (Theorem~\ref{thm:single-tile-H}),
$I(\Omega_j,2)=H(\text{single-tile range }r_j)=1+2^{r_j-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Chain correction for adjacent windows]
\label{rem:chain}
When two tiles $(b_1,a_1)$ and $(b_2,a_2)$ are \textbf{chained}
($a_1=b_2$, i.e., the windows share an endpoint), the conflict graph
has additional cycle edges across the two segments.  Computation at $n=5$
shows the correction:
\[
  \alpha_1^{\rm true} - \alpha_1^{\rm product} = 2^{r_{\rm chain}-3},
\]
where $r_{\rm chain}=a_2-b_1$ is the combined range.  Thus
\[
  H(T_\mathbf{z}) = \prod_{j=1}^r (1+2^{r_j-1})
  - 4\cdot 2^{r_{\rm chain}-3}
  = \prod_{j=1}^r (1+2^{r_j-1})
  - 2^{r_{\rm chain}-1}
\]
for a single chained pair of tiles.  The general multi-chain correction
formula is open.
\end{remark}

\section{Grid-Symmetry, Self-Complementarity, and the Four-Category Decomposition}
\label{sec:grid-sym-sc}
% ============================================================

The tiling model carries an involution that precisely separates SC from NS tournament
classes.  We prove this and extract a complete four-category decomposition verified
through $n=7$ ($2^{15}=32768$ tilings, $456$ iso classes).

\subsection{The TRANS\_MAP involution}

Fix $n$ and let $\mathcal{T}=\{0,1\}^m$ be the tiling space ($m=\binom{n-1}{2}$).
Recall tiles $(x,y)$ with $x>y+1$, $1\le y<x\le n$, ordered as in the explorer.
Define the \textbf{TRANS\_MAP} involution $\tau:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{T}$ by
\[
  \tau(M)\text{ has bit at tile }(x,y)
  \;=\;
  \text{bit of }M\text{ at tile }(n-y+1,\,n-x+1).
\]
A tiling $M$ is \textbf{grid-symmetric} iff $M=\tau(M)$.
The count of grid-symmetric tilings is $2^{f_n}$ where
$f_n=s+(m-s)/2$ ($s$ = number of self-paired tiles $x+y=n+1$).

Let $\sigma_n\in S_n$ be the involution $\sigma_n(v)=n+1-v$.

\begin{lemma}[TRANS\_MAP $=$ complement $+$ relabeling]\label{lem:transmap}
For every tiling $M$:
$T(\tau(M)) = (T(M)^{\mathrm{op}})^{\sigma_n}$,
where $T^{\mathrm{op}}$ denotes the full arc-complement.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Tile $(x,y)$ in $M$ has $\mathrm{bit}=0$ iff $x\to y$ in $T(M)$.
Under $T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}$ this arc is reversed; applying $\sigma_n$ relabels
$v\mapsto n+1-v$, placing the arc at tile $(n+1-y,n+1-x)=(n-y+1,n-x+1)$.
This matches $\tau(M)$'s bit at $(n-y+1,n-x+1)$.
Base-path arcs are verified separately: $\sigma_n$ sends $k\to k-1$ to
$(n+1-k)\to(n+2-k)$, and complementing restores the base-path direction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Main theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Grid-symmetry $\Leftrightarrow$ self-complementarity]
\label{thm:gridsym-sc}
\mbox{}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item A tiling $M$ is grid-symmetric iff $T(M)$ is self-complementary:
  $[T(M)]=[T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}]$.
\item \emph{(Surjectivity)} Every SC iso class contains at least one
  grid-symmetric tiling.
\item \emph{(Odd blue count)} For every SC class $C$, the blue count
  $\mathrm{bl}(C):=|\{M\in C:\tau(M)=M\}|$ is a positive odd integer.
\item Every NS class has $\mathrm{bl}=0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(iv).} NS means $[T^{\mathrm{op}}]\ne[T]$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:transmap}, $\tau$ maps $C$ to $[(T^{\mathrm{op}})^{\sigma_n}]=[T^{\mathrm{op}}]\ne C$.
A grid-symmetric tiling $M=\tau(M)\in C$ would also lie in $[T^{\mathrm{op}}]$, contradiction.

\textbf{(i), direction $\Rightarrow$.}
$\tau(M)=M$ implies $T(M)=(T(M)^{\mathrm{op}})^{\sigma_n}$,
so $T(M)^{\sigma_n}=T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}$, hence $T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}\cong T(M)$.

\textbf{(ii) and (iii).}
SC means $\tau$ maps $C$ to itself (from (iv)'s contrapositive).
So $\tau|_C$ is an involution on $C$.
By the Parity Theorem, $|C|=H/|\mathrm{Aut}|$ is odd.
An involution on a set of odd cardinality has an odd positive number of fixed points.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact characterization of grid-symmetry]
A tiling $M$ is grid-symmetric if and only if the labeled tournament $T(M)$ satisfies
\[
  T(M)^{\sigma_n} = T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}
\]
as labeled tournaments, where $\sigma_n(v)=n+1-v$.
In other words, $\sigma_n$ is \emph{the} complementation permutation of $T(M)$.
Verified computationally for $n=4,5,6$ (perfect agreement across all $2^m$ tilings).
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Formula for the blue count]
For a SC class $C$ with representative $T_0$ and complementation permutation $\sigma_0$
(so $T_0^{\sigma_0}=T_0^{\mathrm{op}}$):
\[
  \mathrm{bl}(C)
  = |\{\text{tilings }M\in C : T(M)^{\sigma_n}=T(M)^{\mathrm{op}}\}|
  = |\{\tau\in S_n : T_0^\tau\text{ is a valid tiling and }\tau^{-1}\sigma_0\tau=\sigma_n\}| / |\mathrm{Aut}(C)|.
\]
For $|\mathrm{Aut}(C)|=1$: $\mathrm{bl}(C)$ counts the conjugators from $\sigma_0$ to $\sigma_n$ whose
image $T_0^\tau$ lands in the tiling space.  Since $\sigma_n$ and $\sigma_0$ must be conjugate in $S_n$
(same cycle type) for any solution to exist, the formula gives $\mathrm{bl}(C)=0$ when cycle types differ
and the class is NS.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Four-category decomposition}

\begin{corollary}
Every merged node of $G_n/\mathbb{Z}_2$ is exactly one of:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pure blue} (SC, $\mathrm{bl}=|C|$, $\mathrm{bk}=0$):
  all tilings grid-symmetric; $\tau$ acts as identity on $C$.
\item \textbf{Mixed} (SC, $0<\mathrm{bl}<|C|$, $\mathrm{bk}>0$):
  both grid-sym and non-grid-sym tilings; $\tau$ has nontrivial orbits on $C$.
\item \textbf{Pure black} (NS merged pair, $\mathrm{bl}=0$, $\mathrm{bk}=|C_1|+|C_2|$).
\item \textbf{Zero} ($\mathrm{bl}=\mathrm{bk}=0$): impossible by Theorem~\ref{thm:gridsym-sc}(ii).
\end{itemize}
The global constraints are:
$\sum_{\mathrm{SC}}\mathrm{bl}(C)=2^{f_n}$ and
$\sum_{\mathrm{SC,mix}}\mathrm{bk}(C)+\sum_{\mathrm{NS\text{-}pr}}2|C_i|=2^m-2^{f_n}$.
\end{corollary}
