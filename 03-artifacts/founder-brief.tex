\section{The Problem and the Formula}

A \textbf{tournament} is a complete directed graph: every pair has a winner. Think elections,
round-robin leagues, pairwise preference surveys. The question open since R\'{e}dei (1934):
\emph{how many directed Hamiltonian paths does a tournament on $n$ vertices have?}

Call this $H(T)$. R\'{e}dei proved $H(T)$ is always odd; we now have a closed-form:
\vspace{-1pt}
\begin{equation*}
  \boxed{H(T) \;=\; I\!\left(\Omega(T),\, 2\right) \;=\; 1 + 2\alpha_1 + 4\alpha_2 + 8\alpha_3 + \cdots}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-3pt}

$\Omega(T)$ is the \emph{odd-cycle conflict graph}: vertices are odd cycles of $T$, edges connect
pairs sharing a vertex; $\alpha_k$ counts independent $k$-sets (pairwise vertex-disjoint odd cycles).
Proved by Grinberg--Stanley (arXiv:2412.10572, 2024); \textbf{our system rediscovered it via six
independent routes} before finding the preprint, then fully verified it through $n\leq 8$
(8\,M$+$ tournaments, zero failures).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ranking entropy:} $H(T)$ measures how contested a ranking is.
    Large $H$: many valid orderings coexist. Small $H$: outcome nearly forced.
    The $\alpha_k$ decomposition reveals structure invisible in $H$ alone.
  \item \textbf{Forbidden values (proved):} $H(T) = 7$ is \emph{impossible} for \emph{any}
    tournament on \emph{any} number of vertices — a direct OCF consequence proved via the
    combinatorics of conflicting odd-cycle triples. $H=21$ is conjectured similarly forbidden
    (verified exhaustively through $n=7$; 2.1\,M tournaments, zero instances).
  \item \textbf{Deletion-contraction} (proved, any digraph):
    $H(D) = H(D\setminus e) + H(D/e)$.
    Enables divide-and-conquer at $O(2^w\cdot n)$ for treewidth-$w$ graphs.
\end{itemize}

GLMY path homology gives Betti numbers $\beta_i(T)$ to any digraph.
$T_7\!: (\ldots) = (1,0,0,0,\mathbf{6},0,0)$, $\chi=7$;\;

\subsection*{3.\ Instant MCMC — full mixing dynamics in $D{+}1$ coefficients \textnormal{(novel)}}

The random arc-flip Markov chain on tournaments has a remarkable closed form.
