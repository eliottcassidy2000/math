\section{Background}

\begin{definition}
A \textbf{tournament} on $n$ vertices is a directed complete graph: for every pair of distinct vertices $\{u, v\}$, exactly one of the arcs $u \to v$ or $v \to u$ is present. Two tournaments are \textbf{isomorphic} if one can be obtained from the other by relabeling vertices.
\end{definition}

\noindent The sequence $T(n)$ counting non-isomorphic tournaments on $n$ vertices is OEIS \href{https://oeis.org/A000568}{A000568}:
\[
1,\; 1,\; 1,\; 2,\; 4,\; 12,\; 56,\; 456,\; 6880,\; 191536,\; 9733056,\; \ldots
\]

Tournaments arise throughout mathematics: as round-robin competition schedules, as complete antisymmetric binary relations, as orientations of the complete graph, and as comparison-based decision systems. Every tournament has an odd number of Hamiltonian paths (R\'{e}dei, 1934), a fact central to the deeper theory.

\subsection{History}

The exact formula for $T(n)$ was first published by \textbf{R.~L.~Davis} in 1954 (``Structure of dominance relations,'' \emph{Bull.\ Math.\ Biophys.}\ 16), derived independently by Harary (1957), and is standard material in Moon's \emph{Topics on Tournaments} (1968) and Harary--Palmer's \emph{Graphical Enumeration} (1973). Implementations in Maple, PARI, Mathematica, and Python exist on the OEIS page (Jovovic 2001, Howroyd 2017, Alcover 2017, Wu 2024).

\noindent\textbf{This document poses three escalating challenges:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Implement} the known formula. (Problem~\ref{prob:formula})
  \item \textbf{Optimize} it to handle $n = 200$. (Problem~\ref{prob:optimize})
  \item \textbf{Break through} with a sub-exponential algorithm. (Problem~\ref{prob:truncated})
\end{enumerate}
The first two are engineering. The third is mathematics.

\begin{problem}[\textbf{Davis's Formula}]\label{prob:formula}

The following result is due to Davis (1954). We present it in two equivalent forms.

\begin{theorem}[Davis's Formula for $T(n)$]\label{thm:davis}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:davis}
T(n) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{\lambda \vdash n \\ \text{all parts odd}}} \frac{2^{c(\lambda)}}{z_\lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = (l_1^{m_1}, l_2^{m_2}, \ldots)$ is a partition of $n$ in exponential notation (part $l_i$ with multiplicity $m_i$), the sum runs only over partitions where every part is odd, the \textbf{cycle exponent} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-exp}
c(\lambda) \;=\; \sum_i m_i \left\lfloor \frac{l_i}{2} \right\rfloor + \sum_i \binom{m_i}{2} l_i + \sum_{i < j} m_i m_j \gcd(l_i, l_j),
\end{equation}
and the \textbf{centralizer order} is $z_\lambda = \prod_i l_i^{m_i} \cdot m_i!$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Derivation]
Apply Burnside's lemma to the action of $S_n$ on the set of $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$ labeled tournaments. A permutation $\sigma$ with cycle type $\lambda$ acts on unordered pairs $\{i,j\}$ by $\sigma(\{i,j\}) = \{\sigma(i), \sigma(j)\}$. A tournament is fixed by $\sigma$ iff each orbit of pairs receives a consistent orientation.

\emph{Even cycle obstruction:} If $\sigma$ contains a cycle of even length $l$, some pair orbits within that cycle force contradictory orientations (the orbit has even length, but arc reversal is an involution of order 2). Thus $\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma) = 0$ for any $\sigma$ with an even cycle.

\emph{Odd cycles:} When all cycles have odd length, every pair orbit is consistent, and $\mathrm{Fix}(\sigma) = 2^{c(\lambda)}$ where $c(\lambda)$ counts orbits on pairs. Within a single $l$-cycle: $\lfloor l/2 \rfloor$ orbits. Between an $l_a$-cycle and an $l_b$-cycle: $\gcd(l_a, l_b)$ orbits. Summing gives~\eqref{eq:cycle-exp}.

The conjugacy class of $\lambda$ has $n!/z_\lambda$ elements. Burnside's lemma gives
\[
T(n) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_\lambda \frac{n!}{z_\lambda} \cdot 2^{c(\lambda)} = \sum_\lambda \frac{2^{c(\lambda)}}{z_\lambda}. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The exponent admits the compact form
\[
c(\lambda) = \frac{1}{2}\!\left[\sum_{r,s} m_r m_s \gcd(r,s) - \sum_r m_r\right]
\]
(expand the double sum into diagonal $r = s$ and off-diagonal $r \neq s$ terms). This is the form used in the OEIS Python implementation by C.~W.~Wu (2024).
\end{remark}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Verify the algebraic equivalence of the two exponent forms.
  \item Implement the formula. Verify against the table in Appendix~\ref{app:values}. Report the time for $T(50)$.
  \item Profile: what fraction of time is partition generation vs.\ per-partition arithmetic?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[\textbf{Optimization}]\label{prob:optimize}

The na\"ive implementation slows around $n = 60\text{--}80$. The bottleneck is the number of odd-part partitions $p_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)$, which grows as $\exp(\pi\sqrt{n/3}\,)$. Your task: make it fast enough for $T(200)$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item \textbf{GCD precomputation} (${\sim}1.5\times$). Build a lookup table $G[i][j] = \gcd(i,j)$ before the partition loop.

  \item \textbf{Avoid rational arithmetic} (${\sim}3\times$). Compute $L = \mathrm{lcm}$ of all $z_\lambda$, accumulate $(L/z_\lambda) \cdot 2^{c(\lambda)}$ as integers, divide by $L$ once.

  \item \textbf{Bucket accumulation} (${\sim}3\text{--}5\times$). Since $2^{c(\lambda)}$ is enormous ($2^{19\,900}$ at $n = 200$):
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item Create \texttt{bucket[t]} indexed by $t = c(\lambda)$.
    \item For each $\lambda$, add $L/z_\lambda$ to \texttt{bucket}$[c(\lambda)]$.
    \item After the loop, compute $\sum_t \texttt{bucket}[t] \cdot 2^t$.
  \end{enumerate}

  \item \textbf{Parallelization} (${\sim}k\times$ for $k$ cores). The partition loop is embarrassingly parallel.

  \item \textbf{Language.} Compare Python vs.\ C/GMP. The per-partition cost is dominated by big-integer arithmetic in the assembly phase; C with GMP is typically $50\text{--}100\times$ faster.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Targets:}
\begin{center}

\toprule
Level & $n$ & Time target \\
\midrule
Bronze & 100 & $< 60$ seconds \\

With the optimization in Problem~\ref{prob:truncated}, the same value can be computed in seconds.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[\textbf{The Truncated Partition Algorithm}]\label{prob:truncated}

This is the key mathematical insight. The identity partition $\lambda = (1^n)$ contributes $2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!$ to the sum~\eqref{eq:davis}. Every other partition contributes \emph{exponentially less}.

\begin{theorem}[Dominance of the Identity Partition]\label{thm:dominance}
For any odd-part partition $\lambda \neq (1^n)$, define the \textbf{deficit}
\[
\Delta(\lambda) = \binom{n}{2} - c(\lambda).
\]
Then $\Delta(\lambda) \geq 2(n-2)$ for all $\lambda \neq (1^n)$, with equality iff $\lambda = (3, 1^{n-3})$. The contribution ratio satisfies
\[
\frac{2^{c(\lambda)}/z_\lambda}{2^{\binom{n}{2}}/n!} \;=\; \frac{n!}{z_\lambda} \cdot 2^{-\Delta(\lambda)} \;\leq\; \binom{n}{3} \cdot 2 \cdot 2^{-2(n-2)},
\]
which is exponentially small in $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The identity partition has $c((1^n)) = \binom{n}{2}$ (all pairs form their own orbit). Any non-identity partition ``merges'' vertices into longer cycles, reducing the number of pair orbits. The partition $(3, 1^{n-3})$ merges exactly three vertices into a 3-cycle, losing $\binom{n}{2} - [1 + (n-3) + \binom{n-3}{2}] = 2n - 4 = 2(n-2)$ orbits. Larger merges lose more: the partition $(5, 1^{n-5})$ has deficit $4(n-3)$, roughly double.

For the ratio bound: $|C_{(3,1^{n-3})}| = n!/z_{(3,1^{n-3})} = n!/(3 \cdot (n-3)!) = \binom{n}{3} \cdot 2$. Every other class has either larger deficit or smaller conjugacy class.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sub-Exponential Algorithm]\label{cor:subexp}
To compute $T(n)$ exactly, it suffices to sum over partitions $\lambda$ with deficit $\Delta(\lambda) \leq D$, where $D$ is chosen so the truncation error is less than~$\frac{1}{2}$. The number of such partitions grows as $p(B)$ where $B = O(n)$ is the ``departure budget'' $\sum_i (l_i - 1)$ over non-unit parts. Since each non-unit odd part contributes departure $\geq 2$, the budget is at most $D/(n-2)$. In practice:
\[
\text{configs needed} \;\approx\; p\!\left(\frac{n}{6}\right) \;\ll\; p_{\mathrm{odd}}(n).
\]
\end{corollary}

\section*{Appendix A: Why Tournaments Matter}

The enumeration formula connects to several active areas:

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Statistical mechanics.} Every tournament is a frustrated spin system on a triangular lattice. The Hamiltonian path count $H(T) = I(\Omega(T), 2)$ is the hard-core lattice gas partition function at fugacity $\lambda = 2$, where $\Omega(T)$ is the odd-cycle conflict graph (Grinberg--Stanley, arXiv:2412.10572).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Game theory.} A tournament is a 2-player zero-sum game (adjacency matrix = payoff matrix). The Nash equilibrium support size, the cooperative game core, and the Shapley value all encode tournament structure. Notably, the Shapley value spread (how unequally Hamiltonian paths are distributed among vertices) is \emph{orthogonal} to the total path count $H(T)$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Coding theory.} A tournament is a binary codeword of length $\binom{n}{2}$. The syndrome is the score sequence. The independence polynomial $I(\Omega(T), x)$ is the weight enumerator, and $H(T) = I(\Omega(T), 2)$ evaluates it at the cardinality of truth values in $\mathbf{Set}$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Forbidden values.} The only odd numbers that can never be $H(T)$ for any tournament are $\{7, 21\} = \{\Phi_3(2), \Phi_3(4)\}$. This connects to cycle forcing: three directed 3-cycles on $n \leq 6$ vertices always create a 5-cycle.
