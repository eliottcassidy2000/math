# David Bessis and Tournament Theory: Surprising Connections

## Who is David Bessis?

David Bessis is a French mathematician who spent the 2000s solving some of the deepest problems in the theory of **reflection groups** and **braid groups** before leaving academia to found a machine learning company. He returned to public view in 2024 with his book *Mathematica: A Secret World of Intuition and Curiosity*, which explores the nature of mathematical thinking.

His core mathematical work:
- **The dual braid monoid** (2003): a new algebraic structure that organizes braid groups using ALL reflections, not just the simple ones
- **The K(π,1) conjecture** (proved 2015, published Annals of Mathematics): proved that certain spaces — the complements of hyperplane arrangements for complex reflection groups — have no higher-dimensional "holes"
- **Noncrossing partitions** and **cyclic sieving**: combinatorial results connecting reflections, cycle structures, and polynomial evaluations

At first glance, none of this seems related to counting Hamiltonian paths in tournaments. But the connections are real, and in some cases they are direct.

## Connection 1: The Staircase IS the Type A Root System

This is the deepest connection, and it's exact.

**In Bessis's world**: The symmetric group S_n has a root system of type A_{n-1}. It consists of:
- **Simple roots**: the n-1 roots e₁-e₂, e₂-e₃, ..., e_{n-1}-e_n — one for each consecutive pair
- **Non-simple positive roots**: all roots e_i - e_j for j > i+1 — one for each non-consecutive pair

There are n-1 simple roots and C(n-1, 2) non-simple positive roots.

**In the tournament tiling model**: To encode a tournament on n players, we fix a reference path (n → n-1 → ... → 1) and:
- **Base-path arcs**: the n-1 arcs along consecutive vertices (n,n-1), (n-1,n-2), ..., (2,1) — these are FIXED and never flipped
- **Tiles**: the C(n-1,2) arcs between non-consecutive vertices — these are FREE, each gets a black/white choice

Count the correspondence:
- **Simple roots** ↔ **Base-path arcs** (n-1 of each, consecutive pairs)
- **Non-simple positive roots** ↔ **Tiles** (C(n-1,2) of each, non-consecutive pairs)

The staircase Young diagram δ_{n-2} — the triangular grid used to visualize tilings — is exactly the **non-simple part of the type A positive root system**.

**What this means**: The tiling model is not just a convenient visualization trick. The decomposition of arcs into "base path" (fixed) and "tiles" (free) is the same decomposition Bessis uses when splitting the classical braid monoid (generated by simple reflections) from the dual braid monoid (generated by ALL reflections, including non-simple ones). The tiles ARE the extra generators that Bessis adds.

## Connection 2: Hamiltonian Paths Are Chambers of the Braid Arrangement

**The braid arrangement** is a collection of hyperplanes (flat surfaces) in n-dimensional space: for each pair i ≠ j, there's a hyperplane where coordinate i equals coordinate j. Together these divide the space into regions called **chambers** or **Weyl chambers**.

Each chamber corresponds to a way of ordering the coordinates: "coordinate 1 is biggest, then coordinate 3, then coordinate 2, ..." and so on. In other words, each chamber = a linear ordering of n items.

**A Hamiltonian path in a tournament** is exactly a linear ordering of n players (arranged in a line so each player beats the one to their right). So:

**Hamiltonian path in tournament T = chamber of the braid arrangement compatible with T**

The count H(T) = number of Weyl chambers of the type A arrangement consistent with the tournament's win/loss structure.

This gives tournament path-counting a geometric home: the n! chambers of the braid arrangement, filtered by the tournament T.

## Connection 3: The K(π,1) Theorem and Why β₂ = 0

Bessis's most celebrated result — the K(π,1) theorem for complex reflection arrangements — has a topological consequence directly relevant to this project.

**What K(π,1) means**: A space is K(π,1) (also called "aspherical") if it has no higher-dimensional holes — only 1-dimensional ones. In terms of Betti numbers: β_k = 0 for k ≥ 2.

- Deligne proved (1972) that the complement of the **real** braid arrangement is K(π,1)
- Bessis proved (2015) that this extends to ALL finite complex reflection group arrangements — the six most difficult cases took entirely new techniques

**The connection to tournaments**: This project found computationally (for all tournaments up to n=8) that β₂ = 0 for all tournaments. This is exactly the "no 2-dimensional holes" statement — just for tournament GLMY path homology rather than for hyperplane arrangement complements.

Bessis's theorem says: the space of all linear orderings (Weyl chambers), topologized as a complex arrangement complement, has no 2-dimensional holes. The tournament project finds: the directed graph homology of tournaments also has no 2-dimensional holes.

These are different spaces and different homology theories. But the vanishing is in the same dimension, for related objects (tournaments are closely connected to chambers of the braid arrangement). This suggests a deeper structural reason why both vanish — possibly that the tournament's topology is "inherited from" the asphericity of the arrangement complement.

**The open problem**: Prove β₂ = 0 for ALL tournaments algebraically. Bessis's techniques — Garside categories, Springer theory — might be exactly the right tools for this proof.

## Connection 4: The Grinberg-Stanley Formula and All-Odd-Cycle Permutations

Bessis's work on **noncrossing partitions** revolves around permutations with special cycle structure. The noncrossing partitions of type A are permutations of {1,...,n} whose cycles are geometrically non-crossing when drawn on a circle.

The **Grinberg-Stanley formula** for H(T) is:

H(T) = Σ_{σ ∈ S_n, all cycles of σ are odd} 2^{number of cycles of σ}

This sums over all permutations of the n players that have ONLY ODD-LENGTH cycles, weighting each by 2^{(number of cycles)}.

**The parallel**: Bessis's noncrossing partition lattice sums over permutations with NONCROSSING cycles, weighting by similar cycle statistics. The tournament formula restricts to ODD cycles (instead of noncrossing ones). Both:
- Decompose permutations by their cycle type
- Weight by 2^{cycles}
- Restrict to a special class (odd-cycle vs. noncrossing)

The independent sets in the conflict graph Ω(T) are exactly the collections of vertex-disjoint odd cycles in T — they're the "non-interfering odd pieces" of the permutation structure. This is the same organizational principle as noncrossing partitions (non-interfering non-crossing pieces).

## Connection 5: Cyclic Sieving and Burnside for Tournament Counting

Bessis and Reiner (2011) proved a **cyclic sieving phenomenon** for noncrossing partitions. Cyclic sieving says: evaluate a polynomial (the q-count of noncrossing partitions) at a root of unity ζ = e^{2πi/n}, and the result is an integer that counts noncrossing partitions fixed by a cyclic rotation.

This project uses **Burnside's lemma** to count distinct tournament isomorphism classes:

Number of distinct n-vertex tournaments = (1/n!) × Σ_{σ ∈ S_n} (number of tournaments fixed by σ)

For rotationally symmetric tournaments (like Paley and cyclic interval), the fixed-point count is exactly the kind of evaluation at roots of unity that appears in cyclic sieving.

**The practical connection**: The Bessis-Reiner cyclic sieving machinery — which has been developed into powerful computational tools for reflection groups — should apply to the Burnside computation for Paley tournament classes. This could give more efficient formulas for A051337 (number of self-complementary tournaments on n vertices) and related sequences.

## Connection 6: Garside Structure and the Tiling Hypercube

The **dual braid monoid** (Bessis's central construction) is a monoid generated by ALL reflections of a Coxeter group, with a specific set of relations. It has a **Garside element** Δ_dual (the "full twist") whose set of divisors — the elements below it in the monoid order — is exactly the noncrossing partition lattice NC(W).

In the tiling model:
- Each tiling is a point in {0,1}^m (a vertex of the hypercube)
- The "all-ones" tiling (all tiles black = complement of the reference path) is the "full twist" analogue
- The elements below this in the natural order are all tilings reachable by setting subsets of tiles to 1

**The Garside structure on tilings**: The hypercube {0,1}^m with componentwise partial order IS a Garside-like structure. The divisors of the "full twist" (all-ones tiling) are all tilings — exactly the set NC(W) of noncrossing partitions is to the Garside element in Bessis's dual braid monoid.

This analogy suggests: the right way to think about the tiling hypercube is as a Garside structure of type A, with the tiling model providing coordinates that Bessis's abstract algebraic construction doesn't have.

## Connection 7: Koszul Duality and the Conflict Graph

Bessis's 2021 paper establishes **Koszul duality** between the dual braid monoid algebra and the cluster complex. In Koszul duality, two algebraic objects are "dual" in a precise way: information in one is reflected in the other.

For our project:
- The **conflict graph Ω(T)** defines a simplicial complex: its faces are the independent sets (collections of non-conflicting odd cycles)
- The **independence polynomial** I(Ω(T), x) is the generating function of this simplicial complex
- Koszul duality, in this setting, would relate I(Ω(T), x) to some "dual" complex

The cluster complex in Bessis's work has:
- Vertices = cluster variables (combinatorial objects from Fomin-Zelevinsky theory)
- Faces = compatible clusters (analogous to independent sets in Ω(T))

**The structural parallel**: Both the conflict graph simplicial complex (our project) and the cluster complex (Bessis's work) organize "compatible configurations" of algebraic pieces. The independence polynomial and the cluster complex generating function play the same role in their respective theories.

Whether there's a precise Koszul duality connecting them remains open, but the structural similarity suggests the answer could be yes for specific families of tournaments (particularly those arising from Coxeter groups).

## Connection 8: Exceptional Types and Exceptional Tournaments

Bessis's hardest work concerned the six **exceptional complex reflection groups**: G24, G27, G29, G31, G33, G34. These don't fit into any infinite family and require ad hoc geometric arguments.

This project similarly finds "exceptional" behavior at small tournament sizes:
- The Paley tournament T₇ has β₄ = 6 (rare, exceptional)
- The forbidden values H = 7 and H = 21 are permanent exceptions to the general pattern
- The crossover between Paley and cyclic interval tournaments near p ≈ 13 is a phase transition with exceptional structure

Both mathematical landscapes — Bessis's exceptional reflection groups and the project's exceptional tournaments — involve discrete objects that escape the patterns governing the generic case. In both settings, the exceptional objects are the hardest to analyze and the most rich in structure.

The specific exceptional groups G27, G29 (related to the icosahedron and its generalizations) involve symmetries of 5-element sets — and 5 is the smallest n where tournament structure becomes genuinely complex (the first non-trivial gaps in H(T) appear at n=5).

## Summary: Where Bessis Meets Tournaments

| Bessis Concept | Tournament Analogue | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Simple roots ↔ non-simple roots | Base-path arcs ↔ tiles | Exact (same count, same structure) |
| Weyl chambers of braid arrangement | Hamiltonian paths in T | Exact (same objects) |
| K(π,1): β_k = 0 for k ≥ 2 | β₂ = 0 for all tournaments | Same vanishing, different setting |
| All-odd-cycle permutations in GS formula | NC partitions in Bessis-Reiner | Parallel structure (different class restriction) |
| Cyclic sieving at roots of unity | Burnside for tournament counting | Same technique |
| Garside element = full twist | All-ones tiling = complement path | Structural analogy |
| Dual braid monoid generated by all reflections | Tile model uses all arcs | Same decomposition |
| Cluster complex (compatible clusters) | Independence complex of Ω(T) | Structural parallel |
| Exceptional groups G24, G27, G29, G31, G33, G34 | Exceptional tournament behaviors | Analogous in character |

**The deepest connection**: The staircase Young diagram that is the visual heart of the tiling model is exactly the non-simple part of the type A root system — the same algebraic object that Bessis's dual braid monoid adds to the classical braid monoid. This is not a coincidence. The tiling model is implicitly a realization of the Garside structure of type A, with tilings as Garside elements and the conflict graph Ω(T) measuring their compatibility.

**The open program**: Use Bessis's techniques — Garside categories, Springer theory, K(π,1) methods — to prove the outstanding conjectures:
1. β₂ = 0 for all tournaments (the tournament K(π,1) problem)
2. A bijective proof of the OCF formula via the Garside structure
3. A precise Koszul duality between I(Ω(T), x) and a natural "dual complex" indexed by cluster-like structures

## Bessis's Broader Mathematical Philosophy

Beyond the specific results, Bessis's book *Mathematica* (2024) articulates a philosophy directly relevant to this project's approach:

> Mathematics progresses through **intuition-reason dialogue**: intuition generates the pictures and patterns, reason verifies and makes them precise.

This project exemplifies exactly that: computational experiments (building the metagraph, computing path homology, finding forbidden values) generate the intuitions, and the algebraic/topological framework (OCF, Garside, K(π,1)) provides the verification.

Bessis spent years building intuition for the K(π,1) theorem before the proof came together. His description of that process — holding a complex geometric object in the mind until its structure becomes clear — matches the experience of working with the tournament metagraph and its spine/ribs/sea structure.

## Key Words

- **Dual braid monoid**: Bessis's algebraic structure using ALL reflections of a Coxeter group (not just simple ones) — mirrors the tile model's use of all arcs
- **K(π,1) space**: a space with no higher-dimensional holes (β_k = 0 for k ≥ 2) — Bessis proved this for reflection arrangements; the project finds it computationally for tournament homology
- **Weyl chamber**: a region of space defined by the hyperplane arrangement of a reflection group — equals one linear ordering = one Hamiltonian path
- **Noncrossing partition**: a partition of {1,...,n} whose blocks don't geometrically cross when drawn on a circle — the type A analogue of independent sets in Ω(T)
- **Cyclic sieving**: evaluating a q-polynomial at roots of unity to count orbits — the same technique as Burnside's lemma for tournament isomorphism classes
- **Garside element**: the "full twist" that generates the center of the braid group — corresponds to the all-ones tiling (complement of reference path)
